
The arms lower him to the floor when his legs don't hold. He lets them. He ends up flat on his back, staring at the ceiling, or possibly at the sock.

I slide down and sit next to him. My heart takes its time coming back from wherever it went.

Neither of us says anything for a while.

"Is that yours," he says eventually. Not a question. Still looking up.

"It was a disagreement."

"Who won."

"The arm. On points."

He accepts this. There's a logo on the wall panel beside us. ARGON INDUSTRIES / LSI DIVISION. I watch his paw find it without him looking. Trace the letters once. Stop.

"Why do I remember them," he says. Not really to me.

Something flickers behind his eyes. Whatever it is, he doesn't let it finish.

I get up and offer him a paw. He takes it.

"I'm the engineer, I assume," he says, "based on the title I've been called so far." Monotone, the vocal equivalent of a shrug.

"Ethyn," I say. "Vasek. Secondary scientist. I found out about an hour ago, from a tube. I also, uh. I found some other things out. You're going to want to sit back down for those."

"I just stood up."

"I know. I'm sorry. It's been that kind of morning."

---

He looks at me for a solid minute without saying anything. Then he walks over to a console across the room, one I'd walked past twice without noticing, because I was busy yelling at furniture. One line of amber text on a dark screen.

LSI-ASM&C / STANDBY

"We should figure out where we are," he says. He's blunt about everything, built without the option of softening a sentence.

"I did that. Sort of. Upstairs." I hold up my sleeve. It's covered in numbers. "I'll explain. It's not good."

He looks at the sleeve. He doesn't ask. He clicks something and starts reading the screen out loud instead, from the top, and I let him, because it's the first thing all morning that's been read to me and not demanded of me.

"Long Shot Initiative. Vanguard MK1. Welcome aboard. You are awake."

In the bottom corner of the screen a small notification blinks into existence.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. READY TO SWITCH WITH LSI-ASM&C.

"If you are reading this, your comatose state is over and you're awake. By now you have likely met your crew, had a conversation or two, and are starting to piece things together. This program is here to help with the rest."

I'd like to know who wrote this, so I can show them my sock.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. SWITCHING. PREPARING HANDOVER.

"Below are your crewmates. You probably already know who they are. This is just to confirm it."

He reads it flat. A man reading a repair manual who happens to find every repair manual useful.

"Your Pilot is a large framed Canis Lupus. Male. Methodical. If he hasn't already assessed every system on this ship since waking up, he will shortly."

Row one, top bed. The big wolf I knocked on like a door. He hasn't assessed anything.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. HANDOVER IN PROGRESS.

"Your Doctor is a slight Feline Serval. Female. Certified biologist. If something was wrong with any of you since waking up, she has already clocked it."

Middle bed. Ears still flattened under the hood.

"Your Mechanical Engineer is a stocky Canine. Male. Hardware, systems, anything physical on this vessel. He is probably already annoyed about something. This is normal."

Bottom bed, row one. The jaw. I'll take their word for it.

"Your Software Engineer is a lean Canine. Male. Quiet. Gets things done without mentioning it."

He stops for half a second.

"Yup, that's me." Then he keeps going without breaking stride. "Your Lead Scientist is a Canid Vulpes. Female. Multiple doctorates. You will know her by the way she talks about the Phengoclept like it personally wronged her."

The vulpes under my bed. The one I said ma'am to. I push my glasses up, mostly out of solidarity with her. I already like her. I'd like her to wake up so I can tell her.

"Your Secondary Scientist is a near-sighted Amber Vulpes. Male. Mid-twenties. More doctorates than is strictly reasonable for his age. If he has his glasses, he is fine."

"I have my glasses," I say. "I'd like it noted that I'm not fine."

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. NOW OPERATING LSI VESSEL.

Then nothing. It just sits there humming in the background, like it's been waiting the whole time and has no particular feelings about finally being acknowledged.

"By now this should all sound familiar," he reads. "If it doesn't, give it time. It will come back. If you recognise yourself, good. If you recognise someone else, find them."

A beat.

"Good luck out there."

I look at row one again. The pilot, the medic, the other engineer. All of them asleep. And the lead scientist, in the middle of our row. Four people asleep, two people awake, and only one of the two awake ones has thrown anything.

One of the arms drops from the ceiling and taps him on the shoulder. It's holding a tube. He takes it, reads the label, and eats it in one go. No ceremony, no argument. Tube up, tube down, tube in his pocket. Then he holds the label up so I can see it, without being asked.

KENZO, J. / SOFTWARE ENGINEER
MEAL: 2/3 / DAY: 1

"Kenzo," I say.

"Apparently."

"What's the J for?"

He thinks about it. I watch him think about it, and I watch him find nothing, and I recognize the face, because I wore it for most of the morning.

"Unclear," he says.

---

The ladder is easier the second time. Not easy. Easier.

He goes up first. He doesn't ask about the whiteboard when we pass it, or the lab, or the console with the camera feed still glowing on it. He looks at all of it once, left to right, like he's filing it, and keeps climbing.

At the top he stops below the hatch and looks at me. I climb past him, which is a strange thing to do on a ladder, but he moves aside without being asked.

IDENTIFY TO PROCEED.

"Ethyn Vasek," I say. Second time today. I'm getting good at it.

A pause. Longer than I'd like.

"Dr. Vasek."

It's a different voice. Smoother. Slightly synthetic, with the quality of something very capable that has no intention of letting you forget it. The old one in the medbay sounded like a vending machine. This one sounds like the vending machine went to college. And the name comes out strange, pulled from somewhere else in whatever library this thing keeps, a flat statement-voice dropped into a sentence built for questions. Like someone recorded my surname once, in one register, and the machine is stuck using that clip no matter what it's trying to say with it.

"Identity confirmed. Who is your co-pilot? Please state their name."

This time I have one. I look down at him.

"Engineer Kenzo," I say. "I trust him. He read me a list."

"Mr. Kenzo's identity is partially confirmed. First name required."

"He doesn't remember it."

"That information is available to confirmed crew only."

"I am confirmed crew. You just said so. You said it in a weird voice, but you said it."

A pause.

"Jinzhou," it says. "Mr. Jinzhou T. Kenzo."

I look back down the ladder at Kenzo. At Jinzhou.

"Jinzhou," I say.

He tries it. "Jinzhou Kenzo." Then, quieter, just to himself, like checking a measurement: "Jinzhou."

The hatch clicks. I climb up first and take the left seat. He climbs through and takes the right.

The console in front of me is not, it turns out, entirely in a language I can read. Most of it is the same tall, narrow lettering as the lab console. A strip of switches runs along the lower edge of my side of the panel, worn smooth at the edges like they get used constantly. More out of habit than expectation, I mouth the words on them.

They don't mean anything. Sounds with no content behind them.

Jinzhou glances over, follows where I'm looking, and doesn't say anything about it. I notice that, without knowing yet why it matters.

"What is the purpose of this mission," the new voice asks.

I open my mouth. Close it. Something surfaces, slow and certain, like the name did.

"To stop Nakuet from dimming," I say. "To save Wask-ki from freezing."

A pause. Longer than the question should need.

"Partial confirm. Dr. Vasek, you are cleared as secondary pilot. Do you vouch for Mr. Kenzo as co-pilot."

"Yes."

"Jinzhou T. Kenzo." His full name arrives in a different register from the sentence around it, warmer, slightly higher, as if it was pulled from a separate recording made on a better day by someone in a better mood. "Co-pilot access granted. Welcome aboard, you two. ARACCSIM is now at your service."

"ARACCSIM," I repeat. "That's you."

"Correct."

"Talking to you is like talking to a god damn Spell'n'Speak," I say. "I hate it."

"It can hear you," Jinzhou says.

"I'm aware. I'd like it noted that I was polite about it."

"Noted," ARACCSIM says, in what I'm fairly sure is the voice of a different, much friendlier machine.

I pull up the navigation screen. I know what it's going to say. I want to see it anyway. The star map is centered on a point labeled ASI-06A, sitting right in the middle, with a trajectory line running from somewhere far to the left all the way to where we are now. There's a label in the top right corner, in the lettering I can't read.

"Jinzhou," I say. "Read that. Please."

He looks at it for a second, longer than it should take someone who actually knows what it says.

"Current system," he says, slow, like he's translating it as much for himself as for me. "Asimov. Distance from Nakuet system. Six point four light years."

Six point four. I didn't have the number. I had the shape. Now the shape has a number, and the number is worse, because numbers don't let you round down.

I hold out my sleeve to him. He reads the columns of timestamps going up to my elbow, and the thirty-two circled at the bottom, three times.

"Rotation period," I say. "I did it upstairs. In the lab. Alone. Three spots. That isn't our star."

"No," he says. "It is not."

"ARACCSIM. Confirm."

"Stephfren Asimov Memorial System, designation ASI-06A. You are currently inbound within the Asimov system. Approximate time to Asimov-09D is about one week."

I let out a breath I'd been holding since the lab.

"I know," I say. "I just wanted somebody else to say it."

---

"Partial confirm" turns out to be a phrase ARACCSIM likes a lot more than I do.

"What does partial mean," I ask. "Partial like eighty percent, or partial like you're being polite about a D minus."

"Mission purpose response was substantively correct. Additional detail withheld pending full crew confirmation."

"Withheld by who."

"By design specification."

"That's, uh, not an answer. That's a sentence shaped like an answer, wearing an answer's clothes."

"Correct."

I look at Jinzhou, half expecting backup, but he's already moved on. He's scrolling through something on the main screen with the flat focus of a man who has decided arguing with the ship is not a productive use of his remaining daylight. There is technically none. We're in a tin can six point four light years from the nearest daylight, a couple of side windows notwithstanding, and I've decided to be personally offended by that.

"It's fine," I say, mostly to myself. "We'll, uh, get the rest eventually."

"Or we won't," Jinzhou says.

"Thank you for that. Very helpful. I'm putting it on a motivational poster."

He shrugs, mostly with his ears, with slightly more shoulder than seems structurally necessary.

I turn back to my screen. The dimming number is still sitting there. Sixteen point two percent. I hate it a little more every time I look. It's also the only thing on this ship that has never once been vague with me, and I'm starting to resent the honesty. The number is a splinter I can't dig out, so I do the only thing that helps, which is work, because work at least pretends to go somewhere.

My paw finds the edge of the console without me looking, and I notice, not for the first time but for the first time properly, that the physical switches down here don't match the screens above them. The screens, whatever ARACCSIM is rendering onto them, are consistent, every panel the same unreadable Indonesian I ran into this morning. The switches are a mess by comparison. Some still carry their original stamped plates. Others have a strip of tape pressed over the old lettering, something written across it in permanent marker, in handwriting I don't recognize, in a language I do.

I run a claw over one. THRUST TRIM, the tape says, in blunt English capitals, stuck down slightly crooked over whatever the plate used to say.

The one next to it hasn't been touched. PENGENDALIAN SIKAP, stamped clean into the original plate, no tape, no translation. I have no idea what it does. I have a guess, given that it sits beside thrust trim, but a guess is all I've got.

Further down, one that has been fixed, block capitals over a torn strip of tape: EMERGENCY STOP. I decide I like whoever did this labeling. They knew which one to do first.

Two more sit untouched past that, original Indonesian intact, small enough that I have to lean in to read the shapes of the letters. DAYA LISTRIK UTAMA. STRUKTUR KOMUNIKASI. I say them under my breath, badly, the way you sound out a word in a language you've never studied, more noise than pronunciation.

"Someone did this by paw," I say.

Jinzhou glances over. "The tape."

"Yeah."

"Can't reprint a switch plate mid-transit," he says, and goes back to his screen.

He's right, and he's left out the interesting part, which is the person. Somebody with decent English and a label maker sat down at some point and fixed what they could reach, in the order that mattered, and then stopped. Ran out of time, or tape, or patience. I look down the row. About half the switches have been fixed. The other half still carry their original stamped Indonesian, waiting for someone who never got around to them.

"ARACCSIM. Show me the full luminosity curve. Not the summary. Every data point you've got."

A pause, longer than I'd like from something that thinks in numbers I can't even count to.

"Working."

The screen fills with a graph. Hundreds of points, not two. A long, mostly flat line for years and years, and then, near the end, a curve that bends downward like it's tired of holding itself up.

Honestly, relatable.

"That's not steady," I say.

"No," Jinzhou agrees.

"It's accelerating."

"Yes."

I trace the curve with the stylus. I don't need to, but tracing things helps me think. Some part of my brain only trusts information once my paw has touched it, which is an inconvenient way to be built when most of my job now happens on a screen.

"If it keeps accelerating like this," I say, "give me the days to critical."

Jinzhou pulls the number without being asked twice, which is either efficient or slightly unsettling. I haven't decided which.

"Two thousand one hundred," he says. "Give or take a season."

"Give or take a season. You say that like seasons are small."

"They are. Compared to two thousand one hundred days."

"That's not comforting math, Jinzhou. That's math wearing a disguise."

He doesn't respond to that, which I've started to understand is its own kind of response.

I do the real math out loud because it's easier that way. "That's, uh. That's under six years."

"Yes."

"That's not the number on the whiteboard downstairs."

"No," he says. "It is not."

"Crap," I say, mostly to the console.

Neither of us says anything else for a while. The cockpit hums around us, small fans, distant machinery, a room built to keep two people alive somewhere that would very much prefer they weren't.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Why weren't we told the rate was increasing before departure."

"That information was classified under mission need to know protocols."

"We're the mission."

"Confirmed crew. Not full crew."

I stare at the ceiling like it personally owes me an explanation and a small apology.

I say a few things to it. They're short, they're quiet, and they're not going in the record.

"There it is again."

"Yes," Jinzhou says.

---

We keep at it for a while. Fuel curves, trajectory windows, deceleration budget for arrival at Asimov-09D. It feels productive right up until I notice we've been at it for two hours and I still don't know why we're here, only how we got here and how fast the clock is ticking while we figure out the rest. Two hours, zero answers, one headache. A strong first day.

"ARACCSIM," I say, mostly out of habit at this point. "Run a full system diagnostic. I want to know what's actually working on this ship before I trust another number you give me."

"Diagnostic initiated."

The screens cycle through a long list of subsystems, each one blinking green as it clears. Phengomotor housing. Comms array. Hull integrity. Life support. Down the list it goes, calm and thorough, in the exact tone of someone reading a grocery list they've read a hundred times and stopped caring about the order of.

"No anomalies detected," it finally says.

The word lands somewhere behind my eyes and doesn't let go.

Anomaly.

"Ethyn."

I don't answer. I'm not entirely in the room anymore.

---

My office. I know it by feel, not sight. The chair has a lean to it I've never fixed, mostly out of spite for whoever ordered these chairs in bulk without checking that any of them sat level.

Y'shari's voice comes through the speakerphone badly, the signal dropping every few words, wherever she's calling from.

"Say that again," I tell her. "Slower. You're explaining the feeding cycle like I haven't read the same papers you have."

"You have read the same papers."

"I skimmed the abstracts. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and I resent that you keep pretending there isn't one."

She sighs, which over a bad phone line comes through as mostly static with feeling.

"Fine. The anomaly." Papers shuffle on her end. "Every system we've mapped follows the same pattern. Line appears, star dims, line gets brighter, star dims faster. Every single one. Except ASI-06A."

"What's different."

"The line's there. Full strength, hasn't grown or faded in two years of watching. But the star isn't dimming. Not even a fraction of a percent."

I lean back. The chair creaks its usual complaint.

"So the light's being taken but nothing's actually being lost. That's, uh. That's not how theft works, Y'shari. Theft implies something ends up missing."

"That's the theory."

"That doesn't make sense."

"Welcome to my week."

I pull my notepad closer and start writing shorthand. Y'shari has told me twice to just type my drafts. She's right. I don't, mostly because typing feels like admitting the notepad has lost, and I'm not ready to let the notepad lose.

"Walk me through what that would mean," I say. "If the arc's, uhm, stable and permanent instead of migratory."

"Two possibilities. One, whatever's out there stopped moving. Found a stable population and stayed."

"And two."

"Two is worse."

"Everything with you is worse lately. You've become a doom prophet. It's a bad look on you."

"Two is something's keeping it in balance. Something eating it back as fast as it grows. A predator, maybe. The system found equilibrium on its own instead of getting stripped bare like everywhere else."

I stop writing. Read back what I've got. Equilibrium. I underline it twice, hard enough that the pen nearly tears the page.

"If that's true," I say, "that's not just interesting. That's the whole ballgame."

"I know."

"That's not a study guide chapter, Y'shari. That's the reason to build a ship."

Silence. A beat too long.

"They're already talking about it," she says, quieter now. "Not officially. But they're talking."

"Talking about building a ship to go look."

"Talking about who they'd send."

I laugh, because the alternative is not laughing, and not laughing feels premature.

"Good thing that's not my problem."

She doesn't laugh back.

I should have noticed that. I didn't.

"I have to go," she says. "But sit with this before you write anything. It's not settled science. It's a working theory built on one weird system and a lot of hope."

"Hope with citations."

"Something like that. Talk soon."

The line goes dead. I sit there staring at the word equilibrium, underlined twice, with no idea I'm looking at my own future, no idea that hope with citations is about to become my entire job description.

---

"Ethyn."

Jinzhou's voice, closer now, flatter, the flatness he uses when he's said my name more than once and is starting to wonder if I've become furniture.

I blink. The cockpit resolves back into place around me. The screens. The green diagnostic checkmarks. Jinzhou's face, mildly expectant, the same expression he'd probably wear if the ship were on fire, which I find both reassuring and a little worrying about his priorities.

"You went somewhere again," he says.

"Yeah." I push my glasses up. "Uh, good one this time. Actually useful, not just emotionally devastating for no reason."

"Useful?"

"Extremely." I sit up straighter. "ASI-06A isn't a random stop. The line here doesn't grow like every other one they've mapped. Something's kept it stable. In, uh. Balance. Equilibrium."

Jinzhou considers this with the enthusiasm of a man reviewing a parts inventory, which is to say a very specific and controlled amount.

"So we're not observing," he says.

"I don't think we ever were. I think we're supposed to find out what's balancing it and, uhm, figure out if we can take it home."

He's quiet for a second, which for Jinzhou counts as a dramatic reaction, possibly the equivalent of someone else gasping.

"That is a significantly larger task than the whiteboard implied."

"The whiteboard didn't have all the data. Neither did I, apparently, until about thirty seconds ago. My brain's rationing information like it's on a meal plan."

"Convenient timing."

"I don't control when the memories show up. Believe me, if I did, I'd schedule them for less catastrophic moments. Maybe over breakfast. Somewhere with better lighting."

He lets that stand, which from him is as close as it gets to agreement.

---

Neither of us wants to sit in the cockpit chewing on the actual size of the problem, so we go looking for something else to do with our paws. That turns out to mean going looking for the rest of the ship.

Back down through the lab shaft, past the whiteboard, past the equipment neither of us has touched since the read-aloud. Partway down, Jinzhou pauses at a hatch set into the wall that I hadn't clocked before, half hidden behind a rack of strapped-down gear.

"What's that."

"Unclear," he says, which is his answer to most things, but he says it while already prying at the release latch, so I don't think unclear is stopping him.

The hatch gives with a hiss of equalizing pressure. Beyond it is a short connecting passage, low ceilinged, lined with storage lockers on both sides. Personal effects, most likely, judging by the little embossed nameplates on each one.

"Dormitory annex," ARACCSIM offers, unprompted, which is new. "Personal storage. You are cleared for access to your own compartment. Crewmate compartments require owner authorization or confirmed incapacitation."

"Confirmed incapacitation," I repeat, and saying it out loud makes it worse, not better.

"Correct."

Neither of us pushes on what that means. Some doors you open slow.

I find mine near the end. VASEK, E. stenciled in block letters, same font as the food tubes, same font Argon apparently uses for absolutely everything, from meal packs to personnel lockers to, presumably, someone's headstone somewhere. My paw hovers over the latch longer than it probably should.

"You going to open it," Jinzhou asks, "or are we doing a moment."

"We're doing a moment. Give me a second. Some of us process things instead of putting them off and moving on with our lives."

"I put things off. I still open lockers."

"That's not the same thing and you know it."

He gives me several seconds anyway, which I appreciate even while complaining about it. I contain multitudes, apparently, several of which are contradictory.

I open it.

Not much inside. A folded set of civilian clothes that don't match the ship's utility gray. A small stack of actual paper, handwritten notes in my own cramped scrawl, none of which I remember writing. I appear to be a man of few possessions and questionable penmanship. And, wedged in the back corner like someone snuck it past inspection:

A single, slightly crushed box labeled, in handwriting that is absolutely not mine, in looping cursive:

FOR WHEN YOU FORGET HOW TO HAVE FUN. -P.

A small heart has been drawn in place of the period after the P, drawn without thinking, muscle memory more than decoration.

Real food. Actual solid food, not tube gravy. A dense, dried fruit bar, six of them, individually wrapped, and I'm staring at this box like it personally solved the Phengoclept crisis.

Six bars. I run the rationing math. The math recommends discipline. I'm ignoring the math.

"What," Jinzhou says.

"Someone smuggled me snacks."

"Define smuggled."

"I don't know, but they hid it well enough that ARACCSIM didn't flag it during inventory, which means either Argon's quality control is worse than I thought or this person is, uh, genuinely talented at crime. They signed it with a P, and I think." I stop. Something warm and stupid rises in my chest. "I think I know exactly who that is."

"Helpful?"

"Extremely helpful. Morally, this is the best thing that's happened to me since I woke up, and I'm including the part where I, shoot, the part where I learned my own name in that ranking."

Jinzhou opens his own locker without ceremony. Stares into it for a long moment.

"KENZO, J." he reads off his nameplate, mostly to himself. Inside: neatly folded gray clothes, identical to mine, and a single object sitting dead center like it was placed there on purpose.

A small square of paper, folded so many times the creases have gone soft and gray.

He opens it slowly, with the care you give something you already know matters. A crayon drawing, or close enough. A shape that might be a house. A shape that might be him, if you squint, all stick legs and triangle ears, but unmistakably him.

No signature. It doesn't need one.

"No note," he says anyway.

"You don't need one for that."

"I don't understand it."

"You will. Give it time." I hold up one of the fruit bars. "In the meantime. Trade you a snack for information on the drawing."

He looks at the bar. Looks at the drawing. Folds it carefully back along its own creases, like it might tear otherwise, and tucks it into the locker before taking the bar instead.

"Deal," he says. "The drawing stays."

"Wasn't asking for the drawing. I have some boundaries."

"Good. You wouldn't have gotten it regardless of your boundaries."

We sit there for a minute in the narrow little passage, eating stolen morale food six point four light years from anyone who packed it for us. For the first time since I woke up not knowing my own name, something in my chest unclenches, just slightly, just enough to notice.

"Two thousand one hundred days," Jinzhou says eventually, apropos of nothing, chewing.

"Please don't ruin the snack. I was having a moment."

"Just context."

"I know the context. I did the math, remember. I'm the one who, uh, does the math around here. That's my whole contribution to this operation."

"I remember." He almost smiles. It's a small thing, barely a shift in his whiskers, but I'll take it. "Good bar, though."

"Told you."

Somewhere above us, ARACCSIM logs the moment in whatever way ARACCSIM logs things, silent and unbothered, storing it next to fuel percentages and diagnostic checkmarks as if it's just as important, which, honestly, at this point, it might be.

*END OF CHAPTER THREE*
