
I'm going to be honest. I don't remember most of the first week.

Not in the amnesia way. I've got plenty of that already. I mean the normal way, the way you don't remember most of a long drive. The ship pushes at one point seven G the whole time, and one point seven G makes everything take longer and feel heavier and end with a nap. So the week comes back to me in pieces. Some of the pieces are important. Most of them are Jinzhou and me pointing at things.

These are the pieces.

## Day Two

The first word I learn is *berat*.

I don't learn it on purpose. I'm lying on the medbay floor, because the floor and I have reached an agreement, and Jinzhou walks past me, stops, looks down at me, and says, "*Berat.*"

"Is that a diagnosis?"

He points at the floor. He pushes down on the air with one flat paw, slow and hard, like he's pressing a lid onto a box that doesn't want to close. "*Berat.*"

"Heavy," I say. "Berat is heavy." I try it. "*Berat.*" It comes out flat, like a door that won't latch.

"R is hard," he says. "Have to roll."

I try again. It doesn't roll. It sort of tips over.

He nods once anyway. Then he lies down next to me. He doesn't explain. He doesn't need to.

We lie there on the floor, two grown professionals, being heavy together.

"*Berat,*" he says again, after a while, to the ceiling.

"Heavy," I agree.

That's the whole lesson. It's a good one. I still use it.

## Day Two, Later

Here's my problem. I know some of his language. A little. Not enough to do anything with, but enough to be confusing.

Somebody taught me. I don't know who. When I reach for it, there's a kitchen table, and a laminated sheet of phrases, and somebody laughing at my vowels. That's all I get. I don't push it. I've learned that pushing just makes the memories hide.

What I have from the laminated sheet is this: hello, thank you, yes, no, and one complete sentence.

I try the sentence on him at dinner. Dinner is a tube.

"*Di mana toilet?*"

Jinzhou stops chewing. He looks at me for a long second. Then he points, without a word, at the little door at the end of the medbay. The one with a picture of a toilet on it.

"Thank you," I say. "*Terima kasih.* I knew where it was. I just wanted to see if it worked."

"*Sama-sama,*" he says, and goes back to his tube.

So that's two things I know now. The sentence works. And Jinzhou thinks I'm an idiot.

## Day Three

His English comes back in the wrong order.

You'd think it'd be the small words first. Yes, no, the, a. The words a toddler gets. It isn't. It's the big ones. He's reading a coolant readout on the Lab B console, in the language I can't read, and he points at a number and says, out of nowhere, in perfect English, "Pressure."

Then he looks surprised. Then he says, "Efficient."

Then, a minute later, about nothing at all: "Harness."

"Those are great words," I tell him. "Those are very professional words. Do you have 'and'?"

He thinks about it. Visibly. Like he's checking a drawer.

"No."

He has "no," though. Good. That one's useful.

So that's where we are on day three. He can tell me the coolant pressure in two languages and he can't tell me he's hungry in either one. Meanwhile I can ask where the toilet is and that's it. Between the two of us, we make about one functional adult.

## Day Three, Later

He reads the console out loud. That's how I learn the most.

He doesn't do it for me. I think he does it to make the words hold still. He traces a line with his finger and says it under his breath, fast and flat, and I sit next to him and catch whatever I can.

"*Tekanan tangki tiga stabil.*"

Okay. That's too much. That's a whole sentence of engineer. But in the middle of it there's a word I know, because I've been counting things my whole life.

"Tiga," I say. "Three. That's three."

He looks at me. "*Tiga.*" He holds up three fingers.

"*Satu, dua, tiga,*" I say, and he blinks, because I didn't know I knew that until it came out. It came from the laminated sheet. Apparently the sheet had numbers on the back.

He keeps going. "*Empat. Lima. Enam.*"

"Four, five, six."

"*Tujuh.*"

Seven.

He gets a look. It's a very small look, on a face that doesn't do many looks. Then he says, very carefully, in a voice that's flatter than usual, which on Jinzhou is almost a performance:

"*Tiga tambah empat?*"

Three plus four.

I stare at him. He stares back.

"No," I say. "Absolutely not. I'm not doing this with you."

"*Tiga tambah empat?*"

"*Tujuh,*" I say, through my teeth. "It's seven. It's always been seven. Leave me alone."

He doesn't smile. But his ears go up about a quarter of an inch, and he turns back to the console, and he's quiet in a way that feels, I swear, smug.

So he got the same question I did when he woke up. Of course he did. Same ship. Same computer. It just asked him in his language. I'd love to know if he got it right the first time. I'm not going to ask. I don't want to know.

## Day Four

I'm teaching him English with objects. It's the only method I've got. I pick up a thing, I say the thing, he says the thing back.

"Cup."

"Cup."

"Tube."

"Tube."

"Glasses." I point at my face.

"Glasses." He points at my face too, which isn't how it works, but I let it go.

"Sock."

He looks at the sock. He looks at me. He looks at the arm on the ceiling, which still has my other sock hanging off its wrist, because I've given up on getting it back.

"*Kaus kaki,*" he says.

"I know that one," I say. "You said it on day one, looking at that arm."

I've been saying it all week. I just never took it apart.

He nods.

And then he does something I don't expect. He taps his own chest, his shirt, and says "*Kaus.*" Then he taps his foot. "*Kaki.*"

Shirt. Foot.

Wait.

"Foot shirt," I say. "Your word for sock is foot shirt."

"Yes."

"That's the best word I've ever heard. That's better than sock. Sock is a terrible word. It sounds like what happens when you drop something heavy. Foot shirt is a description. You know exactly what a foot shirt is the first time you hear it."

Jinzhou looks at me the way you'd look at a dog who just discovered its own tail.

"Kaus kaki," he says, patient.

"Foot shirt," I say, delighted.

We do not agree on which one is better. We never will.

## Day Four, Later

Things I learn about his language, in the order I learn them:

One. *Air* means water. Water is *air*. I learn this when I ask for water and he hands me water and says "*air,*" and I spend a good ten seconds trying to work out why he's describing the atmosphere. (I'm going to drown in this language. I'll be the first person to drown in air.)

Two. If you want more than one of something, you can say it twice. One star is *bintang*. Stars are *bintang-bintang*.

"That's it?" I ask him.

"Not always." He thinks. "If it is clear, *bintang* is enough. Many star. Still *bintang*."

"So you can say it twice, or you can just not, and everybody knows anyway? No 's'? No 'es'? No 'mouse, mice, goose, geese'?"

"Yes."

"Your language is better than mine," I say, and I mean it, and I think about it for most of the afternoon. I have a doctorate. Possibly several. Nobody ever told me English was this badly organized. I think everybody knew and agreed not to say anything.

Which is the real lesson, I think. Half of his language is the listener filling in the blanks. You don't say the thing everybody already knows. You trust them to have it.

Two and a half. It's not only plurals. That evening I'm going up the ladder too fast, at one point seven G, and my foot slips, and Jinzhou says, from the bottom, "*Pelan-pelan.*"

Slow slow.

"Is that two slows?"

"Yes. Slow. More slow."

So sometimes saying it twice means there's more than one. And sometimes it just means *more*. Careful is *hati-hati*. Slow is *pelan-pelan*. You say it twice because once wasn't enough.

I like that a lot. It's how I talk when I'm excited anyway. Good good good. Okay okay okay. Turns out there's a whole language where that's correct.

Three. *Makan* is eat. *Enak* is tasty. *Lapar* is hungry. These come up a lot. We're on tubes.

Four. *Sudah* is already, done. *Belum* is not yet. Not no. Not yet. There's a whole word that just means it'll happen, give it time. I'm going to need that one.

## Day Five

He eats my fruit bar. The one I was saving.

I'm not upset about it. I want that on record. I'm a little upset about it. I had it set aside for the evening, on the bench in Lab B, under a clipboard, where nobody could possibly find it. He found it. He found it, ate it, and folded the wrapper into a very small, very neat square, and put the square on the bench where the bar used to be. Like a receipt.

I pick up the square. I look at him.

He looks back. Then he says, slowly, the way you'd carry something full to the brim:

"Sorry."

It's the first time. Five days of pressure and harness and coolant and efficient, and that's the one that took the longest.

"Are you?" I ask.

He considers the question honestly. He always does.

"Bar was good," he says.

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

I put the little square in my pocket. I don't know why. I still have it.

## Day Five, Later

*Maaf* is sorry, too. He tells me that afterward, like an apology for the apology. *Maaf.* It's softer than ours. It sounds like somebody putting down a cup very gently so they won't wake anybody.

I think he had *maaf* the whole time. I think the English one was just hard to find.

## Day Six

The best word I learn all week is *matahari*.

We're in Lab B in the evening. The camera feed is up. The star's on the screen, the one that isn't ours, a pale gold disc turning once every thirty-two days. I point at it. "What's your word for that?"

"*Matahari.*"

"Matahari," I say. It's long. It's pretty. "Is that just 'sun'? Or does it mean something?"

He frowns. Not at me. At the word. Then he points at his own eye. "*Mata.*"

"Eye."

Then he waves at the room, the screen, the whole lit-up span of the day, the way you'd gesture at a morning. "*Hari.*"

"Day."

Eye.

Day.

Oh.

"The eye of the day," I say. "Your word for sun is the eye of the day."

"Yes."

"Did you know that? Like, did you always know that, or did you just figure it out right now?"

He thinks about it. "Now," he says. "I always say it. I never look at it."

I know exactly what he means. You can say a word ten thousand times and never once take it apart to see how it works. It's like living in a house for twenty years and then one day noticing the door.

I look at the screen for a long time. The wrong star. The eye of somebody else's day.

"*Matahari,*" I say again, quietly. I'm going to keep that one.

## Day Six, Later

We figure out a system.

He teaches me one word in his language. I teach him one word in mine. One for one. Fair trade. He insists on that. He keeps count.

By the evening of day six, I have *berat, air, makan, enak, lapar, tidak, ya, bintang, matahari, maaf, kaus kaki, belum, selamat pagi, selamat malam, selamat tidur,* and *di mana toilet,* which I still maintain is a full sentence and should count as four.

He has *heavy, water, eat, good, hungry, no, yes, star, sun, sorry, sock, not yet, good morning, good evening, good night,* and *where is the toilet,* which he refuses to count at all, on the grounds that he's never going to need it.

"What if you're lost?" I ask.

"I am not lost. I know where it is."

"What if you're somewhere new?"

He looks around the ship. The medbay. The ladder. The six beds and the two of us. The wrong star on the screen.

"Where?" he asks.

Fair point.

That night, going into my cubby, I say, "*Selamat malam.*"

"*Tidur,*" he corrects me, from his. "*Selamat tidur.* Malam is evening. Tidur is sleep."

"So there's a good night for when it's night, and a good night for when you're going to bed."

"Yes."

"*Selamat tidur,* Jinzhou."

"*Selamat tidur,*" he says.

## Day Seven

I wake up before him. I sit up in my cubby, which takes about a full minute at one point seven G, and look across the medbay.

He's awake too. He's just lying still with his eyes open, the way he does. He looks over.

"*Selamat pagi,*" I say.

"Good morning," he says.

Two words. Right order. My language.

Neither of us says anything about it. That's how we do things. You don't make a big deal about the good stuff, in case it hears you and leaves.

But I lie back down for a second. Just one. I'm a little proud of him. And I'm a little proud of me. We started the week with one word between us, and it was *halo*, and now we can say good morning in both directions.

It's not much. It's two people, very heavy, in a very small ship, a very long way from anybody.

It's enough to start with.

"Okay," I say, and sit up again. "*Makan?*"

"Makan," Jinzhou agrees.

We go get tubes.

*END OF CHAPTER THREE*
