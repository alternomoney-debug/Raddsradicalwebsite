Moving fuel between two ships turns out to be mostly a plumbing problem, and plumbing problems in space are the same as plumbing problems anywhere else, except that if you get one wrong, nobody can call anyone.

Petryn lays it out in Lab B the night before, on the whiteboard, under Bejors' header, in a drawing that looks like a family tree for pipes.

"MK2's third tank has a transfer port on the outside. Every Vanguard does. It's how they filled us on the ground." He draws a line from it. "Your tanks got the same port. Same spec, same fittings. Argon don't retool. So I run a line from their port to yours, outside, along the collar. Then we pump."

"How long?"

"Five hours. Maybe six. Fuel's cold and it don't like to hurry."

"Outside," I say.

"Outside," Petryn agrees. "There's no other way to a tank. Not on your ship and not on ours."

"Who goes?"

"Me," Petryn says. "And somebody to hold the light and the other end of the line."

---

Nobody asks me to go outside. I notice that.

I bring it up anyway, at the airlock, on the morning of the transfer, while Petryn is checking the coil of transfer line for the fourth time and Jinzhou is pulling on a suit with the arm that healed while we slept and that he still holds a little close, with an expression that dares anyone to mention it.

"I can go," I say. "I've done an EVA. I'm practically a veteran."

"You were hit by a locker," Jinzhou says, without looking up.

"Once."

"You were hit by a locker once, and then you were unconscious, and then I had to carry you."

"That's an unfair summary of an extremely brave afternoon."

"It is an accurate summary." He gets the second glove on and flexes the fingers, slowly, the left paw a little slower than the right. "You are the only one on this ship who can read a fuel slide. If the line carries anything it should not, you are the one who will see it. You are more useful in here."

I'd like to argue with that, and I can't, which is the most annoying kind of argument there is.

"Fine," I say. "Then I'm checking your seal."

He stops. Looks at me. Then he tips his head back and lets me.

The helmet goes on the way mine did, lower piece up from under the chin, upper piece down to meet it, the seam settling across the middle of his face like a horizon. The gaskets find each other. *Tsss.* I run a glove along the seam, both sides, the whole way around. Then I go back to the start and do it again.

"You are doing the thing where you check twice," Jinzhou says, through the suit speaker, flat and a little tinny.

"Yes."

A pause. Long enough that I think that's the end of it.

"Do not stop," he says.

I tap the top of his helmet twice, the way he did to mine, and I don't say anything else, because if I do, it'll come out wrong.

Petryn, beside us, has been watching all of this with his usual slow almost-smile.

"Y'all are somethin' else," he says, and lowers his own helmet, and the airlock takes them both.

---

It happens outside, and I watch it on a screen, which is its own kind of terrible.

Petryn goes out along the collar with the line coiled across his chest and works himself over to MK2's top tank, the scorched one, rail by rail, like a man climbing a ladder that someone has laid on its side and then set on fire. Jinzhou follows with the other end. Marsh reads the line pressure out loud every ten minutes, in a voice she has clearly decided is neutral. Maslin volunteers for the job nobody else wants, which is sitting in the cockpit steering the hull floodlight so it stays on Petryn's paws.

"It's the most important job on the ship," I tell him.

"you dont have to say that"

"I know. I'm saying it anyway."

I'm in Lab B with a sample tap on our side of the line and the cold stage ready. Every hour, the pump pauses for thirty seconds, and I pull a drop of whatever is arriving, and I look at it, and I say "clean" into the comm. It's the easiest job I've had since I woke up, and I hold my breath for every single one of them.

Their suits are good for six hours and forty minutes. I watch that number too.

At hour four and twenty minutes, the floodlight drifts.

It's not much. A degree, maybe two. The beam slides off Petryn's paws and onto the strut beside him, and on the screen his work goes from bright to a gray blur, and over the comm I hear him say, very calmly, "Little help, man."

Maslin is already on it. He's in the cockpit with both paws on the floodlight controls, and he's been there for four hours and twenty minutes without once letting go, and when it drifts he brings it back in about a second and a half. Then he locks it, and then he keeps holding it anyway.

"sorry," he says. "sorry. its back. its back petryn"

"I see it, man. You're good. You're doin' good."

"i had it the whole time i just- the mount slipped- its not me its the mount"

"I know it's the mount."

At hour five and forty minutes, Petryn says, "That's the last of it. Closin' the port." The pressure on Marsh's readout drops to nothing. A minute later he says "Port closed," and a minute after that, "Line's comin' home," and I let out a breath that goes all the way to the floor I don't have.

"Transfer complete," ARACCSIM says. "Tank one and tank three, filled to capacity. Combined reserve now exceeds the departure and arrival profile by nine percent."

"Say it again," I say.

"Nine percent."

They come back through the airlock with fifty-one minutes of air left. Petryn calls that plenty. Jinzhou calls it fifty-one minutes.

Maslin doesn't let go of the floodlight controls until I go up and tell him he can. He looks down at his paws like he'd forgotten they were his.

"i didnt let go," he says.

"I know. I was watching."

"good," he says. "somebody should know."

---

We undock in the evening.

There's no ceremony for it, because nobody planned one, and because I think if anybody tried, it would go wrong. ARACCSIM releases the collar. A soft clunk through the hull, and then a long quiet, and then the small, polite knocks of the attitude thrusters, nudging us away.

Maslin watches MK2 on the hull camera the whole time. It gets smaller slowly, the way a building does when you drive away from it, and then it stops getting smaller, and just hangs there, dark, a kilometer off, turning a little.

"bye you stupid ship," he says.

Nobody tells him that's not a goodbye. It obviously is.

"Its gonna drift around out here forever huh"

"Probably," I say. "Or until it finds the planet. It's got a long time."

"its got my tally marks on it"

"I know."

"a hundred and twenty two," he says. "nobody's ever gonna count them again"

"I counted them," I say. "That's enough. Somebody counted them once."

He doesn't say anything to that. After a while he unhooks from the rail and goes to find Petryn, and I hear them in the hall, not talking, which on this ship is a kind of talking.

---

"ARACCSIM," Jinzhou says the next morning. "Tank walls."

"With tank 2-E empty and tanks one and three free of Asicell, tank walls may return to level one. Feed line and motor housings must remain at level two. Bus draw at that configuration, thirty-one percent."

"Centrifuge?"

"Centrifuge operation is available."

Jinzhou looks at me. I look at Jinzhou. Neither of us says it, because both of us know the other one is thinking it, and that's a whole conversation already.

"Everybody strap in," I say instead, over the ship-wide. "We're getting a floor."

The keys go in together this time, mine and Jinzhou's, at the panel in the cockpit. No sailing past slot two. No laminated card. Two people, two keys, the way the sign meant it.

SPIN-UP INITIATED. 1G IN 5 MINUTES. SECURE ALL LOOSE ITEMS.

I feel the latches let go first. A long shudder, and then nothing, and then the slow, strange sensation of the crew section moving away from everything else, paying out along its cables, the tanks and the engines falling behind us into the dark until we're two halves of one ship swinging around each other like a pair of skaters holding paws.

Then weight comes back the way a rumor does. First a lean. Then a lean you can feel. Then the whole room turns ninety degrees around me without anything actually moving, and the wall becomes a floor, and the floor pushes back.

Somewhere behind me in the cockpit, Maslin makes a noise I can only describe as a happy cough.

"oh my god," he says. "down. theres a down. i forgot down"

Marsh, who has been weightless for longer than any of us, stands up very carefully, holds onto the back of her seat, and does not say anything for a long time. When she lets go, she keeps one paw near it, just in case.

---

Dinner that night is the first meal any of us has eaten sitting down in a very long time.

Petryn insists on it. He finds a folding table in the storage block that nobody on MK1 knew we had, sets it up in the middle of the medbay floor between the two stacks of berths, and puts five tubes on it in a row like a waiter laying out silverware.

"It's tubes," Maslin says.

"It's tubes on a table, man. That's a whole different meal."

He's right, and I hate that he's right. We sit on the edges of the berths and the floor and one upturned crate, five people around a table built for none of us, and I squeeze my tube onto a tray the arms bring down, and the food stays on the tray. It just stays there. It doesn't drift. It doesn't wander off toward the ceiling to start a new life. It sits on the tray and waits for me like food is supposed to.

I look at it for long enough that Jinzhou notices.

"It is not going anywhere," he says.

"I know. I'm enjoying that."

Marsh eats with a napkin on her lap, which I've decided not to ask about, because the napkin is plainly the most important thing in her week. Maslin eats like he's worried somebody will take it. Petryn eats slow and talks between bites, about nothing, about a diner near the launch site that did a sandwich so big it had its own weather. Jinzhou eats exactly half of his, folds the tube, and clips it into the pouch with the other folded tubes, and then eats the other half.

Nobody mentions the feed line. Nobody mentions the Asicell still asleep in it, or the restart, or what it's going to cost to get them out. For one meal, on one table, with a floor under it, there's nothing to fix.

"This is nice," Maslin says, at the end, suspiciously, like he's waiting for the catch.

"It is," I say. "It's allowed to be."

"since when"

"Since about an hour ago. I checked. There's a down now. Things are allowed to be nice when there's a down."

He thinks about that.

"ok," he says. "ill allow it."

---

The grates in Lab A lift on their own once the spin settles. I hear them from the hall, a long rattle and a clank, like a shop opening for the morning.

I go in by myself.

It's exactly as I left it. The microscope on its mount. The autoclave. The sample fridge and the oven, back level on their gimbals. And on the far wall, upright again the way a whiteboard is supposed to be, in her handwriting, quick and careful and readable by somebody who wasn't her:

Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP

Underlined twice.

I stand in front of it for a while. I don't touch it. I don't need to. I've been looking at my copy of it for months, and it turns out the copy did its job, which was to keep me company until I could come back and see the real one.

"Hi," I say. "We're going home. Probably. I'll keep you posted."

Then I go back to Lab B, where my copy is, and I leave both of them up. One for her. One for us.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO*
