Maslin has found my book.

I don't know how. I didn't bring a copy, I would never bring a copy, and in all the months the archive downstairs has been sitting there with most of what anyone ever wrote on it, I've been very careful never to look myself up. But on the morning of day sixteen as a five-person ship, he floats into Lab B holding a tablet in both paws with the expression of a man who has found a photo of his teacher at a nightclub.

"is this you," he says.

It's the cover. *PHENGOCLEPT: A STUDY GUIDE*, in the font the publisher insisted on, with my name underneath it in letters smaller than the word GUIDE.

"Where did you get that?"

"mk2 crew library," he says. "it was loaded on our tablets. it was the only book on there with pictures"

"Argon put my book on your ship?"

"argon put your book on everyones ship. petryn has it. marsh has it. marsh has *notes* on it"

I take a moment with that. Somebody at the program office, who knew about a second ship I didn't, decided that the three people on it should have my study guide for the trip. They didn't tell me about the ship. They did give it my book.

"I'm going to need a minute to decide how I feel about that."

"take your time," Maslin says. "i read chapter nine"

"Nobody reads chapter nine."

"i did. four months in a cockpit. i read everything. i read the sleeping bag one twice." He scrolls. "it says stay calm in an unfamiliar environment. it says focus on what you can control and make a list"

"I wrote that in a café."

"did it have a window"

"It had a very nice window."

He looks at me over the top of the tablet. "did it work. the list thing. for you."

I think about the medbay, the four empty cubbies, the fishing cable, the cold shutdown. Lists, every time. Numbers on a whiteboard. Things I could control, written down so I could see how small they were.

"It worked," I say. "It worked badly. But it worked."

"same," Maslin says, and that's apparently all he needed, because he floats off toward the medbay with the tablet under his arm, and I hear him say to Petryn in the hall, "its him. its really him. he says it worked badly."

"That's the best review you ever gonna get, man," Petryn says.

I want to argue. I find I can't.

---

Petryn finds me in Lab B that afternoon with a data pad and the look of somebody who has been up too long with a question.

"Ethyn. Can I show you somethin'?"

He turns the pad toward me. A graph, mostly flat, with tiny spikes along the bottom edge, like a pulse on a monitor that's trying not to be noticed.

"MK2's tank logs," he says. "I been through the cache twice since you got here. Third time, I looked at somethin' I'd been callin' sensor noise."

"What is it?"

"Excitation events. Cells lightin' off with no lure pulse. Little ones, weeks before the first tank went."

"Lighting off for no reason," I say. "That's not a cause. That's a symptom."

"Yeah. That's what bugged me." He scrolls. "A cell only goes off like that when it thinks it sees Aluk'. Carbon dioxide. But they can't see through steel, man. Not through a hull, not through the thermal panels. The panels are built to stop exactly that. So for a cell to see CO2, the CO2's gotta be right up against the tank."

Okay. Why would that matter?

Because carbon dioxide glows. Not so you'd see it. In infrared. Warm CO2 gives off a very particular kind of infrared, and Phengoclept has spent a few billion years learning to love that exact glow. That's how it finds Aluk'. The thermal panels around a tank are there to block that kind of glow. But if the gas got *behind* the panels, there's nothing left in the way except the tank's own skin. And the skin is thin.

"How would it get behind the panels?" I ask.

"That's the third time through." Petryn pulls up a different file. A build record. Work orders, rows and rows of them, every line signed. "MK2 got put together in orbit, fast, so she could launch four months behind you. Look at this one."

She taps a line. Tank bay panel install, tanks one and two. Signed complete. Fastener count, one short of spec. And a note in the margin: *Seal on scrubber vent run deferred. Fit at next service.*

"There was no next service," I say.

"There was no next service." She taps the screen again. "Scrubber vent runs right through that bay. Behind the panels. One bad bolt, one seal nobody fit, and every time the scrubbers cycled, a little bit of our breath leaked into the gap between the tank and its panels."

"So the cells see carbon dioxide."

"Right up against the wall. Close as it gets."

"And they think Aluk' is right there."

"And they go," Petryn says. "But there's a wall in the way."

A wall. Like Kalen's cube. They jumped at it.

"And jumping cells dump everything," I say. "So they get hot."

"And hot cells get more excited."

"And at the bottom of every runaway is the word bang."

"Never heard it said like that." He nods slowly. "Accurate."

"So the fuel did not break your ship," Marsh says, from the doorway. I didn't hear her arrive. She has a way of arriving. "A bolt did."

"Bolt broke it. Fuel did the rest." He scrolls again, slower. "But here's the thing I come to show you. The third tank. The top one, the one that got cooked on the outside and didn't go." He taps it. "Other side of the ship. Different bay, different shift, different work order. Every bolt signed. No vent run anywhere near it. And the log's flat. Not one flash."

I stop breathing for a second. Not dramatically. Just long enough to notice.

"So the fuel in it was never the problem."

"Never was. And it's still sittin' out there, sealed, with most of its load in it."

"Logs aren't a sample."

"No," Petryn says. "That's why I come to you."

---

Getting a sample takes most of the next day.

MK2's fuel taps still answer to Petryn, the way old machines answer to whoever has been patient with them the longest. She draws a vial from the third tank through the engineering panel in the dead cockpit, walks it back through the collar in a cold box, and hands it to me like she's handing over a pet that might bite.

I put a drop on the cold stage and look.

Phengoclept. Small bright emitters at twenty-two point seven zero six, exactly where they should be, exactly as many as a fuel sample ought to show. No smear. No crowd. No little blue-membraned diners. Just fuel, being fuel, which is the most beautiful thing I've seen under glass since the day I found the Asicell.

"Clean of Asicell," I say. "That's one."

"What's two?"

"Two is whether they're still good fuel. Whether they still want to go."

I don't have an Aluk'. I have the next best thing, which is our own breath. Jinzhou rigs a line off the scrubber outlet, a thread of the carbon dioxide we've been storing so carefully away from our tanks, and I bleed the smallest breath of it across the slide.

The cells scramble.

All of them. At once. They turn toward the line like a crowd hearing its name. Little flashes, the slide lighting up in a hundred places.

Yes!

That's perfect. That's exactly what they should do. Healthy cells want Aluk'. That's the whole engine. A cell that ignored carbon dioxide would be a broken cell, and a broken cell won't jump when the lure says go. These ones would jump at anything that smells like home.

Which means the danger was never in them. It was in where you keep them.

"Jinzhou. Our tanks. The gaps behind our panels."

He's already looking. "Gap pressure on all three tanks is hard vacuum. No carbon dioxide. No leak."

"Since launch?"

"Since launch." A pause. "Somebody on our build crew did their bolts."

"I'd like to send that person a fruit basket."

"Not one leak," I say. "And the fuel is good."

Petryn lets out a breath she's clearly been holding for most of a day. Maslin, who has been hanging upside down in the doorway pretending not to care, says "is that good. thats good right. somebody say its good"

"It's good," I say. "It's very good. It's the first good thing that's happened to anybody's fuel on either of these ships."

---

Maslin wants the rest of it, so Petryn tells him, and it takes about two sentences. A bolt went in one short during the rush in orbit, and a seal never got fit. Their own breath leaked in behind the panels, and that is what blew the tanks.

Maslin doesn't say anything for long enough that I notice.

"a bolt" he says. "one bolt"

"One bolt and one seal."

"one bolt" Maslin says. He hangs there, one boot hooked on the rail, looking at nothing. "four months in that cockpit i thought it was something i did. something i signed for. the loadout i should have checked-"

"Man," Petryn says. "That panel went on before any of us came aboard. You couldn't have checked it. Nobody walked that bay but the build crew."

"i know that. i do know that. its just- its a lot better than something i did"

"There is no step for it," Jinzhou says.

Maslin looks at him.

"I checked the crew procedures." Jinzhou's voice has not changed in any way. "There is no step for the tank bay panels. It was signed off before launch. It was never on your plate."

"you checked"

"Yes."

"when"

"Last week."

Maslin opens his mouth, and then closes it.

"thanks," he says, quietly, and I decide I didn't hear it, so that he can have it all to himself.

---

We hold the meeting in Lab B, because it's the only lab that's open and because it's the room with the whiteboard. Nobody says the second part.

Marsh does the numbers. She's been doing them since the vial came back clean, and she lays them out on the board under Bejors' header in handwriting that looks typeset.

"Our reserve, after what the Asicell ate on the way out of orbit, still clears what it takes to get home and stop, but not by much," she says. "Tank 2-E holds fourteen percent of it, and 2-E is full of Asicell. We cannot burn it. We cannot keep it. Every day it sits in our tanks, we are one bad thermostat away from losing the rest."

"so we vent it," Maslin says.

"So we vent it. That leaves us short." She draws a line, and then another under it. "MK2's third tank holds a little over a fifth of a full load. Clean, by Ethyn's slide. If we move it across, we replace 2-E and then some. We arrive home with margin."

"And the ones in the lines," Jinzhou says. "The feed line. The motor housings."

"Are not solved by this," Marsh agrees. "This removes the crowd. It does not remove the stragglers."

"One problem at a time," I say. "The crowd's the one that can sink us."

"Then it's a trade," Marsh says. "We give up a tank of our own fuel and gamble on a tank of theirs. If Ethyn's slide is wrong, we have swapped a known problem for an unknown one." She looks at the board, and then, in a way I'm not ready for, at me. "Dr. Vasek."

I'm holding a marker. I don't remember picking it up.

"Under the Initiative's chain of authority," she says, formally, in the same voice she uses to read wall temperatures, "the primary scientist is deceased. The secondary scientist holds the decision on any matter concerning the Phengoclept or its antagonist. I would like to hear it from you."

I want to say that I'm a study guide author. I want to say it so badly my ears go flat, and it's in my mouth, right there, practiced, ready. *I'm a study guide author, not certified for anything.* It's been a good line. It's gotten me out of a lot of rooms.

But nobody in this room is going to let me out of it, and that isn't cruelty. It's something else. One of them has seen me in the cold and on the cable. Three of them have seen me scream through a door about a suit. They're not asking what I am. They're asking what I think.

I think about every time someone decided something for me. Y'shari and her clause. A clause number I memorized for no reason except that it was the only paperwork I ever got. The facility, the launch, a dormitory with a door that opened on me and nobody else. I think about how I never said yes to a single piece of this. Not the mission, not the ship, not the part where I woke up and everyone I'd trained with was dead.

I was never asked.

"Hm," I say. "Give me a second."

I look at the board. At Bejors' header, in my copy of her handwriting. At Marsh's two lines. At the slide, still on the cold stage behind me, with its clean bright fuel that still wants to go.

"Vent it," I say. "Vent 2-E. We take MK2's tank." My voice doesn't shake, and I'm a little offended by that. "Yes. I'm saying yes. Put it in the log."

"Logged," ARACCSIM says. "Authority registered, Dr. Vasek."

"Say it again for the record," Jinzhou says.

"I said yes."

"I know." The flat voice, the monotone. Under it, for half a second, something that on anyone else I'd call pride. "I wanted it twice."

---

We vent it the next morning, from the cockpit, all five of us crowded around the hull camera like it's a fireplace.

"Before we do this," Marsh says, "I would like someone to tell me where it goes."

"Out," I say. "And then down." I point past the camera's edge, at the hazy little coin of Bejors. "Anything that comes out of that tank and goes looking for a planet only has one planet to find. And that planet is where the Asicell come from. Its whole atmosphere is the thing that eats Phengoclept."

"So if any of the fuel goes lookin' for home," Petryn says slowly, "it finds the one place that kills it."

"And the Asicell go home with it."

Maslin thinks about this. "thats kind of nice actually," he says. "in a gross way"

"Most of my job is nice in a gross way."

"ARACCSIM," Marsh says. "Tank 2-E. Vent on crew authority."

"Venting."

It doesn't make a sound. Nothing out here does. On the screen, a valve on the outside of tank two opens, and a plume comes out of it, white and glittering, a long slow breath of frozen fuel turning over in the light. It spreads. It catches the far sun and holds it for a second, like frost on a window, and then it keeps going, thinning out into the dark in the direction of the little hazy planet that started all of this.

Somewhere in that cloud is a crowd of very patient diners, going back where they were born.

"Goodbye," I say to them, very quietly. I mean it more than I expected to.

"Tank 2-E, empty," ARACCSIM says. "Remaining tanks, wall temperature minus fifty-five degrees Celsius. No Asicell detected in tanks one or three."

"Say it again."

"No Asicell detected in tanks one or three."

I let go of the rail with both paws and hang there, grinning at a gauge. Maslin whoops. Marsh writes something on her tablet and underlines it twice. Jinzhou says nothing at all and drifts a little closer to the screen, which from him is applause.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO*
