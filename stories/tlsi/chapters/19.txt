
Nobody decides to start working. It just happens. The feelings run out of road, the practical part takes the wheel, and somebody has to be the somebody. Standing around in a dead cockpit isn't a plan. I checked.

"ARACCSIM," I say, once the cockpit has stopped being a standoff and started being a meeting. Same people. Fewer folded arms. "I need you patched into whatever you can reach on this ship. Structural status. Anything."

"Attempting connection now. Blip-D's systems are largely non-responsive. I am able to establish limited contact with residual power systems only."

"Limited is better than nothing."

"Agreed."

Maslin watches this like he's never heard a ship's computer agree with anyone.

"That thing's got a personality," he says. "Thats like- thats a weird flex for a spaceship but okay"

"You have no idea," I say.

---

"Right, so," I say, once ARACCSIM has told us what it can and can't reach, which is mostly can't. "Let's figure out what we're working with. Structural. Supplies. What's salvageable, what isn't. I don't want to decide anything about what happens next until I understand the shape of the problem."

Maslin snorts.

"Shape of the problem," he repeats. "Man you sound like every briefin i ever sat through before all this- i love that for you, truly, never change"

"I'm trying to be organized about a crisis. It's the only hobby I brought."

"Im not sayin its bad, im sayin its funny, theres a difference." He hooks himself into the seat that's apparently also his bed and folds his arms. He's stopped treating us like a threat. He hasn't stopped checking. "Go ahead, do your little assessment thing, ill just be over here, existin"

Petryn is already drifting toward one of the dead terminals, unhurried, running a paw along its edge like he's saying hello to it.

"This one might still have somethin' in it," he says. "Local cache, even if the network's shot. Won't know till I try, though, and I don't wanna get anybody's hopes up before I know for sure. That's a bad habit. Gettin' folks hopeful before you got a reason to."

"Take your time," I say.

"Oh, I intend to," he says, like rushing never occurred to him. "Rushin' a dead system just breaks it worse. Learned that the hard way, more than once."

Marsh hasn't said much since her one question earlier. She's anchored beside a small console tucked into the corner. It took me a minute to see it was a flight station. It took her none.

"Dr. Vasek," she says, with a careful formality neither of the other two has bothered with. They dropped straight into first names. "May I ask what your ship's current fuel status is. I understand if that isn't information you're prepared to share with strangers."

It's the correct question. What gets me is how carefully she asks it. She's sanded every edge off it first. I could hand it to a toddler.

"You can ask," I say. "I don't mind sharing. We've got enough fuel to get home. We just can't use it. That comes with conditions, and most of them are cold. It's a, uh, long story, and I'd like to tell it in order. We weren't budgeting for extra passengers."

"Cold," she says.

"In order," I say. "I promise."

"I understand." She nods once, precise. "I don't want to presume anything about what's possible. I only wanted to understand the constraints before anyone starts discussing solutions that might not be feasible."

"That's fair."

"Marsh does the math before she does the feelins," Maslin says, from his seat, not unkindly. "Its her whole thing, very responsible, very correct, kind of a buzzkill sometimes, no offense Marsh"

"None taken," Marsh says, in a tone that makes it entirely unclear whether she means it or is simply too polite to say otherwise.

---

Jinzhou has been quiet, which is normal. But it's his three-steps-ahead quiet, not his regular quiet. I've learned to tell them apart. It took nine months and a coma.

"The math doesn't work," he says, finally. "Not with five people on our current supply projection. We'd need to know what's actually salvageable from this ship before we can say whether it's survivable."

"That is what I was getting at," Marsh says, and something in her voice unclenches about a millimeter. "I didn't want to be the one who brought it up first. It felt presumptuous."

"It's not presumptuous," I say. "It's the actual question. You're allowed to ask the actual question."

She looks at me for a second like she's deciding whether to believe that.

"Noted," she says. "Thank you, Dr. Vasek."

"You can call me Ethyn."

"I'll consider it," she says, which from her, I'm starting to understand, is roughly the equivalent of Jinzhou almost smiling.

---

Petryn gets the terminal working about twenty minutes later, a soft chime that makes all of us turn at once.

"There we go," he says, quiet and pleased, like the terminal just remembered his name. "Local cache survived. Not much, but it's somethin'. Cargo manifest, mostly. What we launched with, what we've used, what's left."

"Read it out," I say.

"Gimme a sec, man. Let me actually look at it first 'fore I start promisin' anybody numbers I ain't confirmed."

Maslin unclips and drifts over Petryn's shoulder. On him, hope looks more nervous than suspicion did.

"Cmon cmon," he says, half to him, half to the screen. "Whatve we got- gimme somethin good"

"Gimme a second, Maslin. I'm workin' on it. You know rushin' don't help."

"I know, I know, I just-" He stops himself. Runs a paw through his fur. "I just want it to be somethin, you know? after all this, i want it to actually be somethin"

"I know," Petryn says, gentler, still looking at the screen and not talking to it. "I know, homie. Give me a sec. Let's find out together."

Marsh watches. Jinzhou, beside me, says nothing at all. In this room, right now, it's the correct amount of nothing.

---


"Okay," Petryn says, and something about how he says it, careful, unhurried even now, tells me before the actual numbers do that this isn't going to be the clean good news any of us were hoping for. "Here's what we got."

He reads it slow. Bad news doesn't land softer if you rush it. He knows that. I'm learning it.

"Ration stores, about sixty percent intact. Rest got compromised in the breach, atmosphere exposure, some of it just straight up vented into space with the hull section it was stored in." He looks up. "Sixty percent of what was packed for nine people, a year or two. Recalculated for three of us stretched out over however long we been out here, that shoulda lasted us a good while longer than it has."

"Should have," I repeat.

"Yeah." He says it plain, no dressing it up. "We been rationin hard. Real hard. Longer than we shoulda had to, if the math had gone like it was supposed to. But four months sealed in a cockpit does somethin to how careful you get about a can of somethin'."

Maslin is quiet for once, arms crossed, watching the numbers scroll on the screen like they might change if he stares hard enough.

"Water reclamation," Petryn continues. "Still functional, mostly. Damaged, not dead. We can pull some of it if we got the tools to do the transfer safely."

"We've got tools," Jinzhou says. "That's not the hard part."

"Medical supplies," Petryn says. "Pretty much untouched. Wasn't stored anywhere near the breach. That's somethin, at least."

"Fuel," I say. "What about your fuel."

The room goes quieter than it already was.

"Fuel's gone," Maslin says, before Petryn can get to it, sharp and fast, the words coming out before he's finished deciding how to say them. "Two tanks blew, whatever the fuck actually happened in there, and the third one got cooked on the outside, and whats left in it- it aint enough. Its nowhere close to enough. This ship aint flyin anywhere ever again, man, thats just the actual truth of it, and ive known that for months, and it dont get less true no matter how many times i say it out loud."

Whatever happened in there. I keep my face very still. I know what it's like to not know what your own fuel is doing.

Nobody says anything for a second.

"So what you're telling me," I say, finally, "is that MK2 has a lot of ship and exactly none of the part that makes it go."

"Correct," ARACCSIM confirms, quiet, from somewhere in the room, present without needing to be seen. "Vessel is not spaceworthy in its current configuration."

---

I do the math out loud, because that's what I do. Saying numbers out loud has always made them feel more manageable than letting them sit silent in my own head.

"Our supplies. Two people, mostly asleep, for a trip home of about eight years once we can actually start it, sitting at enough margin to be comfortable. Add three more people." I stop. Start the actual arithmetic. "Add three more people, and even with whatever's salvageable from your stores, we're cutting it a lot closer than comfortably."

"How close," Marsh asks. Quiet. Precise. The first thing she's said since the terminal chimed, and it lands exactly on the question that actually matters.

"I don't know yet," I say, honest. "I need real numbers, not estimates. I need to sit with ARACCSIM and run this properly before I tell anyone it's fine. If it's not fine, I'd rather know now than find out halfway through the dark."

"Smart," Marsh says, and there's relief in it. Somebody respected her caution instead of waving it off. I get the feeling that's rare for her.

Maslin is drifting now, the small side-to-side he does when holding still isn't an option anymore.

"So whats the actual plan here," he says. "cause i dont love just sittin in a dead ship waitin on a math problem to solve itself, no offense."

"None taken," I say. "The plan, as far as I can tell, is you three come with us. Onto MK1. We combine what's salvageable from here with what we've got, and we make the numbers work, however we have to make them work."

"Just like that," Maslin says.

"Just like that. What's the alternative? You three stay here on a ship with no fuel and dwindling supplies, and we fly home without you? That's not a plan. That's a different, worse version of what's already happening to you."

Maslin doesn't have a fast response to that, which I'm coming to understand doesn't happen to him often.

"Okay," he says, finally, quieter than anything he's said since we opened that bulkhead. "Yeah. Okay. That tracks."

---

"In order," Marsh says. "You promised."

So I tell it in order. It's the first time I've told the whole thing out loud, start to finish, to people who weren't there for any of it, and it turns out to be a strange experience, like reading your own diary to a jury.

"We went to Asimov to bring back an organism called Asicell," I say. "It kills Phengoclept. That's the whole mission, in one sentence. We found it. It's real. It works."

"thats good news," Maslin says, cautiously, like he's already heard the tone of the next part.

"It's great news. Then some of it got into our fuel."

The cockpit goes very quiet.

"Not on purpose," I add. "We had to take the ship down into the top of Asimov's atmosphere to collect it, and while we were down there, one of our tanks got breached. It was open for four seconds before it sealed itself. Four seconds is plenty, it turns out. Wild Asicell got in, and then they started digging toward the rest. The details are long, so I'll skip to the part that matters. The Asicell is asleep in our tanks right now. Cold keeps it asleep. So we made the tanks very cold. Colder than they were ever meant to be. Minus fifty-five degrees Celsius. That's what I meant by cold."

"How cold were they meant to be," Marsh asks.

"Minus eighteen."

She closes her eyes for a second. Just one. I'm beginning to understand that's how Marsh swears.

"And keeping them that cold takes most of our power," I go on. "Ninety-four percent of the bus. So no spin, no gravity, the centrifuge is off, and two of our three labs are sealed behind their grates until we get home. Everything else on the ship runs on what's left over."

"you put the thing that eats fuel," Maslin says slowly, "in the fuel"

"I'd like it noted that it put itself there."

"and youre plannin to fly home on it"

"Not yet. That's the problem."

"Thats the worst sentence ive ever heard," he says, with something almost like admiration. "and ive been livin in a cockpit for four months"

"There's a second worst sentence," Jinzhou says. "To leave, the engines have to restart. Warming them takes twenty-two hours. The burn home takes five months."

"And the Asicell?" Marsh says.

"Wake up about fifty-one hours after the warming starts," I say. "Plus or minus six. In the one place on the ship where all the fuel goes past."

Nobody says anything for a long moment. Petryn is the one who breaks it, slow and thoughtful, still half looking at his manifest.

"So you got about two days of a five-month job, man."

"On paper."

"And then they eat the rest."

"Yes."

"Huh," Petryn says. He doesn't sound frightened. He sounds like a man who has just been handed an engine with a strange noise in it and is already listening for where the noise lives. "That's a real interestin' problem."

"I was hoping someone would say that," I say, and I mean it more than I can explain. "Everybody else has called it a disaster."

"Oh, it's a disaster," Petryn says. "It's also interestin'. Things can be two things."

Marsh opens her eyes.

"Then the problem is not the engines," she says. "The problem is that the fuel is not ours anymore. It belongs to them, and they are letting us look at it."

"That's what I keep telling Later-Ethyn."

"Who is Later-Ethyn," Maslin asks.

"Me, in the future. I give him all my problems."

Maslin looks at Jinzhou. "is he always like this"

"Yes," Jinzhou says. "You get used to it. Or you don't, and it does not matter, because he continues."

---

Petryn is still looking at the manifest, something thoughtful behind his eyes that hasn't resolved into words yet.

"Six of you started this," he says, slow, working it out as he goes. "Two made it this far. Three of us started somethin else entire, and all three of us made it this far. Five of us total, now, tryin to get home on one workin ship." He shakes his head slightly, not in disagreement, more like he's marveling at the shape of it. "That's, like. That's a lot of loss to still end up with this many people standin in one room."

"It's a good number," I say. "Five. I'd like to keep it."

"I don't know if I believe in luck," he says again, the same thing he said in the cockpit a while back, "but I believe in that."

Marsh has moved closer to the terminal, looking at the raw numbers herself now instead of just listening to Petryn's summary, cross-checking something I can't quite see from here.

"Dr. Vasek," she says. "Ethyn." A small correction, deliberate, and I notice it, and I don't say anything about it, because some things you let people arrive at on their own time. "I'd like to help with the calculations. If that's alright. I understand ship's fuel and trajectory work reasonably well, and a second set of eyes on numbers this important seems prudent."

"That would be a huge help," I say, and I mean it. "I also owe you that long story about the freezer."

"In order," she says.

"In order," I agree.

She nods once, satisfied, like being useful settles her nerves better than any reassurance I could offer.

"Alright," I say, looking around the room at all four of them, at the dead ship around us and the living one waiting outside the hull. "Let's get everyone and everything we can move over to MK1. We've got work to do."

"Bout time somebody said that," Maslin says, and for once, there's nothing rhetorical about it at all.
