Maslin has found my book.

I don't know how. I didn't bring a copy, I would never bring a copy, and the ship's library is three hundred technical manuals and one very long document about the correct way to fold a sleeping bag. But on the morning of day sixteen as a five-person ship, he floats into Lab B holding a tablet in both paws with the expression of a man who has found a photo of his teacher at a nightclub.

"is this you," he says.

It's the cover. *PHENGOCLEPT: A STUDY GUIDE*, in the font the publisher insisted on, with my name underneath it in letters smaller than the word GUIDE.

"Where did you get that?"

"mk2 crew library," he says. "it was loaded on our tablets. it was the only book on there with pictures"

"Argon put my book on your ship?"

"argon put your book on everyones ship. petryn has it. marsh has it. marsh has *notes* on it"

I take a moment with that. Somebody at the program office, who knew about a second ship I didn't, decided that the three people on it should have my study guide for the trip. They didn't tell me about the ship. They did give it my book.

"I'm going to need a minute to decide how I feel about that."

"take your time," Maslin says. "i read chapter nine"

"Nobody reads chapter nine."

"i did. four months in a cockpit. i read everything. i read the sleeping bag one twice." He scrolls. "it says stay calm in an unfamiliar environment. it says focus on what you can control and make a list"

"I wrote that in a café."

"did it have a window"

"It had a very nice window."

He looks at me over the top of the tablet. "did it work. the list thing. for you."

I think about the medbay, the four empty cubbies, the fishing cable, the cold shutdown. Lists, every time. Numbers on a whiteboard. Things I could control, written down so I could see how small they were.

"It worked," I say. "It worked badly. But it worked."

"same," Maslin says, and that's apparently all he needed, because he floats off toward the medbay with the tablet under his arm, and I hear him say to Petryn in the hall, "its him. its really him. he says it worked badly."

"That's the best review you ever gonna get, man," Petryn says.

I'd like to argue. I find I can't.

---


Marsh doesn't trust anything that reports in adjectives.

I find this out about nine minutes after she gets a login. The ship's cruise control, LSI-ASM&C, describes everything as "nominal" or "within tolerance," and Marsh has taken it personally. Three weeks into being a five-person ship, she has pulled its raw logs for every tank wall since the handoff and is working through them in Lab B with her feet hooked under the bench rail, one column at a time, with the focus most people save for defusing things.

I drift in to ask if she wants dinner. I don't get to the question.

"Ethyn," she says, without looking up. "Come and look at this, please. And tell me I'm reading it incorrectly."

"That's a worrying way to start."

"I'm aware of that."

She turns the screen toward me. A graph, flat as a tabletop for nearly its whole width, and then, at the right edge, not flat.

"Tank 2-E," she says. "Wall temperature, since the handoff."

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Numbers. Short version, and I'm going to pretend I didn't hear the long one."

"Tank 2-E wall temperature has risen four point one degrees over the past eleven days. Current reading, minus fifty point nine degrees Celsius. Rate of rise is increasing."

Minus fifty point nine.

I know that neighborhood. I wrote it in the log myself, the night I found out where the Asicell go to sleep. At minus fifty they stop. At minus forty they're commuters at the end of a long day. At minus thirty-two, one of them twitches, and eighteen minutes later the whole smear is swimming around like nothing happened.

"We're one degree from the line," I say.

"That is my reading as well." She hesitates, which she doesn't do often. "I spent forty minutes hoping to be wrong."

"LSI-ASM&C," I say. "Freeze line status."

"All freeze lines within tolerance." The voice is flat and cheap, like a recording in a train station that knows nobody is listening.

"Define tolerance."

"Plus or minus ten degrees."

"Of minus fifty-five."

"Correct."

"So you'll let me know at minus forty-five."

"Correct."

"And what are the Asicell doing at minus forty-five?"

"Query outside function."

Marsh closes her eyes for exactly one second. "It was built for level one," she says. "At minus eighteen, ten degrees is a tight band. At minus fifty-five it is the entire margin and then some."

"Level two was my idea," I say. "The alarm band was not. I'd like that on the record."

"Noted, Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says.

"Thank you, ARACCSIM."

I do the arithmetic in my head, and it's the kind I'd hoped not to need. Three days to minus fifty, when they start to drift. Nineteen to minus thirty-two, when they swim.

"ARACCSIM. Confirm."

"Projection confirmed. Minus fifty in approximately three days. Minus thirty-two in approximately nineteen."

"Shoot," I say, quietly, to the graph.

Then I get on the ship-wide and say "cockpit, please" in the voice of a man who isn't panicking.

---

The cockpit is full by the time Marsh and I pull ourselves up the hall. Five people orbiting one screen, hooked to rails and seat backs and each other's straps. Five bodies where there used to be two is still not a sensation I've gotten used to.

Marsh puts the graph up. Nobody says anything for a second. Then Maslin does, because someone has to.

"four degrees dont sound like much," he says, already sounding like he doesn't believe his own reassurance. "right thats not- thats not a big number is it"

"It's not a big number," I say. "It's a big trend. Warming in a tank that's supposed to be frozen means something is failing. The question is what."

"A freeze line fault," Marsh says, quiet and immediate. "A loss of coolant is the most likely explanation for a progressive rise in wall temperature."

Petryn has gone very still beside her, which for him is its own kind of alarm.

"Which tank," he says, slow and careful, like he already knows and is hoping someone talks him out of it.

"2-E."

He lets out a breath through his nose. "The one that opened."

"The one that opened," I say. "The one with the crowd."

"wait the crowd" Maslin says. "the predator things. the ones that eat fuel. theyre in the fuel. again"

"They're in one tank. They're asleep. Cold keeps them asleep."

"and its getting less cold"

"Yes."

"cool" Maslin says. "cool cool cool. i left one ship with a fuel problem to get on another ship with a fuel problem. at least this one hasnt exploded"

"Yet," Marsh says. "'Yet' is doing a great deal of work in that sentence."

"They don't just wait, either," I say. "Last time, the fuel was calling them the whole way. They dug straight through the tank lining to get to it. Like it was a door."

Maslin considers this.

"thats kind of impressive," he says. "in a way i really dont wanna be impressed by right now"

"We should not admire the thing that is waiting to eat our engines," Marsh says, dry and precise, and it's such an unexpected line from her that I almost laugh despite everything.

"noted. retractin the impressed. its bad. its very bad. i hate it"

"It's worse than that," Jinzhou says. He hasn't moved in his seat. Nothing in his voice has either. "The restart plan assumes the tanks stay at minus fifty-five. It was the only number in that plan without a plus or minus."

"And now it has one."

"Yes."

"Four hours of margin, plus or minus six," I say. "I called it a mood."

"It was a mood," Jinzhou says. "This is a forecast."

Petryn is already unclipping.

"I wanna see the line," he says.

---

He gets as far as the cockpit hatch before he remembers, and stops with one paw on the frame.

Nobody on this ship can see the line. Not with their own eyes. The fuel is in three big tanks that run the length of the ship on the outside, with us in the middle of them, and there's no way into them from in here. No hatch, no crawlspace, no duct you could fit a paw down. Somebody at Argon put a wall between the crew and the fuel on purpose, and today I can't decide whether to thank them or file a complaint.

What we do have is a camera. ARACCSIM puts the hull inspection feed up on the main screen and walks it back along the tank cluster, slow, one frame at a time, five of us crowded around the cockpit like it's a fireplace.

Petryn finds it first. He doesn't say anything. He just leans past Marsh and puts a claw on the glass.

A hairline seam in the freeze line manifold, barely visible unless you already know to look, and along it, growing straight out into the dark, a fur of white frost. Coolant, weeping through the crack and freezing the second it meets nothing. It doesn't fall. There's no down. It just keeps growing, in small delicate feathers, toward the lens.

"There," Petryn says quietly. "That's where it's weepin'."

"How long has it been open?" I ask.

"Estimated fault duration, eleven days," ARACCSIM says. "Micro-fracture propagation likely began significantly earlier, potentially months prior, undetected due to gradual onset."

Months.

I sit with that the way I sat with the two thousand one hundred days number at the very start of all this. Something has been quietly failing for months, invisible and patient, and none of us noticed, because we were too busy being relieved about the last crisis to go looking for the next one.

"Why this line?" Marsh asks. "Why here?"

"Spec on these is minus eighteen," Petryn says. He taps the screen beside the seam, not on it, like it might feel it. "Somebody took it to minus fifty-five in under six hours. Metal remembers that kind of thing."

"Somebody."

"Not blamin' anybody, man."

"It was me. I picked level two. It was the right call, and I'd like to be a little upset about it anyway."

"That's allowed," Petryn says.

"Can we track them?" Jinzhou asks. "The Asicell. Where they are."

"Partial tracking available via Y'shari-frequency absorption signatures," ARACCSIM says. "Current concentration is highest in tank 2-E. Secondary concentration, low, in the aft feed line and motor housings. All dormant."

"All dormant," I repeat. "In the tank and in the engines. Both. Asleep."

"Correct."

Nobody says anything for a while. Five people crowded around one screen, watching a hairline seam grow feathers.

"We have five people and a problem," Marsh says, steady even now. "That is more than Ethyn and Jinzhou had for the fishing mission, and they solved that."

"Barely," I say.

"Barely still counts." She looks at the seam on the screen, and back at us. "We should begin by understanding exactly what we are dealing with. How fast the line is failing, whether the damage is confined to 2-E, and what it would take to hold that wall at minus fifty-five until we can repair it properly."

Petryn nods slowly, the stillness in him turning into something more active.

"I got some ideas," he says. "Nothin' solid yet. But ideas."

"i got ideas too" Maslin says. "mine mostly involve panickin but im workin on the second half"

"We'll take whatever we can get," I say, and I mean it.

Nineteen days. I've done the arithmetic four times and it keeps being nineteen. Out past the hull, in the dark, a crowd of very patient diners is being kept asleep by a pipe about as wide as my arm.

I'd like to keep it that way.

---

I can't sleep, so I don't try.

It's the middle of the night cycle when I drift back into Lab B, and Marsh is already there, feet hooked under the bench rail, a column of raw numbers scrolling slowly past her face. She doesn't look up. She doesn't look surprised either.

"You should be asleep," she says.

"So should you."

"I am checking the other tanks."

"Are they warming?"

"No." She scrolls. "Not that I can see. I would like to keep not seeing it for a while longer before I believe it."

I hook a foot beside hers and watch the numbers go by. Wall temperatures, every tank, every hour, since the day we went cold. Most of them are a flat line of minus fifty-five that goes on forever, like a ruler.

"Can I ask you something?" I say.

"You may."

"Why don't you trust the summaries? The cruise control said everything was within tolerance. Most people would have taken its word."

She's quiet long enough that I think she's going to decline. Then she stops the scroll.

"On MK2," she says, "I read the summaries. Every morning. Tank status, nominal. Fuel, nominal. Systems, nominal. It was a very reassuring way to start a day." She looks at the screen, not at me. "Then one morning the summary said nominal, and by that afternoon we had one tank left, and it was scorched. Petryn has been through our logs twice since and has not found why. I have started to suspect the reason was there the whole time, under the word nominal, and that none of us looked under the word."

"Marsh. If it was there, nobody would have caught it. Petryn hasn't, and he's the engineer."

"I know that," she says. "I have been told that. I agree with it. It does not change what I do now." She starts the scroll again. "I will not read a summary again. Not on this ship. I would rather read every number myself and be tired than be rested and wrong."

I think about my own first week on this ship, when ARACCSIM gave me the short version of everything and I was grateful for it. I think about how many short versions I've asked for since.

"For what it's worth," I say, "you were right. Tonight. The adjective was lying."

"Adjectives usually are," Marsh says. "Numbers only lie when someone has chosen the wrong ones."

I stay until she finishes the last tank. It's flat. They're all flat, except one. She closes the file, and for the first time since I came in, she looks at me.

"Thank you for staying," she says. "It was not necessary."

"I know. I wanted to."

"Then thank you for wanting to." She unhooks her feet. "Goodnight, Ethyn."

It's the second time she's used my name. I notice I'm keeping count.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE*
