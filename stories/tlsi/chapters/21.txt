
Moving a life across the gap between two docked ships turns out to be less dramatic than I expected and a lot more tedious, which I've decided is how most genuinely important things go. It takes four days. The first three are mostly tape.

We work in shifts. Two at a time through the docking collar, hauling what's salvageable, cataloguing as we go, ARACCSIM keeping a running manifest on both ends so nothing gets double-counted or lost. Nothing weighs anything, which sounds like it should make the job easy. It makes the job different. A crate that weighs nothing still has all of its mass, and it has strong opinions about stopping. I learn this on the first morning, with my snout.

The three of them keep sleeping in the cockpit until the move is done, strapped to the rails in a ship that is slowly being emptied around them.

"its like gettin evicted in slow motion," Maslin says, on day two. Nobody argues.

Medical supplies go first. They matter most and take up the least room, so that's an easy call.

"This crate goes to Lab A," I say, marking it. "Storage's tight in there, but it's the driest room we've got and half of this needs to stay cool."

"Copy," Petryn says. He sends the crate down the hall ahead of him with one easy push and follows it, unhurried, a paw trailing the rail, nudging it back on course whenever it drifts, like he's done this his whole life. For all I know he has. "You always this particular about storage, or is this a special occasion?"

"I've had weeks to develop opinions about every square inch of that ship. Yes, I'm always this particular."

"Respect," he says.

Lab A takes the crate. While he clips it down, I ask the thing I've been not asking for four days.

"Can I ask about your fuel? Not the numbers. What happened to it."

He doesn't answer right away. That's how he does it. He lets the question finish arriving first.

"Been through the engineerin' cache twice," he says. "Every tank nominal. Temps good, count good, flow good. Then one isn't. Then the next one." He tests the last strap like it might have an opinion. "That's the whole log. Nothin' came in from outside, I can tell you that much. Whatever it was, it was already in there."

"Which means what?"

"Means I don't know yet, man. And I don't like sayin' that about my own engines." He gives the strap a final tug. "I'll keep lookin'."

---

The water reclamation parts take longer, careful work, and Jinzhou and Marsh end up handling most of it together once it becomes clear they're the two people aboard who speak the same technical language without needing it translated.

"This valve's compatible with our system," Marsh says, turning it over in her paws, checking a threading pattern against a reference sheet she has apparently already memorized half of. "Same manufacturer specification. I'm mildly surprised. I'd assumed MK2 would have used a different supplier, given the build gap."

"Argon doesn't like retooling a supply chain mid-contract," Jinzhou says. "Cheaper to keep using the same parts across a production run, even four months apart."

"That's depressingly sensible."

"Most things Argon does are depressingly sensible. That's sort of their whole business model."

Marsh laughs, short, surprised at herself, like she wasn't expecting Jinzhou to have a sense of humor buried under all that flatness. Honestly, neither was I. I've been with him this whole trip, and I'm a little offended it took a stranger showing up to bring it out of him.

"Our reclamation loop was sized for two awake people, on what's left of the power bus," Jinzhou says, back to flat, already reaching for the next valve. "It's about to feed five."

"Then we should make every valve count," Marsh says.

"Yes."

They get back to it. It's the closest thing to a conversation about feelings I've ever seen either of them have.

---

Maslin insists on carrying his own personal effects himself, which turns out to be a single duffel bag, worn soft at the seams, and he holds onto it through the collar like it's the one thing in this whole transfer he's not willing to hand off to someone else.

"You good?" I ask, catching him halfway through the collar, just floating there for a second, looking back the way we came.

"Yeah, im good, just-" He stops. Starts again. "Just weird, you know? Leavin a ship behind. Even a dead one. Even one that near killed us." He pulls the bag in against his chest. "Spent four months thinkin about nothin but gettin off that thing and now Im actually doin it and it dont feel like I thought itd feel."

"How'd you think it'd feel."

"I dunno. Better, maybe? Bigger. Instead its just-" He gestures vaguely at the collar, at the two hulls on either end of it. "Its just this. Just a hallway. Kinda anticlimactic, honestly."

"Most big moments are," I say. "The ones that feel big while they're happening usually turn out to be small, and the small ones you didn't notice are the ones that end up mattering."

He looks at me for a second like that landed somewhere.

"Thats annoyingly wise for a guy who screamed at me through a door about a suit," he says, and pushes off toward MK1 before I can respond to that.

---

The plants come over last, on the afternoon of the fourth day.

Jinzhou carries them. Nobody asks him to, and nobody offers to take them from him. He holds the little table out in front of him with both paws the whole way through the collar, like a cake at a surprise party, and clips it to the wall of the central hall under the brightest lamp we have. He adjusts the angle twice.

Three people each carefully don't look at the pots. I have a theory about whose they are. I put it in the drawer for Later-Ethyn. He has a lot to look forward to.

---

By the time the last crate is strapped down, the light in the central hall has gone to the dim end of the cycle, and five very tired people are hanging off the rails in a hallway that used to comfortably fit two. Cargo sits netted along both walls in neat bundles. It looks like an avalanche that decided to be polite about it.

"Okay," Petryn says, looking around at the crates and the duffel and the general controlled chaos of a ship that's suddenly hosting more than double its expected population. "Now what. Where do we all actually sleep."

That's a real question, and I realize I haven't thought it through. Four days on cargo. Cargo doesn't need a pillow.

"Dorms," I say, working it out as I go. "Uh, six cubbies, six berths. Two in use. That leaves four open."

"Three of us," Marsh says, quiet satisfaction in it, the pleasure of watching numbers land clean. "So we have one spare."

"One spare," I agree. "Small mercies."

I don't mention why four of them are open. I know the names that go with the other four, and I decide that tonight they're just beds.

"Small mercies," Maslin repeats, and there's something almost disbelieving in it, like he stopped expecting the universe to hand him anything that fit this neatly a long time ago. "Man. Okay. Yeah. Ill take it." He glances down at the duffel. "so the duffel gets its own berth"

Nobody says no.

We pull ourselves down the hall together, five people and a lot of strapped-down cargo, toward the cubbies. This ship was built for six, and it's been running at two for a long time. Tonight it gets to be almost full again. I don't say that out loud. It's not that kind of hallway.
