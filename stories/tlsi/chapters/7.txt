*Written from the perspective of Ethyn Vasek.*

The sample from the collector is still under the microscope where I left it, so day twelve starts exactly where day eleven ended. That's how science works when you have nothing but time and no commute.

"Confirmed mass," I say, mostly to hear it out loud. "Point zero two one two seven six five nine four four six eight zero eight five one zero six three eight two nine seven eight seven two nanograms."

Jinzhou doesn't look up from his screen. "That is a lot of decimal places for something that small."

"It's not my fault! That's the actual number. I checked it four times."

"Four times."

"I wanted to be sure the fourth one agreed with the first three."

"Did it?"

"Yes. Which is somehow the most annoying part. It's a cell. I can't even see it without four hundred times magnification. And somebody, somewhere, cared enough to measure it out to twenty-eight decimal places, and I trust that number more than I trust my own name."

Jinzhou thinks about this. "You could round it."

"I could. I'm not going to. If Bejors were here she'd never let me hear the end of it."

I don't mean to say her name like that. Easy. Mid-sentence. No weight on it. It just comes out. Neither of us stops to make it a thing.

That's new. Progress, maybe. Or I'm just tired.

---

Okay. Here's what I know now that I didn't know two weeks ago, when all I had was a whiteboard and half a phone call.

The Phengoclept eats light. Not literally eats. It soaks up a star's output, the high-energy stuff, and turns it into stored mass. Then it holds onto it.

And it holds a lot. I can actually work out how much, now that I have the mass. Let's see. Point zero two one two nanograms. That's two point one two times ten to the minus fourteen kilograms. Turn all of that into energy, and you multiply by the speed of light, squared. Nine times ten to the sixteenth.

Two point one two times nine is nineteen-ish. Ten to the minus fourteen times ten to the sixteen is a hundred.

About nineteen hundred joules.

In one cell. A cell I can't see.

Nineteen hundred joules is enough to bring a cup of water most of the way to boiling. Every single one of these dots can boil a cup of tea. And there are billions of them out there in the line.

That's why the old notes call them "saturated" when they're full. I've read enough of Bejors' annotations by now to bet that word was hers. It's exactly the dry, slightly annoyed word she'd have pushed for in a meeting full of people who wanted something flashier.

Once a cell is saturated, it can't hold any more. That's when it moves.

It migrates. It rides its own stored light like a sail and crosses open space, and it can go years without eating again. It doesn't seem to plan the trip. It just points itself outward and goes. That's either the most elegant survival trick evolution ever came up with, or the dumbest. I go back and forth.

At home, it goes to Aluk'.

I know Aluk'. Like everything else that's come back to me, it's a fact sitting there fully formed, with no memory attached. Close to Nakuet. Hot. Choked in cloud. Thick with exactly the carbon compounds a saturated Phengoclept can't resist. It breeds there. Parent cell and daughter cell, and both make the trip back to Nakuet to start over.

That's the part that's killing Wask-ki. Not the eating. The breeding. Every round, the population doubles. Twice as many eating the star. Then four times. Then eight. Nothing at Aluk' stops them. So the dimming speeds up, and it'll keep speeding up until there's nothing left of Nakuet worth eating.

That's the curve I saw on day one. The one that bends down like it's tired.

At Asimov, something's different.

I don't know what yet. That's the whole reason we're here.

---

"Say the light thing again," Jinzhou says. He's got his notepad out, the paper one, drawing something in the margin. Maybe a graph. Maybe just him thinking with his paws.

"The Y'shari frequency?"

"That one."

"Twenty-two point seven zero six microns. Infrared. That's the wavelength it puts out when it's burning stored mass back into light. Which is also how it moves." I put the reading up on the shared screen so he can see the spike instead of hearing me describe it. "Every Phengoclept line anybody's ever mapped shows up at exactly that wavelength. Ours too. It's a fingerprint. If you're not looking at twenty-two point seven zero six, you're not looking for it."

"Convenient that it is one number."

"It's not convenient. It's just physics. That's the emission line." I look at him. "Why, did you want it to be complicated?"

"I want to know if I can remember it."

"Can you?"

"Twenty-two point seven zero six." He says it without looking at the notepad. "Yes."

"Show-off."

"You did it first with the mass."

"That's different. Mine has more decimal places. Mine is objectively more impressive."

He doesn't argue. From him, that's a concession. He goes back to his drawing.

---

The Phengomotors are the part that gets me every time I think about it too hard. The thing we came six point four light-years to study is also, right now, bolted to the back of our ship, dying by the gram to keep us moving.

Jinzhou explains how they work better than I can. He's the one who took a housing apart during the week-one diagnostic and put it back together without the manual. That's either really reassuring or really concerning, depending on the day.

"Three phases," he says, ticking them off. "First, low light, tuned to the frequency. Live Phengoclept come to the drive plate. They coat one face. Second, plate rotates out to open space. High-intensity pulse, same frequency. The cells do what they do when they are full and they get that signal. They dump everything. All at once. All one direction, because the plate is angled to make sure. That push moves the ship."

"And the third phase?"

"Plate rotates back in. Cells are dead by then. Nothing survives dumping that much. Scrape them off. Plate goes back out for a new coat."

I think about that.

"So the engine works," I say, "because we're killing the thing we came all this way to understand. A controlled amount of it. On a schedule."

"Yes."

"That's a little on the nose."

"It is efficient," Jinzhou says. That's how he agrees with me without admitting it.

---

We spend the middle of the day chasing something that isn't going anywhere. I only notice about two hours in, after I've already spent the two hours.

"The population math doesn't work," I say. I've rebuilt the model four different ways and gotten the same wrong answer all four times. Which is consistent, technically. "Okay. Think about a bathtub. Water coming in, water going out. If the level stays the same, in has to equal out. Right?"

"Right."

"ASI's line has been the same strength for two years. So the number of Phengoclept feeding here is staying the same. Constant. But at home, it doubles every round. So here, something's draining the tub exactly as fast as they breed. Every new cell is cancelled out by one going away."

"So something caps the population."

"Something caps the population." I lean back. "Either nothing new is showing up, or..."

"Or they do not make it back."

"Or they don't make it back." I look at the number again. It still won't move. "If they head off to breed, wherever the local Aluk' is, and something gets them before the return trip, you'd get exactly this. Steady in. Steady out. No growth."

"A predator," Jinzhou says.

"Maybe. Y'shari floated that before we ever left. I had no way to test it back then." I look at the trajectory data. At Asimov-09D, sitting down there, huge and patient. "I think I might have a way now."

Neither of us finishes that out loud. We both know what testing it means, and neither of us is in a hurry to say the word "fishing" before we have to.

---

Later, once we've both stopped pretending the population model needs more of us today, Jinzhou brings up the other thing.

"I want to look at the medical logs," he says. "The four."

I know exactly which four.

"Okay," I say.

We go down to the terminal together.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Pull the coma vitals logs. Full mission. All four."

A pause. Longer than the request should need. A pause like that used to mean "withheld pending crew status confirmation," and I brace for it out of habit.

"Retrieving," it says instead.

No partial confirm. No conditions. Whatever lock used to sit on this file, it's gone. It's been gone since the day we did the burials. I don't know if ARACCSIM was told to stand down, or if it never cared about hiding this part.

The logs are thorough. Vitals every six hours for the whole trip. Right up until they stop. The cause-of-death fields, all four, say the same thing.

UNDETERMINED. CONSISTENT WITH KNOWN RISK FACTORS.

"'Known risk factors,'" I read out loud. "That's not a cause. That's a shrug in a lab coat."

"There is a note," Jinzhou says, scrolling. "General. Not for each one."

He reads it. I read it over his shoulder.

Coma-resistant genetic markers correlate with survivability but do not guarantee it. Approximately one in several thousand carriers experience undetected cardiac or neurological complications under extended stasis, indistinguishable postmortem from unrelated natural causes absent live monitoring at time of onset.

One in several thousand.

I do the math because I can't not. Call it one in five thousand. For any one person, that's tiny. For four people to have it happen in the same week, you multiply. One in five thousand, four times over. That's one in six hundred and twenty-five trillion.

That's not bad luck. That's not "known risk factors." Things that rare don't happen four times in five days. Something else happened, and it happened to all of them at once.

"It does not add up," Jinzhou says. He's done the same math. He just didn't need to say it out loud first.

"No."

"So they lie."

"Or they don't know. And they wrote the thing that fits on a form." I stare at the screen. "I wanted there to be an answer."

"I know."

"Even a bad one. I just wanted one."

He doesn't say anything. That's an answer too. The kind that doesn't make a thing smaller than it is just to make the room feel better.

We close the file. Neither of us deletes it.

---

Ship's night comes and I'm still awake in the lab. The slide's still under the scope. Twenty-eight decimal places of mass sitting on the readout like it's daring me to argue.

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Dr. Vasek."

"How confident are you in the population model? The one that says the density in the Asimov line is constant."

"Model confidence is high, within the limits of two years of continuous observation."

"And if I wanted to test the predator idea directly. Not from orbit. Down there."

A pause. Long enough that I wonder if I've asked something it doesn't have an answer ready for.

"That would require atmospheric sample collection from Asimov-09D," it says. The sentence comes in two pieces that don't quite match. The first half is smooth and quick. The second half is late, like it had to go looking for words it doesn't keep handy. "Current mission parameters do not include a defined protocol for that maneuver."

"But it's possible."

"With modification. Yes."

I look at the gas giant on the screen. Brown and blue and enormous. Not going anywhere. Perfectly happy to let us take as long as we need to work up the nerve.

"Good," I say. "Start modifying."

*END OF CHAPTER SEVEN*
