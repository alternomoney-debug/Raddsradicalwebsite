
The middle of a trip like this one isn't part of the story. It's a schedule.

Marsh wrote it. It's taped to the wall of Lab B in handwriting that looks typeset, and it tells five people when to be awake, when to be in a berth, and when to eat what's left of the salvaged rations. Two awake, three asleep, the pairs rotating on a cycle I would explain if the explanation were interesting. It isn't. The whole point of the schedule is that nothing happens in it.

Nothing has been happening for over a year.

I'm on day eleven of my current stretch. I know because I've been marking them on the edge of the Lab B whiteboard with a pencil nobody else uses, in the bottom corner, small. Under her header, not on it. Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP is still up there in my copy of her handwriting, underlined twice the way she did it, and I have never once thought about wiping it. Eleven small pencil strokes sit below it like someone counting the days in a very polite jail.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Time to destination, one year, seven months, eleven days."

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

"I was about to look at the clock and pretend I wasn't counting."

"That is what I assumed."

It's a very small thing, and it's the first time in months I've caught it being almost kind. I decide not to ask which part of it was the almost.

Marsh says the supply margin is forty-one days. Jinzhou says forty-one days. I keep checking in case one of them is wrong in my favor, and neither of them has been wrong about anything since the cold shutdown, which I've decided is rude of them.

---

My last stretch was with Jinzhou, and it went differently.

He didn't cook. He ate whatever the medbay arms gave him, at exactly the time the schedule said, and folded the wrapper into a small neat square afterward and clipped it into a pouch with the other small neat squares. I never asked what the squares were for. I think it was the counting. I think he liked having something that got bigger at a steady rate.

What I did, on that stretch, was write a letter.

Not a log. The logs go to ARACCSIM and from ARACCSIM to whoever at Port Jiji is still reading them. This one was a file on my own tablet, addressed to Dr. Emili Y'shari, and I knew while I was writing it that I might never send it. I wrote it anyway. It was something to do with my paws.

On day six, Jinzhou floated up behind me and read it over my shoulder. He didn't ask. Jinzhou doesn't ask to read things. He reads them the way he reads code, which is to say all at once, with his eyes moving along a line only he can see.

"Line four is a lie," he said.

"Which part?"

"The part where you say you are fine."

"That's a figure of speech."

"It is a figure of speech that is a lie." He pointed, not quite touching the screen. "Line eleven is true. You say you are angry at her and you do not want to be. Keep that one."

"Thank you. Any other notes?"

"Paragraph three repeats paragraph one. You say you understand why she did it, twice."

"Maybe I'm trying to convince myself."

"Then say it once and mean it, or do not say it." He considered the screen a moment longer. "The end is good."

The end said: *We made it this far. Five of us. I'd like you to know that, whatever else.*

"You can keep the end," he said.

"I wasn't asking permission."

"I know. I am giving it anyway."

I deleted line four. I kept eleven. I took out the second time I said I understood her, and then, after a long time looking at the first one, I took that out too, because I didn't, and Jinzhou was right, and I'd rather send her nothing than send her something that wasn't true.

The letter is still on my tablet. It's shorter now. It's better. It still might never go anywhere.

Jinzhou went into his berth on day nineteen of that stretch. Before the cubby closed, he said, "When you send it, tell her I said line eleven was good."

"You've never met her."

"I have read line eleven," he said, and the berth slid shut.


---

Every night of every stretch, before I sleep, I go to the medbay and check the beds.

Not the screens. The screens are fine. ARACCSIM watches the screens all day and all night and has never once missed a number, and I trust it with them. I check the little analog dials on the bed frames, the ones Jinzhou found on our first week, the ones you'd only see if you were looking for them. Eight-digit readouts. Small needles. They don't need me. I check them anyway.

Three berths are slid out and occupied. Jinzhou on the left, very still, the way he sleeps, like someone set him down carefully and walked away. Marsh beside him, her ears flat, one paw curled up near her chin. Maslin on the right, who even asleep manages to look like he's in the middle of saying something.

One more berth is out, and it has the duffel in it. Strapped down neatly with the berth's own straps, a year and a half after he asked if it could have its own bed and nobody said no. Nobody's moved it since. I'm not going to be the first.

The last two are pushed into their cubbies, mine and Petryn's, waiting for us.

I go along the row. Heart rate, breathing, the slow drift of the temperature needle. All of it where it should be, all of it boring, which is the best thing a number can be in this room.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says, very quietly. It has learned to say things quietly in here. I don't know when. "Their vital signs are monitored continuously."

"I know."

"You check them each night."

"I'm monitoring them discontinuously. It's a different service."

A pause, with the flourish in it. "Understood."

It isn't that I don't trust the ship. It's that four people went to sleep in these same beds, on this same ship, while the same screens watched them, and the screens said they were fine right up until they weren't. I know the numbers can't save anyone. I know me looking at a needle doesn't change where the needle points. I just can't be the kind of person who doesn't look.

I check Jinzhou's dial twice. I don't tell anyone that part.

Then I go to bed.

---

Petryn and I have the same stretch this time. He cooks.

I want to be clear about what that means. The medbay arms heat a tube, or a pouch, or whatever the Argon packaging department decided to call a meal that week. What Petryn does is take the least promising item in the salvaged MK2 stores, hold it up to the light like a man judging a diamond, and say, "Oh, you're gonna like this one."

"I'm not," I say.

"Green one, man. Try it."

"It's called Garden Medley. There's no garden in it. I've read the label."

"Try the green one."

I try the green one.

It's good. I'd like that on the record somewhere other than my own head. It's warm and a little bit sharp and there's an undertone of something like lemon that has no business surviving in a tube. I chew with an expression I keep entirely neutral, which is a skill I developed in a school cafeteria and have never lost.

Petryn watches me over the top of his own pouch.

"It's not bad," I say.

"Mm-hm."

"It's adequate."

"Your ears came up, man."

I put a paw on one. They had. "That's circulation."

"Sure it is," Petryn says, and goes back to eating with the expression of a man who is not going to say it, because he doesn't have to.

I'm opening my mouth to defend my ears when it comes back, and it comes back whole, like the rest of my facts. A bar. A table with a wobble. A chipped bowl set down in front of me, and across from it a stocky canine with a book face-down by his elbow and no expression at all.

"You'll like it," Nelson says. "You won't say so."

"I'm not going to like it."

He waits. He's very good at waiting. He once sat through an entire six-minute argument about a movie just so he could say one word at the end of it.

I eat. I look at the soup for a second longer than the soup deserves.

"I liked it."

"I know," Nelson says, and goes back to his book.

That's the whole memory. It's not even a good one. Soup, a bar, a man who never gloated because gloating takes a kind of effort he'd have charged me extra for. I wait for the rest of it to arrive and the rest of it doesn't. But the feeling underneath it does, all at once, the way a leg falls asleep in reverse, and I put my pouch down, very carefully, in the air in front of me, and hold on to the rail.

"Ethyn?" Petryn says.

"Give me a second."

"Sure thing."

The tally paper has been in my jacket pocket since the day we went through the lockers, and I take it out now, a small soft square of paper that has been folded and unfolded more than I'd like to admit. Two columns. His initials and mine at the top. A total, circled twice at the bottom, in his handwriting, which is precise and small and slightly annoyed.

Sixty marks.

I've spent a long time assuming I owed him sixty marks. Money. A debt I'd forgotten the reason for, which struck me as the kind of thing I'd do. I said it to him out loud, at the airlock, with his body between us, that I had no idea what I owed him sixty marks for, and I was sorry.

It was never a debt.

I look at the two columns again, and I count the little vertical strokes under my initials, and there are sixty of them. One for every time he said *you'll like it* and I said *I won't*, and every time I did. Sixty recommendations. A place to eat, a book, a song with no words, a repair shop that fixed things nobody else would touch. Sixty times I was wrong, and sixty times he marked it down and never mentioned it to anyone, including me.

He wasn't keeping score of what I owed him. He was keeping score of how often he was right, in the quietest way anyone's ever been right, and then letting me think it was money.

"I liked it," I say, to the paper, in a small voice that sounds ridiculous in an empty medbay. "All sixty."

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "I did not register a query."

"It wasn't one."

"Understood."

A pause. Then, with the flourish nobody has ever explained: "I will not log it."

"Thank you."

I fold the paper back up and put it in my pocket. Petryn, to his credit, has found something extremely interesting to look at on the far wall.

"The green one was good," I tell him, after a minute. "I'm saying that out loud for the record."

"I know," says Petryn.

It's a coincidence that he says it the same way. I'm almost sure of that.

---

The shift change comes four days later, with Maslin sailing into Lab B as though he's been looking forward to this for some time.

"ok so," he says, before the door has finished closing. "i did the logistics on the restart."

"Please don't," Petryn says.

"and the logistics say that i will probably explode, but in a really organized way. like i have a spreadsheet for it. there are tabs."

"Maslin."

"its color coded"

"That is not funny, man."

"its a little funny," Maslin says, and looks at me for backup.

I think about it with the seriousness it deserves. "It's a little funny," I say. "I'd like to see the tabs."

"the third tab is just you," he says. "its called ethyns face when someone says the number."

"Which number?"

"any of them"

Petryn makes a sound that's half a laugh and half a prayer, and Maslin looks enormously satisfied, and for about a minute the lab is the way it was in the first days, four people who don't entirely know each other, being gentle in the only way they know. Then it passes, the way it does, and Maslin floats up to the whiteboard and reads my little row of pencil strokes. His mouth goes quiet.

"eleven," he says, not really to anyone. "thats a lot of strokes."

"It's the part of the trip where that counts as a lot."

"oh." He considers the board. He considers me. "do you want me to do one for the next ones? i got good handwriting."

"You really don't."

"i got legible handwriting"

"That one I'll accept."

He takes the pencil and draws one small stroke, very straight, at the end of mine. Then he stays there a second, one paw still on the board. Under the header I copied from Bejors, under the shape of her theory, under the whole careful structure of a woman who could see this far and no farther.

"we're gonna make it," he says. It's not a prediction. It sounds more like something he's trying on to see if it fits.

"We have a margin of forty-one days."

"thats not what i said"

"I know. I'm doing the math on what you said."

"and?"

"It's a lot of math." I look at the pencil. "I'm leaning toward yes."

He smiles at that, a little crooked, and floats off to check the stores with the air of a man who's been given an assignment. I stay with the board for a while. A year and seven months. A very small stroke at the end of my row, in handwriting that isn't mine.

I take a second pencil and write, in the lower corner, small, in a place where her header won't have to share:

*47 / 51 ± 6*

It's not an answer. It's a question, written down so I can see how big it is. Later-Ethyn can have the rest.
