The floor lasts nine days.

I'd love to say we use them well. Mostly we use them to walk. Maslin walks laps of the hallway at all hours, for no reason, because he can. Marsh walks exactly as much as she needs to and not one step more, and every time she puts weight on a foot she looks a little surprised that it works. Petryn finds the folding table again and we eat at it every night. Jinzhou stands in the cockpit for an hour on the first morning. Just stands. When I ask what he's doing, he says, "Standing." I decide that's a complete answer.

And on day nine, we have to give the floor back.

"The flush needs thrust," Jinzhou says, in Lab B, in front of my copy of Bejors' board. Nobody writes on the real one. "Thrust needs the ship in one piece. The centrifuge comes down."

"I know," I say. "I'm just saying, out loud, that I'm going to miss the floor."

"Noted," says ARACCSIM.

---

The plan fits on half a whiteboard. I've learned not to trust a plan that fits on half a whiteboard.

"The stack is cold," Jinzhou says. "To run it, we warm it. Twenty-two hours. Then we run the motors at full pulse, as hard as they are rated, and we keep running them. Clean fuel from tank one and tank three goes down the line, through the housings, out the back. Anything in the line goes with it."

Okay. Why would that work?

Because the motors are hot. Really hot. The fuel in a running motor sits at the boiling point of water. Nothing small and alive gets through that. The motor doesn't care what goes through it. It just cooks it. So if the Asicell are in the line, and the line goes through the motors, then the motors are a very big, very rude oven.

"So we flush them through the oven," I say.

"Yes."

"How long?" Marsh asks.

"Ballpark, ten hours at full pulse to push the whole line through twice," Jinzhou says. "Flush complete at hour thirty-two."

Ballpark. That's a Y'shari word. She said it in every meeting, usually right before a number nobody liked. He worked under her longer than I did. Some of her stuck.

"And the Asicell in the line wake at?"

"Fifty-one hours after the thaw begins. Plus or minus six."

"Thirty-two against fifty-one," Petryn says. "That's a nice gap, man."

"It's a lovely gap," I say. "I don't trust it."

"Why not?"

"Because every gap I've trusted on this trip has had an opinion."

Jinzhou draws the ship on the board. Very flat. A long rectangle, motors at one end. His line is as clean as everything else about him.

"Then we make it bigger," he says. "The thaw does not have to be even. If we warm the front manifolds first and bring the back housings up last, the clock for the Asicell does not start at hour zero. It starts at hour five or six."

Oh, that's clever. The Asicell wake up based on how warm they get. So don't warm the part where they live until the last possible minute.

"Fifty-six, plus or minus six," Marsh says. "At the bad end, fifty."

"Against thirty-two."

"The spin motors," Petryn says. He's already leaning in. "Man, that's the part I don't like. Those first six hours, the stack runs rough even on a nice even thaw. Thaw it uneven, and the stack's gonna be lopsided. One end warm, one end cold. That's gonna shake."

"Can you tune it?"

"I can tune it. I can't promise to tune it fast."

"Front first," I say. I don't wait for anybody to ask me this time. "Back housings last. Petryn tunes the stack. If it starts walking, we stop and rethink, and I'll own that call too."

I write it on the board under Jinzhou's rectangle, in letters I'd have been too shy to make this big a few months ago:

*32 / 56 ± 6*

---

The spin comes down with two keys, the way it went up. The weight leaves the way it came. A lean. Less of a lean. None. The cables reel in, slow and careful, and the crew section climbs back down to where it started until the latches close with a thunk I feel in my teeth. The grates drop. The ship goes back to being tall. The five of us go back to being things that float.

Five stations. We take them without anybody having to assign them.

Marsh flies. On a ship that hasn't gone anywhere in months, that means a lot of watching an attitude indicator. Petryn takes the medbay, the room closest to the engines, with his back flat against the aft wall, because he's not going to trust a vibration he can't feel with his own spine. Maslin takes the fuel readout. It has one job and no room for him to be funny at it. Jinzhou's at the software bench in the cockpit, with a screen of code that looks like a very long apology. I'm in Lab B, in front of the thaw map and the Asicell tracking, because I'm the only one aboard who knows what a sleeping Asicell looks like right before it stops sleeping.

"Thaw sequence initiated," LSI-ASM&C says, in the voice of a man reading a parking ticket. "Reason code, crew authority."

The front manifolds start to warm.

For an hour, nothing happens. That's the best news I get all day. The numbers climb the way numbers are supposed to. Minus fifty-five. Minus fifty-two. I stand at the board and read them out loud, because they're mine. Maslin says over the comm, "you dont have to read the numbers to us," and I say, "I know. I like how they sound."

At hour three, I explain it, mostly to have something to say.

"Phengomotors work in three steps. Attract, pulse, scrape." I'm running Kalen's demo in my head. The one that started confusing and ended with a very good safe version. "The cells go where the motor tells them. That's the pull. Then the pulse. Then the spent cells get scraped off and thrown out the back."

"and if the scrape doesnt work?" Maslin asks.

Good question. What happens to an Asicell that gets thrown out the back alive?

"Then it's still awake," I say. "And it goes looking for the nearest thing that smells like home. Carbon dioxide. Out here, the only planet close enough for it to find is the one whose sky eats it. So if we're going to get this wrong, this is the safest place in the universe to get it wrong."

"oh," Maslin says. "thats a great reason"

"It's the best one I've got."

At hour five, the stack starts to tick.

A faint sound through the bulkhead. A rhythm. Not quite steady. Like a clock that's been told it's late. *Tick.* Pause. *Tick.* Then two together. Then one. Then something in between. The spin motors, running rough on a cold start, exactly like I was warned. I put that warning in the drawer in the server room. Later has now come to collect it.

"That's the rough part," Petryn says in my ear. Warm. A little out of breath. "I'm on it, man. It's just the housing talkin'."

"It sounds like a clock."

"Sounds like a bad clock."

"It sounds like a clock in a hurry."

*Tick. Tick-tick.* The ship is counting. Counting toward what? I don't know. Also, I know exactly what. Knowing doesn't help the not-knowing even a little.

At hour five and forty minutes, ARACCSIM says, "Aft housing warming initiated."

"Clock for the Asicell, now," Jinzhou says, flat as ever. "Hour five and forty. Revival estimate, fifty-six, plus or minus six. Flush complete at thirty-two."

I write it on the board anyway. I need the handwriting. Then I turn to the tracking panel, look at a row of sleeping dots in the motor housings, and do the only job I have. I look at them very hard, in case that helps.

It doesn't. But it gives my eyes something to do.

---

Hours six through twenty are the longest and shortest of my life.

The ticking settles. It eases into a hum. The hum gets smoother. Petryn stops sounding like a man bailing out a boat. Marsh reads the stack temperature every ten minutes. Maslin refills a tube of something and leaves it next to me without a word. I eat it without looking at the label. It's not the green one. I'm a little sad about that.

At hour twenty, Jinzhou floats into Lab B, holds the rail next to me, and says nothing for a while. Then:

"The arm is fine."

"I didn't ask."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking about the stack."

"You were thinking about the arm, and then the stack. In that order." He looks at the board. "I am telling you before you ask, so you do not have to."

I take that, fold it up, and put it somewhere I'll be able to find it later.

"Thank you," I say.

"It was not a favor."

"I'm going to treat it like one."

He doesn't say anything to that. That's how I know I'm right.

At hour twenty-two, ARACCSIM says, "Phengomotor stack at operating temperature. All housings within tolerance. Flush burn may begin at crew authority."

Marsh's voice. Formal. Very slightly shaky. "Dr. Vasek."

I look at the board. Bejors' header, in my handwriting. Jinzhou's flat little rectangle of a ship. My *32 / 56 ± 6*, bigger than I'd have dared a month ago.

I said yes once already, in this room. Turns out I can keep saying it.

"Light them," I say.

---

The burn starts so quietly I'm not sure it has.

I'm in Lab B with my paws flat on the bench and my eyes on a screen, and nothing moves. No shove. No roar. For the first minute the ship is still weightless. The only proof that something huge just started is a number on ARACCSIM's readout that isn't zero anymore.

"Phengomotor burn in progress," ARACCSIM says. "Acceleration, point zero zero four of a G."

"That's it?"

"That is the first minute, Dr. Vasek. It accumulates."

"Thank you. I'd have panicked if you said it was exciting."

"I would not say that."

"I know. That's why I like you. For about ninety seconds a day."

It accumulates. Over the next half hour, down comes back. And it doesn't come back where I left it. It comes from the engine end, like the day I first woke up. The whole room turns ninety degrees around me without anything actually moving. The wall behind the bench becomes the floor. The grate slides shut under my boots and I'm standing on it, in a shaft, bench at my hip, whiteboard running sideways again, the way this ship was built to be lived in under thrust. The cold stage and the instrument racks swing around on their gimbals to meet the new down, slow and level, like they've been waiting for it.

Somewhere below me, a tube I forgot to clip down makes the whole trip to the medbay. Petryn reports it over the comm like a weather event. (Note to self: clip things down. Note to self, again: you wrote that note last time.)

Somewhere at the back of the ship, a thousand and nine motors are doing what Kalen showed us, as hard as they're allowed to. Attract. Pulse. Scrape. And with them, if this works, everything that came down the line goes through a motor hot enough to boil water. Nothing small and alive would ever want to go there. I can't hear it. Petryn can feel it through the medbay floor, which an hour ago was the wall he had his back against. He says it feels like leaning on a really big refrigerator that decided to purr.

"How's the stack?" I ask him.

"Steady, man. Real steady. She's lopsided in a way I can live with."

"Can you live with it for ten hours?"

"Ask me at hour nine."

---

Hour twenty-three. Hour twenty-five. I stop saying the times out loud. Maslin asked me to. And the sound of them is starting to do things to the room.

I eat when somebody hands me a tube. Marsh reads the stack temperature every ten minutes, in a voice she's clearly decided is neutral and isn't. Maslin watches the fuel readout and says, "forty percent of the way to a boring day," and then, quietly, to nobody, "please be boring."

The tracking panel shows a row of dots in the back housings. Green and still since the thaw started. Asleep. Waiting, in the dark, the way they've waited for months.

At hour twenty-six and ten minutes, one of the dots turns amber.

Wait.

What?

I don't say anything at first. I think the display's broken. That's how you know I've been doing this too long. I look at it. The amber dot looks back.

"ARACCSIM."

"Asicell motion detected in aft housing four." The hitch in its voice is bad this time. A skip, like somebody bumped the record. "Activity is above the model estimate. Revival has begun."

Everything goes very quiet.

"Hour twenty-six," Jinzhou says in my ear, flat as a table. "The estimate was fifty-six. The bad end was fifty."

"I know."

"It is a day early."

"I know, Jinzhou."

"The flush has nearly six hours left," Marsh says. She sounds very far away and very precise. "Dr. Vasek. What are they doing?"

I'm already looking. I have to look, because I don't know, and I need to know.

Why now? Why a whole day early? Think. What's different from the model?

The model assumed our fuel. Our fuel was frozen solid for months. So...

Oh.

Oh, fudge.

MK2's fuel.

It went into our tanks warmer than ours ever was. And it's been warming the line from the inside since the second we lit the motors. The housings are hotter than anything on a cold-start chart, because nobody ever wrote a chart for this. The model was right about our fuel. We're not running on our fuel.

And the dot in housing four is moving. A little. Then more. Not swimming. Drifting. The way they drift at minus forty. And it's going the wrong way. Not down the line with the flow, into the motors. Up it. Against the current. Toward the tanks. Toward the biggest meal on the ship.

"ARACCSIM. When do they reach the tank one isolation valve?"

"At present rate, approximately two hours and thirty minutes."

"And the flush?"

"Five hours and fifty minutes."

If they reach the valve, they reach the lining. If they reach the lining, they dig. And on the other side of that lining is a full tank of clean fuel that Petryn walked across on a hose.

"That's a gap of more than three hours."

"Three hours and twenty minutes," ARACCSIM corrects, because it can't help itself.

I put both paws over my face for exactly one second. That's the only luxury I allow. Then I take them off and look at the whiteboard, at the numbers in the corner, and a thought shows up. Whole. Clean. The way the good ones do.

*Nine percent.*

"Ethyn," Jinzhou says. "I have the same thought. Do you want me to say it first?"

"Say it."

"The reserve is nine percent over the profile." No hesitation. No color. "Marsh did the arithmetic and Petryn carried it across on a hose. We can spend it."

"How fast can we burn?"

"The stack is rated for full pulse, and we are running full pulse. Past that, it is not rated. It will do it anyway." A pause. "It will also waste cells. The faster the pulse, the more cells leave before they finish. Efficiency falls."

"By how much?"

"I would estimate six percent of the reserve."

"Six."

"At the pulse rate we need to finish before the valve, yes. Six."

Okay. Arithmetic. It takes me less than a second and feels like most of a day. Nine minus six is three. Three percent over what it takes to get home and stop. Not zero. Three. With nothing left over for anything we haven't thought of yet.

Good news: we brought extra fuel.

Bad news: we're about to set two-thirds of it on fire on purpose.

"Petryn. Can the stack take it?"

"Man." His voice comes over the comm from the medbay, and I can hear his teeth in it. "I just got her sounding nice."

"I know."

"She's gonna walk."

"Can you hold it?"

"I can hold it past redline for two hours if I don't blink, and if Jinzhou keeps the software from fightin' me."

"The software will not fight you," Jinzhou says.

"Say it again."

"The software will not fight you."

"That's the sweetest thing I ever heard from a man with no inflection," Petryn says. "All right. Do it."

I look at Marsh on the cockpit camera. Very still. Both paws on the controls. She has the face of somebody who's waited a long time for somebody else to make a decision, and just realized she wouldn't want it any other way.

"Dr. Vasek," she says. "Your authority."

"Spend six," I say.

It's the shortest sentence I've said in a month. It drops into the room like a stone into still water, and the ripples go all the way out.

"Logged," ARACCSIM says. "Pulse rate increasing past rated maximum. Efficiency declining. Estimated flush completion, two hours and ten minutes."

"That's the same time as the Asicell," Marsh says.

"Not the same," I say. "It's twenty minutes before. We've got a twenty-minute margin."

"A twenty-minute margin is not a margin."

"It's a gap with a good attitude."

---

The ship starts to shake.

Not violently. A deep sideways rattle that comes up through the bulkheads and into my teeth. The tick from the first six hours is back, louder, and it's not one clock anymore. It's a hundred clocks. Dust shakes loose from every surface in Lab B and sifts down through the grate in the lights. Thousands of little specks, falling toward the engines like they're late for something.

"Aft housing four," ARACCSIM says. "Asicell concentration rising. Housing three, motion detected."

"theyre coming," Maslin says. Small. Flat. The least like himself I've ever heard him.

"They're not coming," I say. "They're drifting toward something they want. That's different."

"is it different"

"It's different in the part where we get to be a little smug about it."

I'm not sure anybody laughs. I'm sure somebody breathes out.

The clock on the wall says twenty-eight hours and nine minutes. I'm standing in front of a row of dots that aren't green anymore, and I'm talking to them. I'm not proud of this.

"Please," I say. "Please be slow. I wrote a book about your cousins. I said really nice things."

"Dr. Vasek. Asicell from housing four have passed the feed line junction. Tank one isolation valve contact in nineteen minutes."

"Flush completion in?"

"Eleven minutes."

Eleven against nineteen. I know how to do that one. I wish I didn't.

The ship shudders. A tube walks itself off a shelf, drops, bounces once on the grate, and rolls into a corner, and nobody reaches for it. Jinzhou says, "Pulse rate holding." Petryn says, from a long way off, "She's walkin', but she's still walkin' *straight*, man, she's still straight, keep it comin'." Marsh says a sentence I've never heard her say, with a word in it she's clearly been saving up for years. Maslin is saying something quietly, over and over. When I listen, I realize he's counting backward from a hundred.

"Revised," ARACCSIM says. "Housing temperatures are rising with pulse rate. The Asicell advance has accelerated. Valve contact in nine minutes."

Wait. Of course. Hotter motors, hotter line, faster Asicell. The harder we push, the faster they swim.

"Flush?"

"Eight."

That's not a margin. That's a coin balanced on its edge, and the coin is us.

"Jinzhou."

"I have it."

"Can we shave a minute?"

"I can push the pulse one more step. It will cost a little more of the six."

"How much?"

"Under half a percent."

"Do it."

"Authority."

"Yours," I say. "Mine. Whoever's. Do it!"

The shaking changes pitch. The hundred clocks lean forward. Somewhere below the floor a very large machine is doing a very large thing very fast, and I notice both my paws are on the edge of the bench, and my ears are all the way down, and I can't tell you when that started.

"Flush, five minutes," ARACCSIM says. "Valve contact, six."

Nobody says anything. I don't breathe. That's not a figure of speech. Around minute two I notice I've stopped. I let the air out and take another one. The only number that matters is five against six.

"Four minutes. Contact, four."

"They are past the junction," Marsh says.

"Three. Contact, three."

The dots go from amber to a color I don't have a name for.

And then the flow catches them.

All that new fuel, shoving down the line harder than the line was ever built for, and the dots stop moving upstream and start moving down. Toward the motors. Faster than they can swim. One by one they reach the stack. One by one, they go dark. Like somebody's switching them off.

"Two."

"They are going backward," Jinzhou says. "Into the motors."

"One. Contact, one."

"Pulse nominal."

"Thirty seconds."

I learn something about myself in those thirty seconds. I'm not afraid of dying. It's a totally different thing. I've spent so long rehearsing every way this goes wrong that I'm not ready for the one where it works.

"Ten," ARACCSIM says. "Nine. Eight."

Maslin's counting finishes. "...three. two. one," he says, half a second off.

"Flush complete," ARACCSIM says. "Pulse returning to departure rate."

And the clocks stop.

---

The silence is enormous.

It's not like the cold shutdown, when the ship went quiet because something got taken away. This one has an edge on it. Like a held breath finally let go. The shaking leaves the room like somebody unplugged it. What's left underneath is a hum. Low. Steady. And a gentle weight that isn't going anywhere. The ship is still burning. It's just burning the way a ship is supposed to. Toward somewhere, instead of at something.

Dust settles on the grate. The tube in the corner stays in the corner. I put my paw down on the bench. It's shaking. I leave it there.

"Stack status," Marsh says. Almost in her own voice.

"Flush cycle ended," says LSI-ASM&C, flat as a stone. "Reason code, complete. Departure profile active."

"Spent cells?"

"Scrape phase complete," Petryn says. His voice is cracked and warm, and he's laughing, I think. Or something close. "All of 'em. Every last one went out the back lookin' at nothin'. Nothin' at all, man."

"Asicell?"

I look at the board. There's nothing on it. Not a dot. Not a smudge. The feed line is a clean green stripe from the tanks to the stack, and the housings are empty.

"ARACCSIM. Asicell. Anywhere aboard."

"None detected."

"Say it again."

"None detected."

None!

Zero!

I want to hug the computer. I can't. The computer is the walls.

They went through the motors. Every single one, swept down a line they'd been trying to climb, into a place that runs at the boiling point of water and doesn't ask anybody's name. And I feel something I wasn't expecting. Sorry. They didn't do anything wrong. They did exactly what they were built for, in the wrong ship. I've got a box of their cousins on a courier heading home, doing the same thing in the right one.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Remaining Phengoclept reserve stands at two point five percent above the departure and arrival profile."

Two point five. Not three. We spent a little more than six. Fine. Two point five is still a number with no minus sign in front of it.

"Thank you."

"Departure profile engaged. Estimated arrival at Wask-ki, seven years, nine months."

"Is it a good trajectory?"

"It is the trajectory we planned."

"That isn't what I asked."

A pause. A flourish.

"Yes," ARACCSIM says. "It is a good one."

I think I'll remember that one for a long time.

---

Nobody moves for a while. Then we do, one at a time, climbing the shaft toward Lab B like people who just heard the party's still going.

Petryn climbs up from the medbay with sweat in his fur and a grin that takes up most of his face. Marsh comes out of the cockpit and doesn't straighten her jacket, which is the loudest thing she's ever done. Maslin shows up the way he shows up to everything, too fast. Then he sits down on the grate with both arms around his knees and doesn't say anything at all.

Jinzhou comes last. He holds the rail next to me a little too long. Then he lets go with one paw and puts it flat on the whiteboard, near the corner with my numbers.

"Five," he says.

"Five what?"

"Five of us." He's not looking at me. He's looking at the board. "I counted. I get five every time. It is a very consistent number."

"It's a good number."

"It is not a large number."

"It's the one we have."

He nods. Once. I am absolutely not going to say anything about his ears.

I pick up the pencil. Under *32 / 56 ± 6*, I write one more line. Small. In the corner, where her header doesn't have to share.

*Done at 28:20. Going home.*

I look at it for a long time.

Then I put the pencil down and tell the ship, very sincerely, "Thank you."

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says.

Later-Ethyn can have the drawer. I'm keeping this one.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE*
