
"Contaminant detected."

That's the first thing I hear. Not good morning. Not welcome back. Two words and a period, in a voice with all the urgency of a bus schedule.

"Contaminant detected."

Again. I'd like it noted that I'm still lying down.

The ceiling is the wrong shade of white and the light has a hum I don't remember agreeing to. My berth is sliding out of its cubby at the speed of a drawer being opened by someone who doesn't want to wake the desk. The arms come down, take my pulse, tilt my chin, shine a light into each eye, and conclude, as far as I can tell, that I am technically a person. They retract.

"I heard you," I say. It comes out as gravel. "Twice. Give me a second to, uh. Be a person."

The alarm repeats. It doesn't have a second to give.

So I do what I always do first. I look across the room.

Jinzhou's cubby is sealed. His berth is in. The readout on his frame is slow and green and so steady it looks like a hobby.

"ARACCSIM. Why am I awake, and why is he not?"

A pause, and a hitch on the number when it comes, like a record skipping once.

"Mr. Kenzo's treatment is sixty-three percent complete. Waking him now carries a risk of permanent tissue loss in the left arm. Dr. Vasek's recovery is within parameters for light duty."

"Light duty."

"Correct."

"Name one light duty that starts with 'contaminant detected.'"

"Contaminant detected," ARACCSIM says.

"No, that was. That was a joke. You don't have to answer jokes."

"Understood."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

"Short version of how we got here," I say. "Please."

"Dr. Vasek was struck by a detached external locker during extravehicular retrieval. Mr. Kenzo performed an extravehicular recovery. Thermal exposure to Mr. Kenzo's left arm exceeded suit rating by six minutes. Both crew were placed in rest state."

I go back over that. Slower the second time.

"He went out there."

"Correct."

"On the hull."

"Correct."

"He's not trained for the hull."

"Correct."

"Huh," I say.

The last thing I remember is a very good word and a locker I didn't see. Apparently the locker saw me. I look at the cubby for a while. Then at the ceiling, which isn't helping.

---

"ARACCSIM. What's contaminated?"

"Biological contaminant. Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's the whole ship."

"Correct."

"Can you narrow it down?"

"Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's not narrowing. That's repeating, with confidence."

"Understood."

I swing my legs off the berth and get my feet under me. My right side, where the locker got me, files a formal complaint. Then a second one, in case the first was lost.

All right. List. I do better with a list.

One. Something alive is on this ship that the ship didn't expect.

Two. The ship can't say where, because the ship's idea of where is here.

Three. There are two people aboard. One of them is me, awake, and the other is a man in a sealed cubby who has been horizontal for two days. If it's one of us, I'd like to be told gently.

"Air," I say. "Full panel on the cabin atmosphere."

"Cabin atmosphere nominal. No contaminant detected."

"Water."

"Nominal."

"Food stores."

"Nominal."

So the air is fine, the water is fine, the food is fine, and the ship is contaminated. That's a very specific way to be contaminated.

A braver man, or a dumber one, would dump the atmosphere out the airlock right now. I considered it for about four seconds. Then I asked the air a question first, and the air said it was fine, and I believed the air. Venting the ship is what you do when you've run out of plans and want to feel like you picked one.

"Anything else wrong?" I say. "Anything. Doesn't matter how small."

"Separate notice. Phengoclept reserve is below projected level."

"Separate from what?"

"From the contaminant alert."

"Why are they separate notices?"

"They were generated by different systems."

"Of course they were. Below by how much?"

"Zero point three percent over the last twelve hours. Rate of loss, increasing."

Zero point three percent doesn't sound like anything. That's the first thing I think. The second thing I think is increasing, and my stomach gets there before I do.

A leaking tank announces itself. Pressure falls, a line frosts over, some alarm with a better personality than this one starts shouting.

"Tank pressures."

"Nominal."

"So it isn't leaving."

"Correct."

Mass down, pressure steady. The fuel isn't getting out of the tanks. It's being used up inside them.

Used by what? Nothing on this ship uses Phengoclept except the engines, and the engines are off. Which leaves exactly one other thing aboard that treats Phengoclept as lunch.

I know what it is. I think I've known since "being used," if I'm honest. I hold the thought at arm's length, like a lab report with my name on it.

It's too obvious, and the obvious answer has a problem. The obvious answer lives in sealed containers. I sealed them myself. I checked every seal twice, because we do everything twice, because that was the rule.

"ARACCSIM. Integrity on every Asicell container aboard. Sample chambers, culture vessels, the backup unit. All of it."

"All containers sealed. Integrity nominal. No breach detected."

"Amazing," I say.

Because here is what I've got. A fuel supply being eaten by the only organism that eats it. A full set of containers swearing that organism is locked in. One of those is wrong, and I would very much like it to be the sensors, because sensors are a thing you can replace.

---

"ARACCSIM. Draw three fuel samples to the Lab A bench. Tank one, the main feed line, and the motor six intake."

I pick motor six because it misbehaved on the way down, and a hunch is just a hypothesis without the paperwork.

"Fuel sample taps require propulsion authority. Mr. Kenzo is propulsion authority."

"He's busy."

"Section four point two permits the secondary pilot to substitute, with two confirmations."

"Confirmed."

"One confirmation recorded."

"Confirmed again."

"Two confirmations recorded. Taps opening."

I'm the secondary pilot and the second confirmation. It's nice to be needed twice.

Lab A is eleven meters away, and my body treats it like a hike. The centrifuge is running, so the floor is a floor, and I'm grateful for that in a small, private way I'd never been grateful for a floor before this month. By the time I get to the bench, the three vials are waiting in the rack with their little tap labels, looking innocent. Fuel always does.

The microscope is the one Jinzhou fixed. I trust it more than I trust most people.

Slide one, tank one.

The Phengoclept cells are there, small bright emitters at twenty-two point seven zero six, fewer than a fuel sample ought to show. And among them, drifting, patient, unmistakable: blue at the membrane, red toward the center, green running through both.

I know them like my own handwriting. I drew them for the write-up. I was going to put them in Figure 3.

I count seven. Seven is a rounding error until you remember what they do next, which is eight.

Slide two, the main feed line.

Forty-one. I count twice. It's still forty-one.

Slide three, motor six.

I stop counting at three hundred and tell the microscope it can keep the rest. The green in them is bright, the green of something that has been eating very well. One of them is wrapped around a Phengoclept cell mid-meal, and I watch it for a while, which is the wrong thing to do and the only thing I can do.

Motor six threw a cell wide on the way down. One every ninety minutes, the simulation said. Not zero. The simulation did not have a line for something in the motor eating the cell mid-pulse. Whether that was the Asicell or just the one-in-ninety is a question for later-Ethyn, who will have more data and fewer feelings.

I say some things to the microscope. They do not go in the record.

It doesn't answer, which is fair. It's an instrument.

---

I sit down on a stool because my knees have an opinion.

So. The containers are sealed. The containers are nominal. The cultures inside them are alive and well, and also the cultures are in my fuel. Both of those are true, and the only way both are true is if the containers are sealed in the sense that matters to ARACCSIM and not in the sense that matters to anything else.

ARACCSIM checks whether the lid is on. It does not check whether the walls are.

Every culture vessel on this bench has a coupon in a side port, a thumbnail of the vessel's lining, there for exactly one reason: so somebody can check whether the culture is eating the wall. I checked mine weekly. I checked it with my eyes. I looked at it and it looked like lining and I wrote "intact."

I was the somebody. I looked at it with my face.

I pull the oldest coupon from vessel one, the first lineage, the one I started from the second collector sample, and slide it under the scope at the highest magnification Jinzhou's repair allows.

Tunnels.

Hundreds of them. Each one exactly as wide as a single cell, running into the lining at a slant, neat and parallel, like a very small and very organized mining operation.

"They didn't escape," I tell the empty lab. "They went through."

Through the vessel wall. Out of the vessel, into the conduit behind it, and along the conduit to the thing they've been practicing on for weeks.

"ARACCSIM. What's the lining on this coupon?"

"Argon inert lining, lot series seven."

"Where else is lot series seven used?"

"Culture vessels. Sample chambers. Fuel tanks. Feed lines. Motor housings."

"Of course it is," I say. "Why would you buy two kinds of lining when you can buy one kind in bulk."

"Section nine of the procurement summary lists the savings."

"I bet it does."

It was a sound decision. I'd like to speak to whoever made it, at length, in a small room.

And then the part I've been walking around since the first slide. I didn't just fail to notice this. I helped.

For weeks I kept a trace of Phengoclept sealed in a window port on every vessel, so I could watch the Asicell hunt. It leaks a little twenty-two point seven through the glass. They like it. They lean toward it. The ones that leaned hardest ate best and divided first, and a generation is about nine hours, which makes sixty-odd generations of the hungriest, pushiest, wall-hugging-est cells I could have asked for.

I wrote a sidebar about selection pressure once. I would like to apologize to the sidebar.

I trained something to dig toward lunch, and then I parked it next to the entire buffet.

"It's the same god damn lining," I say.

"Profanity logged," ARACCSIM says.

"It's not profanity. It's a measurement."

"Understood."

I put my forehead on the cold edge of the bench for a moment. It isn't panic. I know exactly what's happening, and I can move fine. It's the quieter thing, where the cause is in my handwriting. Everything in me goes very calm and very slow, which is how I know it's bad.

Then I lift my head, because the Asicell aren't going to stop eating while I feel things.

---

All right. The engines.

What does an engine do? It pulls fuel out of the tanks, down the line, across the motors. That is the entire job. Tank to line to motor, a conveyor belt of Phengoclept running straight past the diners.

And a few days ago, every motor on this vessel was pulling fuel down that line to make a heat shield. For the better part of an hour we served dinner to the whole system and called it protection.

Shoot.

That's why the loss is increasing. Not just the Asicell breeding. Every cell we pushed through that line was a meal on a plate, delivered, and every one that lit off in a motor was a meal with a fuse.

"ARACCSIM. If we fire nothing at all, no station-keeping, no attitude pulses, nothing that pulls a cell down the line, does the orbit hold?"

"Orbit stable without propulsive correction. Drift within tolerance."

"So we coast."

"Affirmative."

"If we coast, we stop feeding them."

"Affirmative. Recommendation, isolate feed lines and cease propulsive operations."

"That's what I just said."

"I concur."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome."

Stopping the delivery doesn't stop the eating. They're in the tanks. They'll keep going until the tanks are empty or they run out of room to get fat. So I ask the next question, the one I don't want the answer to.

"Projected time until the reserve falls below the return minimum, at the current loss rate."

"Projected, one hundred and ninety-four days. Margin of error, large."

"And the transfer window."

"Opens in two hundred and seventy-nine days."

I do the subtraction. Then I do it again, because that's the rule.

Eighty-five.

The fuel runs out eighty-five days before the door opens. Said out loud to a lab with nobody in it, it still sounds like a number from somebody else's ship.

"Cold shutdown of the Phengomotor stack," I say. "How many steps?"

"One hundred and forty-two."

"And who's authorized to run them?"

"Propulsion authority and a second operator."

Which is Jinzhou. Jinzhou, who understands all one hundred and forty-two, who wrote half of them, who is sealed in a wall with a left arm that isn't done being fixed.

"When can he be woken?"

"Thirty-one hours."

"Is anything scheduled to fire in the next thirty-one hours?"

"Negative."

"Check it."

"Checked."

"Check your check."

"Checked."

I run it myself anyway, line by line, because I'm Ethyn and that's the one thing I've got going for me. Nothing fires. Nothing moves. The ship hangs in its orbit above a planet named after a woman who would have been unbearable about being right, and for thirty-one hours the only thing in motion on the Vanguard is something eating its fuel in the dark.

I make myself useful with what I've got. I label all three slides. I save the coupon in a sealed case, though by now sealed is a word I trust about as far as I can throw it. I write up the tunnels, the gradient, the lining, and the generation count in plain language, written for someone who is going to be very tired when he reads it.

Then I go back across the room and sit down on the floor beside his cubby. Habit. It turns out I've picked up that one too.

"Hey," I tell the readout. "So, uh. I broke the ship."

The readout holds steady at its slow, green hobby.

"In my defense, it was a very good culture. I'd like that on the record."

"ARACCSIM, log that."

"Which part?"

"All of it. Except the profanity."

"The profanity is already logged."

"Of course it is."

Thirty-one hours. I do the math twice. It stays thirty-one.

I put my back against the wall, and I stay there.
