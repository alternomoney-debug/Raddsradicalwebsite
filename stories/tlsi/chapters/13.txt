
## Chapter Thirteen

"Contaminant detected."

That's the first thing I hear. Not good morning. Not welcome back. Two words and a period, in a voice with all the urgency of a bus schedule.

"Contaminant detected."

Again. The light is red. All of it, floor to ceiling, in slow pulses, like the ship is trying to tell me something with its whole body. I'd like it noted that I'm still lying down, and that lying down is the only reason I'm not on the floor.

Everything weighs about seventy percent more than it should. My arm is heavy. My glasses are heavy. I didn't know glasses could be heavy. They sit on my face like a decision.

My berth is sliding out of its cubby at the speed of a drawer being opened by someone who doesn't want to wake the desk. The arms come down, take my pulse, tilt my chin, shine a light into each eye, and conclude, as far as I can tell, that I am technically a person. They retract.

"I heard you," I say. It comes out as gravel. "Twice. Give me a second to, uh. Be a person."

The alarm repeats. It doesn't have a second to give.

So I do what I always do first. I look across the room.

Jinzhou's cubby is sealed. His berth is in. The readout on his frame is slow and green and so steady it looks like a hobby.

Okay. Deduction, while the arms are busy being gentle. I weigh too much. Spin would give me one G, and this isn't one G, this is a floor shoving back. That's thrust. Thrust means the engines are on, and the engines being on means we're going somewhere, and that means somebody has been steering this ship while I was asleep. I'd like to meet them.

"ARACCSIM. Why am I awake, and why is he not?"

A pause, and a hitch on the number when it comes, like a record skipping once.

"Mr. Kenzo's treatment is sixty-three percent complete. Waking him now carries a risk of permanent tissue loss in the left arm. Dr. Vasek's recovery is within parameters for light duty."

"Light duty."

"Correct."

"Name one light duty that starts with 'contaminant detected.'"

"Contaminant detected," ARACCSIM says.

"No, that was. That was a joke. You don't have to answer jokes."

"Understood."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

"Short version of how we got here," I say. "Please."

"Dr. Vasek was struck by a detached external locker during extravehicular retrieval. Mr. Kenzo performed an extravehicular recovery. During the recovery, a structural fragment penetrated Mr. Kenzo's left forearm, and thermal exposure to that arm exceeded suit rating by six minutes. Mr. Kenzo then flew the vessel into orbit manually. Dr. Vasek was found secured in the left seat. Tank 2-E was breached by a stray cell and auto-sealed after four point one seconds. Orbit decay was projected. No crew authority was registered. Contingency return was initiated. The service arms placed both crew in rest state. Current transit day, two."

I go back over that. Slower the second time.

"He went out there."

"Correct."

"On the hull."

"Correct."

"He's not trained for the hull."

"Correct."

"A piece of the ship went through his arm."

"Correct."

"And he flew us up. And buckled me in."

"Correct."

"And then the ship left."

"Correct."

"Without asking."

"The contingency protocol does not ask."

"Of course it doesn't," I say. "Huh."

The last thing I remember is a very good word and a locker I didn't see. Apparently the locker saw me. I look at the cubby for a while. Then I file "tank 2-E" under later. Later-Ethyn's drawer is getting full.

---

"ARACCSIM. What's contaminated?"

"Biological contaminant. Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's the whole ship."

"Correct."

"Can you narrow it down?"

"Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's not narrowing. That's repeating, with confidence."

"Understood."

I unclip the strap and sit up, which at one point seven times my usual weight is an event. My right side, where the locker got me, files a formal complaint. Then a second one, in case the first was lost.

All right. List. I do better with a list.

One. Something alive is on this ship that the ship didn't expect.

Two. The ship can't say where, because the ship's idea of where is here.

Three. There are two people aboard. One of them is me, awake, and the other is a man in a sealed cubby who has been resting for two days. If it's one of us, I'd like to be told gently.

"Air," I say. "Full panel on the cabin atmosphere."

"Cabin atmosphere nominal. No contaminant detected."

"Water."

"Nominal."

"Food stores."

"Nominal."

So the air is fine, the water is fine, the food is fine, and the ship is contaminated. That's a very specific way to be contaminated.

A braver man, or a dumber one, would dump the atmosphere out the airlock right now. I considered it for about four seconds. Then I asked the air a question first, and the air said it was fine, and I believed the air. Venting the ship is what you do when you've run out of plans and want to feel like you picked one.

"Anything else wrong?" I say. "Anything. Doesn't matter how small."

"Separate notice. Phengoclept reserve is below the burn plan."

"Separate from what?"

"From the contaminant alert."

"Why are they separate notices?"

"They were generated by different systems."

"Of course they were. Below by how much?"

"Zero point three percent over the last twelve hours, beyond planned consumption. Rate of loss, increasing."

Zero point three percent doesn't sound like anything. That's the first thing I think. The second thing I think is increasing, and my stomach gets there before I do.

A leaking tank announces itself. Pressure falls, a line frosts over, some alarm with a better personality than this one starts shouting.

"Tank pressures."

"Nominal."

"So it isn't leaving."

"Correct."

Mass down, pressure steady. The fuel isn't getting out of the tanks. It's being used up inside them.

Used by what? Nothing on this ship uses Phengoclept except the engines, and the engines are already in the budget. Which leaves exactly one other thing aboard that treats Phengoclept as lunch.

I know what it is. I think I've known since "being used," if I'm honest. I hold the thought at arm's length, like a lab report with my name on it.

It's too obvious, and the obvious answer has a problem. The obvious answer lives in sealed containers. I sealed them myself. I checked every seal twice, because we do everything twice, because that was the rule.

"ARACCSIM. Integrity on every Asicell container aboard. Sample chambers, culture vessels, the backup unit. All of it."

"All containers sealed. Integrity nominal. No breach detected."

"Amazing," I say.

Because here is what I've got. A fuel supply being eaten by the only organism that eats it. A full set of containers swearing that organism is locked in. One of those is wrong, and I would very much like it to be the sensors, because sensors are a thing you can replace.

Then the thing I filed under later turns out to be now.

"ARACCSIM. Tank 2-E. When it breached, what was outside it?"

"The atmosphere of the cloud layer at breach altitude."

"What's in that atmosphere?"

"Particulate density consistent with the Asicell collection zone."

"So for four point one seconds, a hole in a fuel tank was open to the thing I flew here to collect."

"Correct."

"And then the hole closed. With the cloud already inside."

"Ingress occurred before seal."

I'd like to say I'm calm. I'm something. It has the shape of calm and the weight of a falling elevator.

It wasn't my containers. My containers are fine. My containers have always been fine. It was the planet, reaching in through a hole in the ship and helping itself to a tank.

I'm almost relieved, which is an awful thing to be relieved about.

The relief lasts about two seconds. Then I notice the thing I'm standing on.

The engines are running.

What does an engine do? It pulls fuel out of the tanks, down the line, across the motors. That is the entire job. Tank to line to motor, a conveyor belt of Phengoclept running straight past the diners, and the diners have had two days of it. Every pulse is a meal on a plate. Every cell that lights off in a motor is a meal with a fuse.

"ARACCSIM. Where's the nearest console with propulsion control that isn't in the cockpit?"

The cockpit is the far end of the ship, and I'm in a body that weighs one and a half of itself.

"Backup propulsion console, server room."

"Can the secondary pilot shut the drives down from there?"

"Cold shutdown, yes. Section four point two permits the secondary pilot to substitute, with two confirmations on each of the first two steps. Warning. Halting the drives forfeits the remaining acceleration profile. Revised arrival cannot be calculated until the fuel state is known."

"So the answer is 'later.'"

"Correct."

A ship that gets home fast and a ship that gets home are two different ships, and only one of them has fuel at the end. I do the math once, because there isn't time for twice, and it comes out the same way every time I look at it.

I go.

---

The server room is two decks down the ladder shaft, which at one point seven is two decks down a ladder that has opinions. I go down it the way a sack of flour would, with fewer thoughts.

The hum from the wall is the sound of ARACCSIM thinking. It's louder here. The backup console is a gray slab with a cover over a red switch, because the people who design ships believe in cover plates and in putting the emergency button where only a person who's already panicking will look.

I'm not panicking. I know exactly what's happening. That's the unpleasant part.

"ARACCSIM. Cold shutdown of the Phengomotor stack. How many steps?"

"Six."

I'd been braced for a lot more. I don't say so, in case it hears me.

"Read them to me."

"One. Close tank valves. Two. Halt drive pulses. Three. Turbine to maximum output. Four. Radiators to full dump. Five. Thermal runaway prevention, level one. Six. Turbine spin-down."

"Why does a shutdown have 'maximum' in it?"

"The stack has been at operating temperature for two days. The fuel in the motors is at the boiling point of water. The turbine is the only means of removing motor heat. Maximum output removes it fastest."

"So to cool the engines, I make more power."

"Correct."

"And the power goes where?"

"Nothing aboard requires it. Surplus is dumped to the radiators."

"As heat."

"Correct."

I look at that for a while. To cool the engines, I make electricity. To get rid of the electricity, I turn it back into heat. Then I throw the heat at the stars. I've fixed a heat problem by moving it one room over and giving it a receipt.

That's a little funny. I'm not going to say so out loud.

"Is there a version where the motors stay warm?"

"Yes. The warm loop. Fuel continues to circulate."

"The fuel they're eating."

"Correct."

"Then no."

"Warning. A cold stack restarts poorly. The spin motors run rough below operating temperature."

"Noted. Later-Ethyn can have that one."

"Step one," I say. "Confirmed."

"One confirmation recorded."

"Confirmed again."

"Two confirmations recorded. Closing tank valves."

I feel it through the deck, a row of heavy thunks, like very large and unimpressed doors.

"Step two. Confirmed. Confirmed again."

"Two confirmations recorded. Halting drives."

The floor stops being a floor between one word and the next.

Quiet arrives all at once, like someone opened a door in a loud room. The weight leaves me, and I come up off the deck, and I hook a foot under the console rail before the ceiling gets any ideas.

"Step three."

"Turbine to maximum output."

A sound comes up through the wall, low, and then not low. The console's temperature readout, which has been sitting on a number I didn't like, begins to slide down it.

"Step four."

"Radiators to full dump."

The hull camera shows the radiator fins from the outside. Dull gray, then dark red, then orange. It's a lovely color. It's the color of a problem.

"Radiator surface temperature rising," ARACCSIM says. "Adjacent tank wall temperature rising."

"How adjacent?"

"Eleven centimeters."

"Of fuel that's at the boiling point of water."

"Correct."

And there it is, the second problem. I'm cooling the engines by building a very large new heat source next door to a very large tank of something that does not enjoy being warmed. The panel calls it PGC. I spell it out in my head, out of respect. Phengoclept gets excited when you nudge it. Excited Phengoclept gets hotter. Hotter Phengoclept gets more excited. That's a runaway, and at the bottom of every runaway is the word "bang."

"Step five," I say. My voice has gone up half a note. I bring it back down. "Thermal runaway prevention. Level one."

"Confirm."

"Confirmed."

The tanks have chill lines in their walls, the same idea as the pipes under an ice rink. Somebody who loved me, or at least loved a good insurance policy, put them there so that if anything hot ever got near the fuel, a fire, say, you could freeze the tank before it got ideas.

"Level one engaged," ARACCSIM says. "Freeze lines drawing surplus power."

"It's using the surplus?"

"Correct."

"Finally. A job for it."

"Radiator load decreasing."

"What's level two?"

"Level two is not recommended."

"Then I'm going to stay on one."

Most of the procedure turns out to be waiting. ARACCSIM reads me the temperatures every five minutes, in the voice it uses for everything, and every five minutes the numbers are a little better. I hang in the air with one foot under a rail and watch a gauge go down. It's the least heroic thing I've ever done and possibly the most useful.

"Reserve loss rate," ARACCSIM says at minute twenty-six. "Decreasing."

"Decreasing."

"Correct."

"Is that the freeze lines?"

"Insufficient data."

"Huh," I say.

At minute forty-one, the motor temperatures reach the floor of the readout.

"Step six."

"Turbine spin-down."

The big sound in the wall winds down, down, and out. Then the quiet comes back, a different quiet, one with nothing left in it to wait for.

"Cold shutdown complete," ARACCSIM says.

I let go of the rail and drift for a while.

"Thank you," I say.

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

"Now," I say. "A floor."

I take the ladder shaft up the way a person does when nothing weighs anything, which is badly. At the hatch to Lab A I overshoot, hit the frame shoulder-first, and wake the locker's bruise, which takes the opportunity to hand itself a microphone. I grab the frame. I try again, slower.

The centrifuge needs two keys, turned together, at the panel in the cockpit. The panel says so in capital letters. DUAL KEY REQUIRED. OVERRIDE KEYS AVAILABLE IN LAB. I've walked past that sentence a hundred times and read it as one of those lines that exist so lawyers can sleep.

The override case is a red box on the wall cabinet. The label reads IN THE EVENT THAT ONE CREW MEMBER IS INCAPACITATED. Inside is a single key on a cord, and a laminated card whose first line is Step 1. Do not panic.

I'm not panicking. I just notice that the card thought of me.

The suit lockers are by the airlock, and I put a suit on. I'd like to be honest about why. The contaminant has no address. I was told the containers were sealed by the same sensors that tell me the air is fine, and if I'm going to be wrong, I'd like to be wrong in something sealed. It takes four minutes, and the left boot goes on the right foot once, which the suit is gracious about.

I open the inner door of the airlock the way you open a door in a house where something is wrong. Nothing is behind it. Doors are like that.

The cockpit panel has two key slots a meter and a half apart. The card says the override gives me six seconds between them.

First try, I turn the key in slot one, push off, and sail past slot two with the dignity of a man leaving a party. Second try, I hook a foot under Jinzhou's seat and lean. The key goes in. It turns.

SPIN-UP INITIATED. 1G IN 5 MINUTES. SECURE ALL LOOSE ITEMS.

I buckle into my seat, the left one. I am the loose item.

Five minutes is a long time to float. I spend most of it thinking about the person who strapped me into this seat while there was a piece of the ship in his arm. Then weight comes back the way a rumor does. First a lean. Then a lean I can feel. Then my fur settles against my arms like it's been told to, and then the floor is a floor, and I'm grateful for that in a small, private way I'd never been grateful for a floor before this month.

I take the suit off in the cockpit and leave it on the seat like a shed skin. The air, as ever, says it's fine.

---

"ARACCSIM. Draw three fuel samples to the Lab A bench. Tank 2-E, the main feed line, and the motor six intake."

I pick motor six because it's plumbed closest to 2-E, and a hunch is just a hypothesis without the paperwork.

"Substitution under section four point two remains active. Taps opening."

I'm the secondary pilot and the second confirmation. It's nice to be needed twice.

The walk to Lab A is long enough that my body treats it like a hike. By the time I reach the bench, the three vials are waiting in the rack with their little tap labels, looking innocent. Fuel always does.

The microscope is the one Jinzhou fixed. I trust it more than I trust most people.

Slide one, tank 2-E.

The Phengoclept cells are there, small bright emitters at twenty-two point seven zero six, far fewer than a fuel sample ought to show. And among them, thick as a crowd, unmistakable: blue at the membrane, red toward the center, green running through both.

I know them like my own handwriting. I drew them for the write-up. I was going to put them in Figure 3.

I stop counting at three hundred and tell the microscope it can keep the rest. The green in them is bright, the green of something that has been eating very well. One of them is wrapped around a Phengoclept cell mid-meal, and I watch it for a while, which is the wrong thing to do and the only thing I can do.

They're slow, though. Slower than my cultures ever were, drifting more than swimming. I write "slow?" in the log, with the question mark, because I don't know what it means yet.

Slide two, the main feed line.

Forty-one. I count twice. It's still forty-one.

Slide three, motor six.

Seven. Seven is a rounding error until you remember what they do next, which is eight.

Three hundred, forty-one, seven. That isn't a scatter. That's a line, and it's pointing at the motors, and for two days the motors have been pulling it along.

Motor six threw a cell wide on the way down, long before 2-E ever opened, so that one was just bad luck. Everything since is not.

I say some things to the microscope. They do not go in the record.

It doesn't answer, which is fair. It's an instrument.

---

I sit down on a stool because my knees have an opinion.

So. The containers are sealed. The cultures inside them are alive and well. And tank 2-E, which closed after four seconds, has a crowd in it, and the feed line, which is on the other side of a closed valve, has forty-one.

A closed valve is a promise about fuel. It isn't a promise about things that dig.

"ARACCSIM. Is there a lining coupon in the 2-E isolation valve?"

"Affirmative. Witness coupon, isolation valve 2-E."

Every valve on a fuel line carries one. A thumbnail of the lining, there for exactly one reason: so somebody can check whether the fuel is eating the wall. Nobody checks. Nobody checks because nobody has ever needed to.

The service arm brings it out in a sealed sleeve. I slide it under the scope at the highest magnification Jinzhou's repair allows.

Tunnels.

Hundreds of them. Each one exactly as wide as a single cell, running into the lining at a slant, neat and parallel, like a very small and very organized mining operation.

"They didn't stay in 2-E," I tell the empty lab. "They went through."

Through the lining. Past the valve, along the line, toward the thing they've been smelling the whole way.

I pull my oldest coupon from culture vessel one, three weeks of Asicell on the other side of it. Smooth. Clean. Mine never dug. Mine never had a reason. The wild ones had every tank on the ship, lit up through a curtain.

"ARACCSIM. What's the lining on this coupon?"

"Argon inert lining, lot series seven."

"Where else is lot series seven used?"

"Culture vessels. Sample chambers. Fuel tanks. Feed lines. Motor housings."

"Of course it is," I say. "Why would you buy two kinds of lining when you can buy one kind in bulk."

"Section nine of the procurement summary lists the savings."

"I bet it does."

It was a sound decision. I'd like to speak to whoever made it, at length, in a small room.

"It's the same god damn lining," I say.

"Profanity logged," ARACCSIM says.

"It's not profanity. It's a measurement."

"Understood."

And then the part I've been walking around since the first slide.

The cable.

ARACCSIM offered to cut it. I remember the sentence, and I remember saying no, fast, because everything we came for was on the end of that cable. That sample is the whole mission. It's also, I now think, a minute and a half I spent on a hull under a shield that was coming apart.

"ARACCSIM. How long did untangling the cable cost us?"

"Cable recovery delayed the climb by one minute, fifty seconds."

"Would the covers have held if we'd climbed sooner?"

"Unknown."

"Unknown," I repeat.

"Correct."

Unknown is a verdict I'm going to be carrying for a while. I'd like to set it down somewhere. There isn't anywhere.

I put my forehead on the cold edge of the bench for a moment. It isn't panic. I know exactly what's happening, and I can move fine. It's the quieter thing, where the cause is a sentence I said out loud. Everything in me goes very calm and very slow, which is how I know it's bad.

Then I lift my head, because the Asicell aren't going to stop eating while I feel things.

---

Stopping the delivery doesn't stop the eating. They're in the tanks. But the tanks are frost now, and the loss rate dropped when the freeze lines came on, which is the only good number I have. Lower is a long way from zero. So I ask the question I don't want the answer to.

"Projected time until the reserve falls below the minimum needed to brake at Wask-ki, at the present loss rate."

"Three hundred and ten days. Margin of error, large."

"And the trip home?"

"At current velocity, more than six years."

I do the subtraction. Then I do it again, because that's the rule.

The fuel runs out about five years before the trip does. Said out loud to a lab with nobody in it, it still sounds like a number from somebody else's ship.

I want Jinzhou awake. Not to run the shutdown. I've done the shutdown. The next question is a hard one, and he's the one who's good at hard ones.

"When can he be woken?"

"Thirty-one hours."

"Is anything scheduled to fire in the next thirty-one hours?"

"Negative. Drives cold."

"Check it."

"Checked."

"Check your check."

"Checked."

I run it myself anyway, line by line, because I'm Ethyn and that's the one thing I've got going for me. Nothing fires. Nothing moves. The ship falls through the dark at the speed it had when I pulled the switch, and for thirty-one hours the only thing in motion on the Vanguard is whatever the cold didn't stop.

I make myself useful with what I've got. I label all three slides. I save the coupon in a sealed case, though by now sealed is a word I trust about as far as I can throw it. I write up the tunnels, the gradient, the lining, and the clock in plain language, written for someone who is going to be very tired when he reads it.

Then I go back across the ship and sit down on the floor beside his cubby. Habit. It turns out I've picked up that one too.

"Hey," I tell the readout. "So, uh. I broke the ship."

The readout holds steady at its slow, green hobby.

"Okay. A locker broke the ship. I'd like partial credit."

"ARACCSIM, log that."

"Which part?"

"All of it. Except the profanity."

"The profanity is already logged."

"Of course it is."

Thirty-one hours. I do the math twice. It stays thirty-one.

I put my back against the wall, and I stay there.

*END OF CHAPTER THIRTEEN*
