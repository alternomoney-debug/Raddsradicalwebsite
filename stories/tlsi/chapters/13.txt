"Contaminant detected."

That's the first thing I hear. Not "good morning." Not "welcome back." Two words and a period, in a voice with all the urgency of a bus schedule.

"Contaminant detected."

Again. And the light is red. All of it. Floor to ceiling, in slow pulses, like the ship is trying to tell me something with its whole body. I'm lying down. Lying down is the only reason I'm not on the floor.

Everything is heavy. My arm is heavy. My glasses are heavy. I didn't know glasses could be heavy! They sit on my face like a decision.

My berth slides out of its cubby at the speed of a drawer opened by someone who doesn't want to wake the desk. The arms come down, take my pulse, tilt my chin, shine a light in each eye, and decide, as far as I can tell, that I'm technically a person. Then they go back up.

"I heard you," I say. It comes out like gravel. "Twice. Give me a second to, uh. Be a person."

The alarm repeats. It doesn't have a second to give.

So I do what I always do first. I look across the room.

Jinzhou's cubby is sealed. His berth is in. The readout on his frame is slow and green and so steady it looks like a hobby.

Okay. Deduction, while the arms are busy being gentle.

Why am I heavy? The centrifuge would give me one G. This isn't one G. This is more. This is the floor shoving back. So that's thrust. Thrust means engines. Engines on means we're going somewhere.

And if we're going somewhere, somebody's been steering this ship while I was asleep.

Who?

"ARACCSIM. Why am I awake, and why isn't he?"

A pause. Then a hitch on the number, like a record skipping once.

"Mr. Kenzo's treatment is sixty-three percent complete. Waking him now carries a risk of permanent tissue loss in the left arm. Dr. Vasek's recovery is within parameters for light duty."

"Light duty."

"Correct."

"Name one light duty that starts with 'contaminant detected.'"

"Contaminant detected," ARACCSIM says.

"No, that was. That was a joke. You don't have to answer jokes."

"Understood."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

"Short version of how we got here. Please."

"Dr. Vasek was struck by a detached external locker during extravehicular retrieval. Mr. Kenzo performed an extravehicular recovery. During the recovery, a structural fragment penetrated Mr. Kenzo's left forearm, and thermal exposure to that arm exceeded suit rating by six minutes. Mr. Kenzo had entered a staged throttle ramp before going outside and released the final step from the airlock. Orbit was achieved with both crew in the airlock. Tank 2-E was breached by a stray cell and auto-sealed after four point one seconds. Orbit decay was projected. No crew authority was registered. Contingency return was initiated. The service arms placed both crew in rest state. Current transit day, two."

Okay. That's a lot. Again, slower.

"He went out there."

"Correct."

"On the hull."

"Correct."

"He's not trained for the hull!"

"Correct."

"A piece of the ship went through his arm."

"Correct."

"And he got us up. From the airlock."

"Correct."

"And then the ship left."

"Correct."

"Without asking anybody."

"The contingency protocol does not ask."

"Of course it doesn't," I say. "Huh."

The last thing I remember is a really good word and a locker I didn't see. Apparently the locker saw me. I look at his cubby for a while.

Then I put "tank 2-E" in the drawer for later. Later-Ethyn's drawer is getting full.

---

"ARACCSIM. What's contaminated?"

"Biological contaminant. Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's the whole ship."

"Correct."

"Can you narrow it down?"

"Source field, Vanguard MK1."

"That's not narrowing. That's repeating, with confidence."

"Understood."

I unclip the strap and sit up. At this weight, sitting up is an event. My right side, where the locker got me, files a complaint. Then a second one, in case the first got lost.

Okay. List. I'm better with a list.

One. Something alive is on this ship that the ship didn't expect.

Two. The ship can't tell me where, because the ship's idea of "where" is "here."

Three. There are two people aboard. One of them is me, awake. The other one is in a sealed cubby. If it's one of us, I want to be told gently.

So. Where would something alive hide? Start with the stuff we breathe, drink and eat.

"Air. Full panel."

"Cabin atmosphere nominal. No contaminant detected."

"Water."

"Nominal."

"Food."

"Nominal."

Huh. So the air is fine, the water is fine, the food is fine, and the ship is contaminated.

That's a very specific way to be contaminated.

A braver person, or a dumber one, would dump the whole atmosphere out the airlock right now. I considered it. For about four seconds. Then I asked the air a question, and the air said it was fine, and I believed the air. Venting the ship is what you do when you're out of plans and want to feel like you picked one.

"Anything else wrong? Anything. Doesn't matter how small."

"Separate notice. Phengoclept reserve is below the burn plan."

"Separate from what?"

"From the contaminant alert."

"Why are they two different notices?"

"They were generated by different systems."

"Of course they were. Below by how much?"

"Zero point three percent over the last twelve hours, beyond planned consumption. Rate of loss, increasing."

Zero point three percent. That sounds like nothing. That's my first thought.

My second thought is "increasing," and my stomach gets there before I do.

Okay. Think. Where does fuel go if it's not going into the engines? A leak, right? But a leak announces itself. Pressure drops. A line frosts over. Some alarm with a better personality than this one starts screaming.

"Tank pressures."

"Nominal."

So it isn't leaving.

Mass going down. Pressure staying the same. So the fuel isn't getting out of the tanks.

It's getting used up. Inside them.

Used up by what? Nothing on this ship uses Phengoclept except the engines, and the engines are already counted. Which leaves exactly one other thing aboard that treats Phengoclept like lunch.

Oh.

I know what it is. Honestly I knew at "used up." I just hold it at arm's length, like a lab report with my name on it.

Because there's a problem with the obvious answer. The obvious answer lives in sealed containers. I sealed them. I checked every seal twice, because we do everything twice.

"ARACCSIM. Integrity on every Asicell container aboard. Sample chambers, culture vessels, the backup unit. All of it."

"All containers sealed. Integrity nominal. No breach detected."

"Amazing," I say.

So here's what I've got. Something is eating the fuel. Only one thing eats fuel. And every container that thing lives in swears it's locked up tight. One of those facts is wrong. I really hope it's the sensors. Sensors you can replace.

And then the thing I put in the drawer turns out to be now.

"ARACCSIM. Tank 2-E. When it got breached. What was outside it?"

"The atmosphere of the cloud layer at breach altitude."

"What's in that atmosphere?"

"Particulate density consistent with the Asicell collection zone."

"So for four point one seconds, a hole in a fuel tank was open to the exact stuff I flew here to collect."

"Correct."

"And then the hole closed. With the cloud already inside."

"Ingress occurred before seal."

Oh, fudge.

I want to say I'm calm. I'm something. It's shaped like calm and it weighs as much as a falling elevator.

It wasn't my containers. My containers are fine! My containers were always fine. It was the planet, reaching in through a hole in the ship and helping itself.

I'm almost relieved. Which is an awful thing to be relieved about.

The relief lasts about two seconds. Then I notice what I'm standing on.

The engines are running.

What does an engine do? It pulls fuel out of the tank, down the line, past the motors. That's the whole job. Tank, line, motor. A conveyor belt of Phengoclept rolling right past the diners. And the diners have had two days of it. Every pulse is a meal on a plate. Every cell that lights in a motor is a meal with a fuse.

So stop the conveyor belt. Now.

"ARACCSIM. Where's the nearest console with propulsion control that isn't the cockpit?"

Because the cockpit is all the way at the other end of the ship, and I'm in a body that weighs one point seven of itself.

"Backup propulsion console, server room."

"Can the secondary pilot shut the drives down from there?"

"Cold shutdown, yes. Section four point two permits the secondary pilot to substitute, with two confirmations on each of the first two steps. Warning. Halting the drives forfeits the remaining acceleration profile. Revised arrival cannot be calculated until the fuel state is known."

"So the answer is 'later.'"

"Correct."

A ship that gets home fast and a ship that gets home are two different ships. Only one of them still has fuel at the end. I do the math once. There isn't time for twice. It comes out the same either way.

I go.

---

The server room is two decks down the ladder shaft. At one point seven G, that's two decks down a ladder with opinions. I go down it like a sack of flour. With fewer thoughts.

The hum in the wall is ARACCSIM thinking. It's louder down here. The backup console is a gray slab with a cover over a red switch. Ship designers love cover plates. They also love putting the emergency button exactly where only a panicking person would look.

I'm not panicking. I know exactly what's happening. That's the bad part.

"ARACCSIM. Cold shutdown of the Phengomotor stack. How many steps?"

"Six."

Six! I'd braced for about forty. I don't say so, in case it hears me and adds some.

"Read them to me."

"One. Close tank valves. Two. Halt drive pulses. Three. Turbine to maximum output. Four. Radiators to full dump. Five. Thermal runaway prevention, level one. Six. Turbine spin-down."

Wait.

"Why does a shutdown have 'maximum' in it?"

"The stack has been at operating temperature for two days. The fuel in the motors is at the boiling point of water. The turbine is the only way to carry motor heat away. Maximum output carries it away fastest."

"So to cool the engines, I make more power."

"Correct."

"And the power goes where?"

"Nothing aboard requires it. Surplus is dumped to the radiators."

"As heat."

"Correct."

Okay. Follow the energy. That's the trick with stuff like this.

The motors are hot. Hot is energy. The turbine turns that energy into electricity, and that pulls heat out of the motors. Great. But energy doesn't disappear. It just moves. So the electricity has to go somewhere. And the only place left is the radiators, which turn it right back into heat and throw it at the stars.

So I'm fixing a heat problem by moving it one room over and getting a receipt.

That's a little funny. I'm not going to say so out loud.

"Is there a version where the motors stay warm?"

"Yes. The warm loop. Fuel continues to circulate."

"The fuel they're eating."

"Correct."

"Then no."

"Warning. A cold stack restarts poorly. The spin motors run rough below operating temperature."

"Noted. Later-Ethyn can have that one."

"Step one," I say. "Confirmed."

"One confirmation recorded."

"Confirmed again."

"Two confirmations recorded. Closing tank valves."

I feel it through the deck. A row of heavy thunks, like very large, very unimpressed doors.

"Step two. Confirmed. Confirmed again."

"Two confirmations recorded. Halting drives."

The floor stops being a floor between one word and the next.

The quiet shows up all at once, like somebody opened a door in a loud room. The weight leaves me, and I float up off the deck, and I hook a foot under the console rail before the ceiling gets any ideas.

"Step three."

"Turbine to maximum output."

A sound comes up through the wall. Low. Then not low. The temperature readout on the console, sitting on a number I don't like, starts sliding down.

"Step four."

"Radiators to full dump."

The hull camera shows the radiator fins from outside. Dull gray. Then dark red. Then orange. It's a lovely color. It's the color of a problem.

"Radiator surface temperature rising," ARACCSIM says. "Adjacent tank wall temperature rising."

"How adjacent?"

"They share a wall."

"A wall. With fuel that's at the boiling point of water."

"Correct."

And there it is. Problem number two.

I'm cooling the engines by building a big new heater right next to a big tank of something that really doesn't like being warmed up. Here's why. Phengoclept gets excited when you nudge it. Excited Phengoclept gets hotter. Hotter Phengoclept gets more excited. That's a loop. That's a runaway. And at the bottom of every runaway is the word "bang."

"Step five," I say. My voice has gone up half a note. I drag it back down. "Thermal runaway prevention. Level one."

"Confirm."

"Confirmed."

The tanks have chill lines in their walls. Same idea as the pipes under an ice rink. Somebody who loved me, or at least loved a good insurance policy, put them there so that if anything hot ever got near the fuel, you could freeze the tank before it got ideas.

"Level one engaged," ARACCSIM says. "Freeze lines drawing surplus power."

"It's using the extra power?"

"Correct."

"Oh, finally. A job for it!"

"Radiator load decreasing."

"What's level two?"

"Level two is not recommended."

"Then I'm going to stay on one."

Most of this turns out to be waiting. Every five minutes ARACCSIM reads me the temperatures, in the same voice it uses for everything, and every five minutes they're a little better. I hang in the air with one foot under a rail and watch a gauge go down. It's the least heroic thing I've ever done. It might be the most useful.

"Reserve loss rate," ARACCSIM says at minute twenty-six. "Decreasing."

Decreasing! Why, though? The engines being off would stop the delivery, sure. But the diners are still in the tank.

Unless the tank's getting colder. Cold slows everything down. That's not even science. That's just being a cell.

"Is that the freeze lines?"

"Insufficient data."

"Huh," I say. I put "cold?" in the drawer, with a question mark.

At minute forty-one, the motor temperatures hit the bottom of the readout.

"Step six."

"Turbine spin-down."

The big sound in the wall winds down. Down. Out. And the quiet comes back. A different quiet. One with nothing left in it to wait for.

"Cold shutdown complete," ARACCSIM says.

I let go of the rail and just drift for a minute.

"Thank you," I say.

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

"Now," I say. "A floor."

"Recommended," ARACCSIM says. "Crew is weightless with the drives cold. Bone and muscle loss begin within days without load. Centrifuge operation is advised for the duration of the drift."

"So you want a floor too."

"I do not require a floor. You do."

"Okay. Fair."

I go up the ladder shaft the way a person does when nothing weighs anything. Which is badly. At the Lab A hatch I overshoot, hit the frame with my shoulder, and wake up the locker bruise, which grabs the chance to make a speech. I grab the frame. I try again. Slower.

The centrifuge needs two keys, turned together, at the panel in the cockpit. It says so in capital letters. DUAL KEY REQUIRED. OVERRIDE KEYS AVAILABLE IN LAB. I've floated past that sign a hundred times and read it as one of those lines that exist so lawyers can sleep.

The override case is a red box on the wall cabinet. The label says IN THE EVENT THAT ONE CREW MEMBER IS INCAPACITATED. Inside is one key on a cord, and a laminated card. The first line on the card is *Step 1. Do not panic.*

I'm not panicking. I just notice the card thought about me. Hi, laminator. Again.

The suit lockers are by the airlock. I put a suit on. Why? Because the contaminant has no address. The same sensors that swore my containers were sealed also swear the air is fine. If I'm going to be wrong, I'd like to be wrong inside something sealed. It takes four minutes. The left boot goes on the right foot once. The suit is very gracious about it.

I open the inner airlock door the way you open a door in a house where something's wrong. Nothing's behind it. Doors are like that.

The cockpit panel has two key slots, a meter and a half apart. The card says the override gives me six seconds between them.

Okay. First try. I turn the key in slot one, push off, and sail right past slot two with the dignity of a man leaving a party.

Second try. I hook a foot under Jinzhou's seat and lean. The key goes in. It turns.

SPIN-UP INITIATED. 1G IN 5 MINUTES. SECURE ALL LOOSE ITEMS.

I buckle into my seat. The left one. I am the loose item.

Five minutes is a long time to float. I spend most of it thinking about the guy who dragged me up a hull with a piece of the ship through his arm. Then the weight comes back the way a rumor does. A lean. Then a lean I can feel. Then my fur settles down on my arms like it's been told to, and the floor is a floor. And I'm grateful for it in a small, private way I've never been grateful for a floor before.

I take the suit off in the cockpit and leave it on the seat like a shed skin. The air, as usual, says it's fine.

---

Okay. Time to actually look.

"ARACCSIM. Draw three fuel samples to the Lab A bench. Tank 2-E. The main feed line. And the motor six intake."

Why motor six? It's plumbed closest to 2-E. And a hunch is just a hypothesis without the paperwork.

"Substitution under section four point two remains active. Taps opening."

So I'm the secondary pilot and the second confirmation. It's nice to be needed twice.

The walk to Lab A is long enough that my body treats it like a hike. The three vials are waiting in the rack with their little labels, looking innocent. Fuel always looks innocent.

The microscope is the one Jinzhou fixed. I trust it more than most people.

Slide one. Tank 2-E.

The Phengoclept is there. Little bright emitters at twenty-two point seven zero six. But way fewer than a fuel sample should have. And all around them, thick as a crowd: blue at the membrane, red in the middle, green running through both.

I know those like my own handwriting. I drew them for the write-up. They were going to be Figure 3.

I stop counting at three hundred. The microscope can keep the rest. The green in them is bright. That's the green of something that's been eating really well. One of them is wrapped around a Phengoclept cell, mid-meal. I watch it for a while. That's the wrong thing to do. It's also the only thing I can do.

They're slow, though. Way slower than my cultures ever were. Drifting more than swimming. Why slow? I write "slow?" in the log, with the question mark. Next to "cold?" Huh. Those two might be friends.

Slide two. The main feed line.

Forty-one. I count twice. Still forty-one.

Slide three. Motor six.

Seven.

Seven is a rounding error. Until you remember what they do next. Which is eight.

Three hundred. Forty-one. Seven.

That's not random. That's a line. Most at the tank, fewer down the pipe, a few at the motor. It points straight at the engines. And for two days, the engines have been pulling it along.

Motor six threw a cell wide on the way down, long before 2-E ever opened. That one was just bad luck. Everything since then is not.

"Fudge," I tell the microscope. "Fudge. Fudge, fudge, fudge."

It doesn't answer. That's fair. It's an instrument.

---

I sit down on a stool. My knees have an opinion.

Okay. So. The containers are sealed. The cultures inside them are fine. And tank 2-E, which was only open for four seconds, has a crowd. And the feed line, which is on the other side of a closed valve, has forty-one.

How? How do you get past a closed valve?

A closed valve keeps fuel in. It's a promise about fuel. It isn't a promise about things that dig.

"ARACCSIM. Is there a lining coupon in the 2-E isolation valve?"

"Affirmative. Witness coupon, isolation valve 2-E."

Every valve on a fuel line has one. A little thumbnail of the lining material, there for exactly one reason: so somebody can check whether the fuel is eating the walls. Nobody ever checks. Nobody's ever needed to.

An arm brings it out in a sealed sleeve. I slide it under the scope at the highest magnification Jinzhou's repair allows.

Tunnels.

Hundreds of them! Each one exactly one cell wide. All running into the lining at the same slant, neat and parallel, like a very small and very organized mining company.

"They didn't stay in 2-E," I tell the empty lab. "They went through."

Through the lining. Past the valve. Down the line. Toward the thing they could smell the whole way.

I pull my oldest coupon from culture vessel one. A week of Asicell on the other side of it. Smooth. Clean. Not a scratch.

Huh. So why didn't mine dig?

Because mine had no reason to. They were fed. Nobody waved dinner at them through a wall. The wild ones had every tank on the ship lit up behind a curtain.

"ARACCSIM. What's the lining on this coupon made of?"

"Argon inert lining, lot series seven."

"Where else is lot series seven used?"

"Culture vessels. Sample chambers. Fuel tanks. Feed lines. Motor housings."

"Of course it is," I say. "Why buy two kinds of lining when you can buy one kind in bulk?"

"Section nine of the procurement summary lists the savings."

"I bet it does."

It was a sensible decision. I'd like to talk to whoever made it. At length. In a small room.

"It's the same fudging lining," I say.

"Profanity logged," ARACCSIM says.

"That's not profanity. That's fudge. I don't swear."

"Profanity logged."

"It's a measurement!"

"Understood."

And then the part I've been walking around since the first slide.

The cable.

ARACCSIM offered to cut it. I remember the sentence. I remember saying no, fast, because everything we came for was on the end of it. That sample is the whole mission. It's also, I'm starting to think, a minute and a half I spent on a hull under a shield that was falling apart.

"ARACCSIM. How long did untangling the cable cost us?"

"Cable recovery delayed the climb by one minute, fifty seconds."

"Would the covers have held if we'd climbed sooner?"

"Unknown."

"Unknown," I say.

"Correct."

Unknown is going to be heavy to carry for a while. I want to put it down somewhere. There isn't anywhere.

I put my forehead on the cold edge of the bench for a moment. It's not panic. I know exactly what's happening, and I can move fine. It's the quieter thing. Where the cause of the problem is a sentence I said out loud. Everything in me goes calm and slow. That's how I know it's bad.

Then I lift my head. The Asicell aren't going to stop eating while I have feelings.

---

Stopping the delivery doesn't stop the eating. They're already in the tanks. But the tanks are frosting over now, and the loss rate dropped when the freeze lines came on. That's the only good number I've got.

Lower is a long way from zero, though. So I ask the question I don't want answered.

"How long until the reserve drops below what it takes to get us home? At this rate."

"Three hundred and ten days. Margin of error, large."

"And the trip home?"

"Vanguard MK1 is no longer under acceleration. Current velocity is sufficient for maneuvering within this system only. At current velocity, Wask-ki is not reachable."

"Not reachable in what, years?"

"Not reachable."

I try to do the subtraction anyway, because that's the rule. And there's nothing to subtract from.

That takes me a minute.

The burn I just killed was the burn that takes us home. It was two days into a job that needed months. We're not on our way home, slower. We're barely on our way anywhere. We're just a little to one side of where we started.

Said out loud to an empty lab, it still sounds like a number from somebody else's ship. It's not a number at all. That's worse.

I want Jinzhou awake. Not to run the shutdown. I did the shutdown. The next question is a hard one, and he's the one who's good at hard ones.

"When can he be woken?"

"Thirty-one hours."

"Is anything scheduled to fire in the next thirty-one hours?"

"Negative. Drives cold."

"Check it."

"Checked."

"Check your check."

"Checked."

I check it myself anyway, line by line, because I'm Ethyn, and checking is the one thing I've got going for me. Nothing fires. Nothing moves. The ship just falls through the dark at whatever speed it had when I flipped the switch. For thirty-one hours, the only thing moving on the Vanguard is whatever the cold didn't stop.

So I make myself useful. I label all three slides. I put the coupon in a sealed case, even though "sealed" is a word I trust about as far as I can throw it right now. I write up the tunnels, the trail, the lining, and the clock. In plain language. For somebody who's going to be really tired when he reads it.

Then I go back across the ship and sit on the floor next to his cubby. Habit. Turns out I picked that one up too.

"Hey," I tell the readout. "So, uh. I broke the ship."

The readout keeps doing its slow green hobby.

"Okay. A locker broke the ship. I want partial credit."

"ARACCSIM, log that."

"Which part?"

"All of it. Except the profanity."

"The profanity is already logged."

"Of course it is."

Thirty-one hours. I do the math twice. It stays thirty-one.

I put my back against the wall, and I stay.

*END OF CHAPTER THIRTEEN*
