
Warm.

That's the first thing. Just warm, like you are when you've been asleep long enough that the blanket and the body temperature have reached an agreement and everything is fine and there's no reason to be anywhere else.

I'm not anywhere else. I'm here. Wherever here is.

Something moves at the edge of it. A sound, maybe. A voice.

I don't chase it. It'll come back if it matters.

---

Y'shari's office has a plant in the corner I've never paid attention to before. It's doing fine. Someone waters it. I notice it now because I've run out of things to look at that aren't Y'shari's face and the phone on her desk and the door, which is still open, which is the only fact in the room that currently matters to me.

"The Phengoclept breeding data," she says. "All of it. The full cycle research, the containment protocols, the propagation notes. You know it. There's nobody left alive who knows it the way you do."

"I didn't sign anything," I say.

"You signed the facility clearance documents."

"That's not."

"It has a clause."

She reaches for the phone.

I run the calculation in the half second before her paw gets there. Door still open. Guard outside, I heard him in the corridor ten minutes ago, probably still there. Medic somewhere in the building, had to have been pre-positioned, nobody moves that fast on their own. Stairs to my left, elevator to my right, elevator is slower and has one exit. There's a service corridor off the east stairwell I walked past twice yesterday without thinking about it.

Her paw touches the receiver.

I go.

---

Warm.

Getting warmer, actually. I notice it now, like a tap left running somewhere. Not alarming. Just present. Slightly more there than it was a moment ago.

I'm comfortable. I think I'm comfortable.

Something pulls at me. A word, maybe. A number.

I don't have it yet.

---

The shelving unit is bolted to the wall, not the floor. I know that now. I didn't know it when I grabbed it. You find these things out by testing them.

It holds my weight going up, which is what matters, and I'm over the top of it before Y'shari has processed that I moved at all.

Down the far side. Door.

The corridor is long and white and straight and I can see the east stairwell from here, which means I'm already running before I've finished registering that I'm running. Not upright. Four points of contact, because upright is slower, and there are footsteps behind me now, two sets, close.

The stairwell door. Through it. Service corridor on the left, unmarked, I find it by feel more than memory. Narrower in here, tighter, a corridor built for pipes and infrastructure, not people, which means I'm faster in it than they are behind me.

I can see the exit. The far end of the service corridor has a door and the door has a window and through the window is outside. Grey sky. Snow on the ground. The launch site sprawling away from the building in every direction.

That's all I need. Outside and I'm into the site, and the site is enormous, and enormous is time, and time is anything. Time is a car. Time is someone who isn't LP Defense looking at me and asking what's wrong.

I hit the door. It opens.

Cold air. Real cold air. Port Jiji winter cold that hits the face like a fact.

First checkpoint behind me, building entrance, already clear. Second checkpoint ahead, the main road crossing, two LP contractors in standard black, both looking the wrong way.

I go low and fast across the crossing and they don't clock me until I'm past them, and then the radio crackle starts, and I don't slow down, because slowing down is not an option.

Third checkpoint. I can see it from here. The perimeter gate. The one that puts me outside the facility, outside their jurisdiction, outside the thing that happens if they catch me before I reach it.

One hundred and fifty meters. Maybe less.

I'm going to make it. My legs know I'm going to make it before my brain does, the rhythm of it, four points, snow and gravel and tarmac, the gate getting larger.

One hundred meters.

Something cold moves up my arm.

Wait. That's wrong. There's nothing on my arm. I'm running. There's nothing.

---

Warm.

Very warm now. Too warm. There's something wrong with the warm.

I'm soaked.

The warm is wet. That's not right. That's not how warm is supposed to feel. Warm is dry and heavy and comfortable, not this, not this thing happening to the outside of me that my body is registering as wet and getting wetter, and the warm has gone somewhere past warm into something with a different name, something my skin is trying to report to me as urgent but the reporting mechanism isn't working right.

I try to move.

I can't move.

I try to open my eyes.

Nothing.

The wet gets worse. The wrong-warm gets worse. My body is telling me something about the outside of itself in terms I don't have access to right now but the gist of it is wrong wrong wrong wrong

---

One hundred meters.

Something strikes me in the arm and I hit the ground, and that's the tarmac outside the second gate, and that's as far as I get before the nothing comes.

I never really made it out, did I.

---

The nothing holds for a long time.

What comes back first is pain, which is at least familiar, and the sensation of having been too hot and not being too hot anymore. Relief. Real air. Something cycling. Cold. Real cold.

Then the arms.

I know the arms. I don't open my eyes yet but I know the arms by the sound and the grip, the same precision I woke up to on day one, and they're doing things to me that I'm choosing not to identify specifically. Medical things. Things that are probably necessary. I let them, because there aren't a lot of alternatives right now and at least someone is doing something.

I open my eyes.

White ceiling. Dorms and medbay. The arms working. The lights at the level they go when it's night on the ship's schedule, which means nothing specific but registers as quiet.

The bed to my left.

I look at it for a long time.

Jinzhou is in it.

Not asleep. In the berth. The one we woke up from, six years into a transit we didn't agree to, the oval cubby that holds you and keeps you and apparently also lets you die in it without anyone knowing. The berth is slid out from the wall and the arms are working on him too, or were, they've settled into a monitoring configuration now. Just present. Just watching.

There's a sealed wound on his left arm that I can see from here. Not from the EVA suit. Wrong location for a suit breach.

Something that happened getting me back inside. Something that happened while I was unconscious and cooking alive on the hull of the ship and couldn't do anything about it or know anything about it or stop him from going out after me.

I look at the ceiling.

Then I look at him again.

Then I try to get up, which I shouldn't do, and find out two things at once. The first is that the arms have an opinion about it. The second is that nothing weighs anything. The strap across my chest is the only thing that agrees with me about where the berth is, and the ceiling is getting politely farther away.

The arms close in, firm and unhurried.

"Rest, Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says, from somewhere farther off than it should be.

Across the gap, the readout on his frame is doing the things readouts are supposed to do.

He's in there. He's going to be fine.

I keep my eyes on the readout for as long as I can. It isn't very long.

## Chapter Eleven, continued: JINZHOU T. KENZO

The altitude was on panel four.

I note this first, because it's the kind of fact that matters later, when someone writes the report, and I intend to be the someone. Panel four. Secondary display. Three hundred and ten meters of drift that I didn't see because I was watching the winch, because Ethyn asked me to watch the winch, because the winch was the problem. All of that is true. None of it is useful.

I take the winch load off my main screen and put the altitude where I can see it. It is worse than three hundred and ten now.

On the external camera, the tank covers are leaving. Not falling. Leaving, one after another, lifting off the housings like they've been called somewhere. Tank 2-E is bare. I watch Ethyn turn toward the airlock.

I watch the locker go.

I don't have a clean way to say what the next two seconds are. The locker leaves the rail. Ethyn is turning. The locker comes down. He folds and doesn't unfold, and the tether takes him, and he swings out and hangs at the end of it, aft starboard, hot side, facing down toward Bejors and the cloud we came here for.

His vitals are still on my screen. Heart rate steady. I look at that for exactly one second, which is the only reason I don't do something stupid in the second after it.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Full climb. All motors. Now."

"Full climb with crew on the external hull exceeds recommended limits."

"I know what it exceeds."

A pause. Brief.

"Full climb engaged."

The ship takes a breath and leans. Weight arrives. Not the centrifuge's easy 1G. This is the engines' heavy paw, one point seven times what I'm used to, settling across my shoulders and staying there. Down is aft now. The hull has a direction again.

Ethyn is down.

The winch will finish without me. The probe will be in the bay in ninety seconds, and ARACCSIM can have that one. I'm already on the ladder.

---

I note the following, in order, as I suit up.

The suit goes on in four minutes twenty seconds, which is forty seconds slower than the checklist minimum, and I don't care. At one point seven gravities it is closer to a wrestling match, and the suit wins the first two rounds.

The tether is rated for two. I clip a second line to the primary before I cycle the airlock.

Ethyn is still on the cameras. Not moving. The suit is intact, no breach indicators. He's just unconscious and hanging and cooking slowly, and none of that is acceptable. I check the thermal rating on his telemetry. Fourteen minutes at current external temperature before his core temperature becomes a medical emergency.

I don't think about what the outside temperature is doing to my own suit. Thinking about it doesn't change it.

The airlock opens.

---

The hull is different in person than on a camera.

I've looked at this hull on screens for almost two weeks. From the external cameras it's a surface. A geometry. A set of distances and angles.

In person it's a ladder. Under thrust the rail runs straight down the side of the ship toward the engines, and I go down it paw over paw while the cloud comes up around me. We're climbing into the layer. The orange goes pale. Visibility drops to the length of my arm, and then to the length of my other arm, and the tether is the only fact that matters.

Cells hit me. The motors are misfiring, and whatever they drop wide ends up on the hull, small, hard, and frequent. I note that they hit me. I decline to count.

Debris is coming down the hull alongside me, the way rain would if rain fell the other direction. A bracket. A length of strut. A cover panel the size of a door goes past, and I turn my shoulder to it, and it takes the outer layer of my suit and the paint off the rail on its way down. I keep going.

"Tank 2-E, breach," ARACCSIM says in my ear. "Auto-seal engaged."

I keep going.

"Seal complete. Ingress interval, four point one seconds. Ambient cloud density at breach, high."

I know what's in the cloud. It's the reason we came. It is now also, I note, in a fuel tank, for four point one seconds, which is long enough.

There's nothing to be done about a tank from the outside of a ship with someone at the end of my tether. I put it in the pile. The pile is large. It has a lot of company.

Ethyn is where the tether says he is.

Hanging at the end of his line. Suit intact. Visor fogged slightly on the inside, which means he's breathing, which matters more than the tether.

I clip my second line to his suit anchor before I do anything else. Then I work his primary tether free from where it's caught under what's left of the locker mounting, clip that to myself too, and start back up.

He's not light.

I note that without complaint, because there's nobody to complain to and no time for it. He's not light, and at one point seven gravities he is the heaviest fact I've ever carried, and I'm dragging him up the side of a ship one grip at a time with the cloud pressing at my visor and the rail ringing under every impact.

Something hits my left arm.

I would call it a slap, if a slap came from a door. The suit says something short and unhappy in my ear, and then, a second later, stops saying it. The arm is heavier. I note that the arm is heavier. The note doesn't go anywhere.

If my existence was trivial before, it means everything now.

I don't know when I started thinking this. Somewhere on the rail, maybe. One paw over the other with Ethyn's weight below me and the cloud around me and the thermal clock on his telemetry going from fourteen to thirteen to twelve. It doesn't matter when. It's true. I keep climbing.

---

The airlock hatch is above me, then level with me, then a thing I am shoving Ethyn through headfirst, which is not how the manual does it. The manual has very little to say about how it's done at one point seven gravities with an arm that won't close. Something catches on the frame as I follow him in. I pull. It comes.

I don't look at what.

The hatch cycles. Through the small window I can see the hull falling aft past the glass, panels and strut and bracket sliding down the side of the ship like a drawer being emptied. Pressure equalizes. The inner door opens.

The cockpit is one ladder up.

I don't remember the ladder. What I remember is the left seat, with Ethyn in it, the harness clicked across his chest, and the thought that the secondary pilot belongs in the secondary pilot's seat. I assume I did the ladder. I note the gap in the record. I'll deal with the gap later.

My seat. The harness. The stick.

"ARACCSIM. Status."

"Climb path degraded. Pulse timing irregular on all motors, worst on motor six. Shield envelope absent. Autopilot assumes a complete hull. Manual insertion recommended."

"We have most of one," I say, and take the stick.

The last ninety seconds are the longest of my life, and I mean that precisely: ninety seconds, measured. The ship wants to pull left. Motor six is running ragged, a cough at the edge of every pulse, and I correct for it, and correct for the correction, and correct again. The cloud thins. Dark comes in around the windscreen, and the last of the orange goes with it. Something loud hits the hull and is gone.

"Insertion burn," ARACCSIM says. "Ten seconds."

I hold it.

The last cell fires clean.

The engines cut. The floor lets go. The weight leaves my shoulders and the harness takes the slack, and everything that was down is simply a direction I used to know.

"Orbit achieved," ARACCSIM says.

I let myself have that for about four seconds.

"Orbit altitude is below the Stephfren line," it continues. "Shield envelope absent. Decay projected within six days. Contingency return is available. It does not require the transfer window. It adds seven months to the transit. It will initiate in sixty minutes unless crew authority is registered."

Ethyn's vitals are on the board. Heart rate steady. Breathing even. Whatever the locker did to him, it's still doing it quietly.

I look at my left arm, because it's the last item on a list I didn't know I was keeping.

There is a length of ship through my forearm. Entry, exit, tidy. The suit has closed around it on both sides, which is the nicest thing the suit has done all day. It has been there since the rail. I know this because the arm has been telling me for twenty minutes, and I haven't been listening.

"Mr. Kenzo," ARACCSIM says. "Suit thermal layer, left forearm, compromised since impact. Exposure exceeded rating by six minutes. Recommend immediate treatment."

"I know."

That's when the adrenaline leaves.

Not gradually. All at once, like a door that's been held open for a long time and finally let go. The pain arrives in the order it's owed. Left arm first. Then everything else.

The transfer window is two hundred and eighty-two days out. I know the number. I put it where I put things.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Hold the return."

"Holding requires continuous crew authority."

"Keep him stable."

"Dr. Vasek is stable."

"Good."

I try to note that.

The note doesn't finish.
