
Warm.

That's the first thing. Just warm, like you are when you've been asleep long enough that the blanket and the body temperature have reached an agreement and everything is fine and there's no reason to be anywhere else.

I'm not anywhere else. I'm here. Wherever here is.

Something moves at the edge of it. A sound, maybe. A voice.

I don't chase it. It'll come back if it matters.

---

Y'shari's office has a plant in the corner I've never paid attention to before. It's doing fine. Someone waters it. I notice it now because I've run out of things to look at that aren't Y'shari's face and the phone on her desk and the door, which is still open, which is the only fact in the room that currently matters to me.

"The Phengoclept breeding data," she says. "All of it. The full cycle research, the containment protocols, the propagation notes. You know it. There's nobody left alive who knows it the way you do."

"I didn't sign anything," I say.

"You signed the facility clearance documents."

"That's not."

"It has a clause."

She reaches for the phone.

I run the calculation in the half second before her paw gets there. Door still open. Guard outside, I heard him in the corridor ten minutes ago, probably still there. Medic somewhere in the building, had to have been pre-positioned, nobody moves that fast on their own. Stairs to my left, elevator to my right, elevator is slower and has one exit. There's a service corridor off the east stairwell I walked past twice yesterday without thinking about it.

Her paw touches the receiver.

I go.

---

Warm.

Getting warmer, actually. I notice it now, like a tap left running somewhere. Not alarming. Just present. Slightly more there than it was a moment ago.

I'm comfortable. I think I'm comfortable.

Something pulls at me. A word, maybe. A number.

I don't have it yet.

---

The shelving unit is bolted to the wall, not the floor. I know that now. I didn't know it when I grabbed it. You find these things out by testing them.

It holds my weight going up, which is what matters, and I'm over the top of it before Y'shari has processed that I moved at all.

Down the far side. Door.

The corridor is long and white and straight and I can see the east stairwell from here, which means I'm already running before I've finished registering that I'm running. Not upright. Four points of contact, because upright is slower, and there are footsteps behind me now, two sets, close.

The stairwell door. Through it. Service corridor on the left, unmarked, I find it by feel more than memory. Narrower in here, tighter, a corridor built for pipes and infrastructure, not people, which means I'm faster in it than they are behind me.

I can see the exit. The far end of the service corridor has a door and the door has a window and through the window is outside. Grey sky. Snow on the ground. The launch site sprawling away from the building in every direction.

That's all I need. Outside and I'm into the site, and the site is enormous, and enormous is time, and time is anything. Time is a car. Time is someone who isn't LP Defense looking at me and asking what's wrong.

I hit the door. It opens.

Cold air. Real cold air. Port Jiji winter cold that hits the face like a fact.

First checkpoint behind me, building entrance, already clear. Second checkpoint ahead, the main road crossing, two LP contractors in standard black, both looking the wrong way.

I go low and fast across the crossing and they don't clock me until I'm past them, and then the radio crackle starts, and I don't slow down, because slowing down is not an option.

Third checkpoint. I can see it from here. The perimeter gate. The one that puts me outside the facility, outside their jurisdiction, outside the thing that happens if they catch me before I reach it.

One hundred and fifty meters. Maybe less.

I'm going to make it. My legs know I'm going to make it before my brain does, the rhythm of it, four points, snow and gravel and tarmac, the gate getting larger.

One hundred meters.

Something cold moves up my arm.

Wait. That's wrong. There's nothing on my arm. I'm running. There's nothing.

---

Warm.

Very warm now. Too warm. There's something wrong with the warm.

I'm soaked.

The warm is wet. That's not right. That's not how warm is supposed to feel. Warm is dry and heavy and comfortable, not this, not this thing happening to the outside of me that my body is registering as wet and getting wetter, and the warm has gone somewhere past warm into something with a different name, something my skin is trying to report to me as urgent but the reporting mechanism isn't working right.

I try to move.

I can't move.

I try to open my eyes.

Nothing.

The wet gets worse. The wrong-warm gets worse. My body is telling me something about the outside of itself in terms I don't have access to right now but the gist of it is wrong wrong wrong wrong

---

One hundred meters.

Something strikes me in the arm and I hit the ground, and that's the tarmac outside the second gate, and that's as far as I get before the nothing comes.

I never really made it out, did I.

---

The nothing holds for a long time.

What comes back first is pain, which is at least familiar, and the sensation of having been too hot and not being too hot anymore. Relief. Real air. Something cycling. Cold. Real cold.

Then the arms.

I know the arms. I don't open my eyes yet but I know the arms by the sound and the grip, the same precision I woke up to on day one, and they're doing things to me that I'm choosing not to identify specifically. Medical things. Things that are probably necessary. I let them, because there aren't a lot of alternatives right now and at least someone is doing something.

I open my eyes.

White ceiling. Dorms and medbay. The arms working. The lights at the level they go when it's night on the ship's schedule, which means nothing specific but registers as quiet.

The bed to my left.

I look at it for a long time.

Jinzhou is in it.

Not asleep. In the berth. The one we woke up from, six years into a transit we didn't agree to, the oval cubby that holds you and keeps you and apparently also lets you die in it without anyone knowing. The berth is slid out from the wall and the arms are working on him too, or were, they've settled into a monitoring configuration now. Just present. Just watching.

There's a sealed wound on his left arm that I can see from here. Not from the EVA suit. Wrong location for a suit breach.

Something that happened getting me back inside. Something that happened while I was unconscious and cooking alive on the hull of the ship and couldn't do anything about it or know anything about it or stop him from going out after me.

I look at the ceiling.

Then I look at him again.

Then I get up, which I shouldn't do, and the arms immediately register an opinion about this, but I get up anyway and cross the two meters between the beds and put my paw on the rim of the cubby.

It's sealed. It's working. The readout on the frame is doing the things readouts are supposed to do.

He's in there. He's fine. He's going to be fine.

I stand there for a while with my paw on the cubby.

## Chapter Eleven, continued: JINZHOU T. KENZO

The cable is stuck at two hundred and ten meters.

I know this because the winch motor is running and the cable counter is not moving and those two things together mean one thing. Something on the hull has it.

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Cable retrieval obstructed. External entanglement at aft starboard secondary boom. Cannot resolve remotely."

Ethyn is already moving before I finish hearing it.

"I'll go," he says.

"Ethyn."

"I'm faster on the hull than you. You manage the winch from here." He's already out of the payload bay. I hear the suit locker.

I watch the external cameras. He comes out of the airlock on the spacescape side, tethered, moving along the hull on all fours, which is what he does when he stops thinking about being upright. Fast and low. He gets to the boom. The cable is wrapped twice around a junction bracket that shouldn't have cable anywhere near it.

He works it free. Methodical. Both paws, unwinding the loop.

The cable counter starts moving again.

"Clear," he says over comms. "Winch it."

I engage the winch. The motor climbs toward the draw that worries me, the extra percentage, but it holds.

Then I see it on the camera.

Port side. The EVA tool maintenance locker. The one bolted to the external storage rail between the engine section and the payload bay. Motor six threw a cell wide eleven minutes ago, close enough to that mounting that ARACCSIM flagged stress, and I logged it as minor.

I was wrong about minor.

It shears off.

It moves along the hull on the remaining rail bracket, slow for a second, then not slow, caught by the differential pressure. And Ethyn is on the wrong side of the ship and he doesn't know it's there, because it's coming from his right and he's looking at the cable counter on his suit display.

I open comms.

Too late.

The locker hits him on the right side. Not a glancing blow. Direct. He folds and goes still and the tether catches him and he hangs there off the hull on the hot side, the atmosphere side, facing down toward Bejors, toward the cloud layer and the heat we've been managing for the last hour.

The EVA suit thermal rating.

I check it.

Fourteen minutes at current external temperature before core temperature becomes a medical emergency.

I'm already standing.

---

I note the following, in order, as I suit up.

The suit goes on in four minutes twenty seconds, which is forty seconds slower than the checklist minimum, and I don't care.

The tether is rated for two. I clip a second line to the primary before I cycle the airlock.

Ethyn is still on the cameras. Not moving. The suit is intact, no breach indicators. He's just unconscious and hanging and cooking slowly and none of that is acceptable.

I don't think about the outside temperature and what it's doing to the suit's thermal system. Thinking about it doesn't change it.

The airlock opens.

---

The hull is different in person than on a camera.

I've looked at this hull on screens for almost two weeks. From the external cameras it's a surface. A geometry. A set of distances and angles.

In person it's enormous, and the gas giant below it is more enormous, and the tether is the only fact that matters, and I follow it paw over paw along the rail toward the aft section.

Ethyn is there. Hanging at the end of his line, suit intact, visor fogged slightly on the inside, which means he's breathing, which is the only thing that matters right now more than the tether.

I clip my second line to his suit anchor before I do anything else. Then I work his primary tether free from where it's caught under the remnant of the locker mounting, clip that to myself too, and start back.

He's not light.

I note that without complaint, because there's nobody to complain to and no time for it and it isn't relevant. He's not light, and I'm dragging him along the hull rail one grip at a time, and the suit is doing something warm on my left arm that I note and ignore.

The airlock is ahead.

I get him to it.

Getting an unconscious person through an airlock in EVA suits is not something I have training for. I find out how to do it by doing it. It takes longer than I want it to. I don't track the time, because tracking the time would require attention I need for the problem.

We're inside. Airlock cycling. Suits coming off.

His is intact. No breach. Thermal damage to the outer layer, surface only.

Mine has a tear on the left forearm. I don't know when. The suit sealed around it.

The arm underneath is not sealed.

I note this and move.

---

The medbay.

I get him there. The arms come down before I've said anything, ARACCSIM already reading the situation, and they take him from me with the same precision they used on day one, and I step back and let them work.

The counter on my suit reads four minutes forty seconds outside. Inside the thermal limit. Outside the comfortable limit. Consistent with the arm.

I sit down on the floor of the medbay and look at the arm.

The wound is clean. Suit sealed it almost immediately. Blood loss is not the problem. The problem is whatever the arm is going to tell me when I hold it up for ARACCSIM to assess, which I do now, because the arms are occupied with Ethyn and I'd like to know what I'm working with.

"Mr. Kenzo," ARACCSIM says.

"Yes."

"Laceration, left forearm. Moderate depth. Recommend immediate closure and monitoring for atmospheric contaminant exposure given breach location."

"Close it," I say.

One arm detaches from Ethyn's monitoring configuration and comes to me. I don't watch it work. I watch Ethyn instead. The arms running their assessments. The readout on his cubby frame doing the things readouts are supposed to do.

He's going to be fine.

I have no data to support this. I believe it anyway, which is unusual for me, and I note the departure from standard practice and decide it's acceptable given the circumstances.

The arm finishes on my forearm and goes back to Ethyn.

I sit on the floor a while longer.

---

If my existence was trivial before, it means everything now.

I don't know when I started thinking this. Somewhere on the hull, maybe. One paw over the other on the tether rail with Ethyn's weight behind me and the gas giant below me and fourteen minutes of thermal rating becoming thirteen and twelve and eleven.

Or maybe in the airlock.

Or maybe right now, sitting on the floor of the medbay with a sealed cut on my arm, watching the readouts confirm that the person in the bed is going to wake up.

It doesn't matter when. It's true.

I get up. I go to the berth on the left. I sit on the edge of it.

The medbay is quiet. The arms work. Ethyn breathes.

I have a laceration that is closed and monitored and not a current emergency. I have a left arm that is going to be a problem in about six hours when the adrenaline is fully gone and the sealed wound starts having opinions about being a sealed wound. I have not slept since before the descent. I have not eaten. My paws are doing something I note as shaking, which I'd prefer they not do, but I don't have a mechanism to stop it that doesn't require sleep and I'm not going to sleep yet.

In four hours the ship's trajectory needs a correction burn to maintain the transfer window math. The correction burn requires someone in the cockpit.

I look at Ethyn. At the readout on his frame. At the arms working their patient, methodical work.

He's not waking up in four hours. The trauma assessment ARACCSIM ran puts him under for twelve minimum. Possibly longer.

I look at the berth I'm sitting on.

Then I look at the cockpit ladder.

Then back at the berth.

The transfer window is two hundred and eighty-two days away now. We did the math. We both did the math. Missing it means indefinite station at Asimov. Indefinite station means two people with three hundred and ninety days of food and no way home.

I program the correction burn into ARACCSIM's autonomous queue. I check it twice. I check it a third time, not because the second check was insufficient but because I want to be sure. ARACCSIM confirms the burn sequence. It'll execute in four hours and fourteen minutes without anyone in the cockpit.

The ship will handle it. The ship has been handling things for eight years. It can handle this.

I get onto the bed.

The arms come down.

"Mr. Kenzo," ARACCSIM says.

"Yes."

"Inducing rest state. Estimated duration, forty-eight to seventy-two hours, pending laceration healing and post-exertion recovery requirements."

"That's fine."

"Dr. Vasek's recovery is progressing within expected parameters."

"I know," I say. "I checked."

A pause.

"Rest well, Mr. Kenzo."

I don't answer that. The arms are already working and the cubby is already warm and the last thing I see before the berth slides home is the readout on Ethyn's frame, doing the things readouts are supposed to do.

That's enough.

I close my eyes.
