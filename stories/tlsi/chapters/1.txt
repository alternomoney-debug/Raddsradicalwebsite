"What is three plus four?"

I chatter my teeth three times. It's the only thing my body will give me, and it doesn't count as an answer. Fair. It's all I've got.

"Incorrect."

I don't try again. Partly because I can't, partly because whoever's asking can wait. I'm doing something important. I don't know what it is, but I'm in the middle of it.

"What is three plus four?"

Same voice, same delivery, down to the half-second hitch before "three," like two recordings glued together with nobody bothering to smooth the seam. It's a computer. A computer is getting under my fur and I'm not even awake yet. Fantastic start. Somebody give this operation an award.

"Augh," I manage. It comes out as a choked screech, a sound I'd apologize for if anyone were around to hear it. The rest of me isn't cleared to speak yet either.

"Incorrect."

It wasn't incorrect. My vocal cords just didn't get the memo. I'd like to file a complaint with whoever's in charge of vocal cords.

I try to open my eyes. Nothing. I try harder. One of them twitches.

Progress. I'll take it.

"What is three plus four?"

"Seven." Cleaner. Still not great.

"Correct. What is your name?"

I open my mouth.

Nothing comes out.

It isn't that I can't speak. My vocal cords and I have reached an understanding. I just don't have an answer, which is a strange thing to learn about yourself. The part of me that knows my name has stepped out for a smoke break.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation required."

Wait.

Something cradles the back of my head, precisely and gently, which I find deeply unsettling. I'm lowered back down.

HISS. THUNK.

I pass out for a while. A few hours, maybe. Rude, honestly, to knock a guy out mid-thought.

---

The second time is easier.

My eyes open without a fight. The ceiling is white and very close, and two robotic arms hang from it. I'm immediately unhappy about them, and I don't know why. Some part of my brain that has lost my name but kept its opinions is looking at those arms and saying no, absolutely not, those are for surgery or dismemberment, and neither is my preferred hobby.

"Paw movement detected," the computer says. "Remain still, Dr." The last word comes out clipped, cut a half-beat short, as if a name was meant to follow it and nothing arrived to fill the gap.

I wasn't aware my paws were moving. Apparently they were, without consulting me. Typical.

The arms come down and remove things from me that I didn't know were attached. It's unpleasant and it feels wrong. They're fast, at least. I'll give them that.

Two tubes remain when they're done. Catheter and butt tube. I have several questions about that and I'm going to ask none of them.

I'm wearing something. I only notice now that the arms have stopped being the most alarming thing in the room. It's translucent and peach-toned, soft plastic that clings without quite touching, with a hood still pulled up over my head like I fell asleep mid-costume party and nobody woke me for the reveal. Little printed markings run down one side. FRONT is stamped near my collar, in case I forget which way I'm supposed to face. Round port covers sit over my chest and thigh, where, presumably, things were plugged into me for longer than I want to think about. Wires still trail off the cuffs toward my socks, thin and pale, disconnected, clearly used to going somewhere important.

Amazing. What a start to a life.

I say it flat, out loud, and it sounds practiced. Apparently that's my word for things going badly. Good to know something about me survived the nap.

I sit up. My arms feel like noodles, though they feel better than they should after a nap that probably ran longer than a year. Which means somebody has been exercising me in my sleep, and I would like to know who, and under what level of consent.

The hood's still up. I push it off without thinking and it falls around my shoulders in a soft translucent puddle. My feet are wrapped in something pale and ribbed that splits my toes apart individually, which strikes me as an aggressively specific design choice for footwear. I decide not to examine that any further.

There are six beds here including mine. Oval, wall-mounted, three on each side of a fairly square, tall room. Five other people. I can't see much of them from here. I don't look too hard.

The arms hover nearby. Watching.

I decide, against all available evidence, that I'm ready to stand up.

I am not ready to stand up.

My legs hold for exactly as long as it takes to notice they're holding, and then the floor arrives a lot faster than I expected. I go down in a heap, one ear folded under me, one arm tangled in a fold of the suit that has chosen this moment to become uncooperative fabric instead of clothing.

A word comes up from somewhere. I send it back down. I couldn't tell you why, only that a voice I can't place would have had something to say about it.

"Body movement detected," the computer says, entirely unbothered that I'm getting acquainted with the floor.

"I noticed," I say, into the floor.

"For your continued safety, please remain on the medical platform until muscle function has been restored."

"That would have been considerably more useful thirty seconds ago," I say, still into the floor.

The arms don't help. I get the sense they've run the numbers and decided I'm not in danger, just embarrassed, and this ceiling treats those as two very different categories.

I lie there a moment longer than necessary, out of spite.

"What is your name?" the computer asks.

I open my mouth.

I don't know.

"Dr.," I try.

"Incorrect. What is your name?"

It feels like a trick question, given the circumstances. I think about it seriously, which is harder than it sounds when your brain is running at maybe thirty percent.

Nothing.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation req..."

"Don't." I say it slowly. "Give me a second."

The computer gives me a second. More than one, actually. I can't tell if that's mercy or a processing delay. I'll take either.

Still nothing.

The arm moves.

"Oh, come on. I would like to formally register a compl-"

HISS. THUNK.

---

Third time. At this point it's less a wake-up and more a recurring appointment.

I wake up and all the tubes are gone. That's a win. I can see better, too. I think I'm wearing glasses.

Why do I know that?

There's a window at the far left end of the room. I missed it the first two times, which tells you how much attention those two times had to spare. It's round, reinforced, edged in the same brushed metal as everything else. It looks dark from here, but not the way a wall is dark.

I get up before I've decided to. My legs handle it worse than my brain does. I cross the room mostly by leaning on beds that aren't mine. The beds don't seem to mind, and nobody's around to judge my technique.

I put a paw on the glass.

Stars.

Not many, and not the kind you see from a hill outside a city with the lights turned down. These have nothing behind them. No air to blur them, no horizon to give them scale, just black and points of white so sharp they look like they'd cut you. There's no haze. It just goes.

I count about two dozen.

I don't know where I am. I don't need the specifics to know it isn't anywhere I've woken up before. Somewhere in my chest, a small unhelpful voice observes: well, that's one way to start a day.

I stand there longer than I should, given I haven't mastered standing.

Nobody else moves. Not the shapes in the other beds, not anything past this window, not anything I can hear. Just me and the reflection of a very confused vulpes in reinforced glass, with two dozen stars behind him.

I count to sixty, to give my brain something to do that isn't panic. Then I count to sixty again.

Something ticks behind me. Metal on metal, small and rhythmic, gone before I can turn to find it.

I don't move. My heart is doing something it doesn't need to be doing this early in being alive again.

It comes again. A footstep, maybe, except it doesn't sound like a footstep. More like something set down carefully and picked back up, over and over, patient and unhurried, from somewhere I can't locate in a room I don't have a floor plan for.

My head feels thick, with a pressure behind the eyes that wasn't there a minute ago. My first coherent thought since waking up is that something is wrong with the air. Whatever's cycling through this room is doing something to me that the computer hasn't mentioned. Slow poisoning dressed up as recovery, my brain shutting down floor by floor while a machine reads me a vocabulary test.

I put a paw on the wall to steady myself. It does nothing, because the wall isn't the unsteady thing.

The sound comes a third time. Closer.

I turn too fast and catch myself on the edge of the nearest bed, and my brain, picking this moment to be useful, supplies an entire imagined obituary in half a second. Something about carbon monoxide. Something about how you're not supposed to feel it happening, how by the time you notice you're already most of the way gone. This is rich, coming from the organ that couldn't find my name.

Then I actually look where the sound is coming from, and it isn't the air, and it isn't my brain shutting down.

Row two, bottom bed. The arms are working on someone down there. Unhooking things. Brisk and indifferent, same as they were with me. I've been listening to it for several minutes without once considering that they might be doing it to somebody who isn't a corpse. In my defense, it has been a very short life so far.

The canine sits up.

Not dramatically. He gets about halfway, stops, considers the situation, then finishes the job. I stand across the room with my heart trying to leave through my throat and think, with total clarity:

Oh. There's another one of us.

He looks over and sees me staring at him like he's a ghost, which, fair, is roughly how I've been treating everyone in this room for the last ten minutes.

"Hi," he says.

It comes out flat and hoarse and entirely unbothered, and it's the best sound I have ever heard in my life.

The arms lower him to the floor when his legs don't hold. He lets them. He ends up flat on his back, staring at the ceiling like there isn't a perfectly good wall he could be looking at instead.

I slide down and sit next to him. My heart takes its time coming back from wherever it went.

Neither of us says anything for a while.

There's a logo on the wall panel beside us. ARGON INDUSTRIES / LSI DIVISION. I watch his paw find it without him looking. Trace the letters once. Stop.

"Why do I remember them," he says. Not a question. Just something that came out.

Something flickers behind his eyes. Whatever it is, he doesn't let it finish.

I get up and offer him a paw. He takes it.

"I'm the engineer, I assume, based on the title I've been called so far." Monotone, the vocal equivalent of a shrug.

"No clue what I am," I say. "Uh. Working on it."

---

The engineer looks at me for a solid minute without saying anything. Then he walks over to a console across the room. One line of amber text on a dark screen.

LSI-ASM&C / STANDBY

He looks at it for a moment.

"We should figure out where we are," he says. He's blunt about everything, built without the option of softening a sentence.

"I was going to do that."

"I know. Hence why I'm saying we should."

He clicks something and starts reading the whole thing out loud.

I appreciate that. My eyes work, but my brain is still negotiating with the rest of me, and negotiations go faster when someone else does the reading.

He starts from the top.

"Long Shot Initiative. Vanguard MK1." He pauses. Just reads it. "Welcome aboard. You are awake."

In the bottom corner of the screen a small notification blinks into existence.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. READY TO SWITCH WITH LSI-ASM&C.

I look at it. Look back at him. He hasn't noticed.

"If you are reading this, your comatose state is over and you're awake. By now you have likely met your crew, had a conversation or two, and are starting to piece things together. This program is here to help with the rest."

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. SWITCHING. PREPARING HANDOVER.

"Below are your crewmates. You probably already know who they are. This is just to confirm it."

He reads it flat. Informational. A man reading a repair manual who happens to find every repair manual useful.

"Your Pilot is a large framed Canis Lupus. Male. Methodical. If he hasn't already assessed every system on this ship since waking up, he will shortly."

I look over at row one. Three beds stacked on the left wall. The one at the top, big framed. Hard to tell much else from here. He hasn't moved. "Shortly" seems to be taking a while.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. HANDOVER IN PROGRESS.

"Your Doctor is a slight Feline Serval. Female. Certified biologist. If something was wrong with any of you since waking up, she has already clocked it."

Middle bed. I can see her from here. She looks like she's sleeping.

They all look like they're sleeping.

"Your Mechanical Engineer is a stocky Canine. Male. Hardware, systems, anything physical on this vessel. Keeps things running. He is probably already annoyed about something. This is normal."

Bottom bed, row one. Stocky. Canine. I'll take their word for it.

"Your Software Engineer is a lean Canine. Male. Quiet. Gets things done without mentioning it."

The engineer stops for half a second.

"Yup, that's me." Then he keeps going without breaking stride. "Your Lead Scientist is a Canid Vulpes. Female. Multiple doctorates. You will know her by the way she talks about the Phengoclept like it personally wronged her."

I push my glasses up my nose, mostly out of solidarity with whoever this woman turns out to be. I already like her.

"Your Secondary Scientist is a near-sighted Amber Vulpes. Male. Mid-twenties. More doctorates than is strictly reasonable for his age. If he has his glasses, he is fine."

The engineer stops reading.

Amber vulpes: check. Glasses: check. Row two: check. The tube situation settles the rest, and I'm not thinking about it any further, out of respect for myself.

Secondary Scientist. All right. Modest ambitions, but I'll take employment I already have over unemployment I don't remember applying for.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. NOW OPERATING LSI VESSEL.

Then nothing. It just sits there humming in the background, like it's been waiting the whole time and has no particular feelings about finally being acknowledged.

The engineer finishes the last two lines.

"By now this should all sound familiar. If it doesn't, give it time. It will come back." A beat. "If you recognise yourself, good. If you recognise someone else, find them."

Neither of us says anything.

"Good luck out there."

I look at row one again. The pilot at the top, the medic in the middle, the other engineer at the bottom. All of them sleeping. And the lead scientist, in the middle bed of the row we just woke up from.

The engineer is already looking at something else. There's a ladder on the far wall, running straight up to a hatch in the ceiling.

I stare at it longer than I should.

I know this ship. I know where that ladder goes. The corridor, the lab, the cockpit. I know all of it without knowing how I know it.

Except it's wrong. The ladder should be on the floor. My brain keeps trying to correct it and keeps failing, filing an appeal with a court that has no jurisdiction here.

He puts his paw on the first rung.

One of the arms drops from the ceiling and taps him on the shoulder. He looks at it. It's holding a toothpaste tube. He takes it without comment, reads the label, and sets it aside for a second.

"I wonder what's to eat," I mumble, before the other arm is already in front of me.

"Eat," says ARACCSIM.

"I'm fine right now! Uh, no, not hungry!"

"Eat."

"I said I'm fine!"

"Eat."

I look at the tube. I look at the arm. The arm doesn't blink, because it doesn't have eyes, which somehow makes it more intimidating. At least a person can be worn down by a good stare.

"Fine."

I take it. The label reads:

VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST
MEAL: 1/3 / DAY: 1

I stare at that for longer than I probably should.

Vasek. E.

I push my glasses up. Squeeze the tube. Something close to gravy and potatoes hits my tongue and my throat. I chug it in about four seconds. I'm not going to pretend it wasn't the best thing I've ever eaten. Admittedly, the sample size is one.

But.

I pass the tube back.

The engineer is already eating his. No ceremony, no argument. Tube up, tube down, tube in his pocket. He reads his label the same way I read mine and doesn't react. Eyes back on the ladder.

"What does yours say," I ask.

He holds it up without looking at me.

KENZO, J. / SOFTWARE ENGINEER
MEAL: 2/3 / DAY: 1

Day one.

We both know what it means. Neither of us says it, which is the correct way to handle information you'd rather not have.

He puts his paw on the first rung again. This time the arm stays put.

I follow him up.

The ladder is about fifteen feet and it takes longer than it should. My arms are fine. My legs are fine. Everything works as it's supposed to. Every rung is a small argument I win by a narrow margin, and I'm counting each win as a personal triumph, because at this point I need them.

He's already through the hatch by the time I reach the top.

---

The lab isn't shaped like a room. It's a shaft. Narrow, tall, going up further than I can see, and down to a metal grate a few feet below me, the kind you'd see rolled over a storefront. Here it's doing double duty as a floor and a door, and I'm not going to think too hard about which one it's supposed to be. Below the grate the shaft continues, research equipment bolted along the walls all the way down. Above me, further up than I'd like to climb without a reason, more shelving, more storage, everything strapped and latched like the builders assumed the whole ship might tip sideways and wanted a fighting chance when it did.

Right in front of me, at whatever currently counts as eye level, are an oven and a sample fridge, both mounted on gimbals that keep them level no matter which way the ship points. Small mercies. At least dinner rotates with the craft.

I pull myself off the ladder and through the hatch, and the room does something to my sense of up and down that I don't have a word for yet.

My brain tries to tell me the ladder should be on the floor again. I tell my brain to relax. My brain has opinions and does not care what I think of them.

He's already at the next ladder. Of course he is.

"Cockpit's up there?" I ask.

"A bit further, but I'll go check."

I look around the lab while he climbs. Microscope. Autoclave. Sample fridge. Oven. I know what everything is and how to use all of it. Useful, and it tells me almost nothing about who I am. I get to keep the skills and lose the person who earned them. That's a very specific kind of unfair.

There's a whiteboard on the far right wall, mounted ninety degrees off, so whatever's written on it runs top to bottom instead of left to right.

"It's locked," he says from up the ladder.

I look up. He's at the hatch, not moving.

"Locked how."

"Says to state my name."

"Can you?"

"Kenzo. J. Kenzo." A pause. "Nope."

"That's a data point," I say. "Try the full thing."

"I don't know the full thing." Another pause, longer.

"So?"

"I can't get in with just my last name."

He climbs back down. No frustration in it, just recalculating. He lands and looks around the lab the same way I did, systematic, left to right.

"Logs," he says.

"Yeah."

We split without discussing it. He goes for the console on the far table. I walk toward the whiteboard.

It's covered in handwriting, all of it running sideways. I tilt my head to read. Not neat, not messy, somewhere in between. Someone wrote this quickly but carefully, like they wanted it to be readable by somebody who wasn't them.

At the top, underlined twice:

Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP

Below that, a diagram. An arc, rising from a point I assume is Nakuet, curving outward and downward toward something labeled ASI-06A. The arc widens at the end like a funnel. Annotations everywhere. Wavelength readings. Density estimates. A note in smaller writing that says not visible, IR detection only, see attached data.

I stare at the diagram.

Something moves in the back of my head.

---

The desk is mine. I know because of the coffee cup in the corner and the stack of papers I've been meaning to sort for three weeks. The screen in front of me is halfway through a document I've been writing longer than I want to admit.

The phone is on speaker.

"So the arc itself," I say, typing. "It's, uh, not moving toward the system or away from it?"

"Neither. Sort of." Her voice is clear and unhurried, the voice of someone who has already had this exact conversation with three other people today and is having it a fourth time as a courtesy. "It is fixed relative to Nakuet. Which is what makes it interesting. Everything else in the system moves. This does not."

"And the Phengoclept is what's causing it."

"The Phengoclept is the arc. Or rather, the arc is what the Phengoclept leaves behind when it migrates. Think of it like the Day-Star rockets. You always see an arc from them."

I type that down. A behaviour. I underline it.

"That's not detailed enough for a study guide."

"It's not detailed enough for me either," she says. "That's why I'm giving you twenty minutes instead of five."

I lean back. Outside my window it's early morning, still grey, heavy snow, same apartments as always, same city that's been slowly, patiently freezing for as long as I've been alive in it.

"Y'shari-line," I say. "That's what they're calling it."

A pause on her end. "I've heard that. I don't love it."

"It's accurate."

"It's my name on something I don't fully understand yet." Flat. Not looking for sympathy, just stating a fact she'd rather not have to state. "I'm going to be the thing people hear when they think of the space dots for the rest of my career, whether I've earned that or not."

I don't push on it. There's a version of me that would, that would poke at the discomfort until it turned into something usable for the book, but I've learned by now that Y'shari answers questions on her own schedule or not at all.

"All right. I'm writing a study guide, not an article about your name. So the, uh, baseline understanding for the book is: someone picks this up and they know nothing. What do I tell them?"

She thinks for a moment. I can hear her thinking. It's the silence that means she's working through it, not stalling.

"Tell them Nakuet is losing something it shouldn't be able to lose. Tell them we found the thing taking it. Tell them we don't know why it's taking it or where it's going or whether it knows we're here."

I type all of that.

"Well, we already know that," I say.

"You know the part I've told you. I know considerably more than that, most of which isn't for a study guide."

That lands somewhere I don't examine too closely. It's not the first time she's drawn a line between what I'm allowed to have and what she actually knows, and it won't be the last. I've never once stopped to ask why she keeps taking these calls at all if there's this much of it I'm not cleared for.

"Not very good for a book," I say instead.

"It's the book you're qualified to write." Not unkind. Just precise. "I have a meeting in a few minutes. I'll catch you around."

The line goes dead.

I'm folding a shirt I don't remember owning into a bag I don't remember packing. The TV is going in the corner with the sound turned low, blue light flickering over the wall, on for the noise more than for anything I'm watching.

"And for anyone just joining us," the anchor says, her voice pitched slightly too bright for whatever's actually on the paper in front of her, "scientists are calling it simply the Y'shari-line, named for the researcher who first identified it two years ago. In plain terms, it's a faint band of light stretching out from Nakuet, and like every other line of its kind that's ever been observed, it's been getting steadily brighter while the star it's attached to gets steadily dimmer."

I keep folding. Half listening, the background kind.

"Today, for the first time, a probe is close enough to actually sample whatever's producing it," the anchor continues, "and in just a few minutes, we're expecting the first images back from its onboard instruments. Scientists have told us this could tell us, for the first time, exactly what's out there."

I stop folding.

Not because of anything she said. Because of the name in the corner of the screen, small, in the guest-expert graphic under a live video feed, a Kanadian research office behind her.

Y'shari.

Same voice I was just on the phone with. Same everything, except now she's not talking to me, she's talking to the entire world, calm, composed, doing the thing where she sounds certain even when I happen to know for a fact she isn't.

"The probe's onboard microscope should give us our first real look," she's saying, to the anchor, to the cameras, to however many people are watching this like I'm halfway watching it. "But let's back up, because I know a lot of viewers are just hearing about this today."

"Please," the anchor says, grateful for the redirect.

"What we've mapped so far is a pattern," Y'shari says, and something in her settles once she's talking about the work instead of performing for a camera, a steadiness I recognize even secondhand through a television speaker. "Every star we've observed with a line like this shows the same behavior. The line appears, the star dims a little, and then the line gets brighter and the star dims a little more. Every one of them. We haven't found a single one that stopped."

"And where is that," the anchor asks.

"In our own system, the line runs from Nakuet toward the second planet out, Aluk'. Hot, thick atmosphere, exactly where something might go to breed." She pauses, choosing the next part carefully. "That's the working theory, at least. Something is feeding on Nakuet's output, converting it, storing it, and eventually carrying it off to reproduce there. What we're hoping this probe tells us, for the first time, is what that something actually looks like up close."

"And the concern," the anchor says, "for people watching at home."

"The concern," Y'shari says, "is that ours has been doing exactly what all the others did for two years now, which either means we're earlier in the curve than the models say, or."

She doesn't finish the sentence.

She stops.

Somewhere off camera, not quite quiet enough for the microphone to miss it, a voice says something that isn't meant for broadcast, a short sharp "oh, come on," followed by a second voice, younger and considerably less composed, blurting out something about the number on a screen nobody at home can see. Neither of them is talking to Y'shari. Neither of them seems to remember there's a live feed running.

It isn't a dramatic stop. Not one built for television. Something behind her attention shifts, off camera, a monitor or a feed I can't see, whatever's coming through on the other side of that microscope. In about half a second it takes the composure right off her face and replaces it with something I don't have a name for yet and will spend a very long time trying to find one for.

"I'm sorry," she says, already half turned from the camera, "I have somewhere else I need to be."

The feed cuts back to the anchor, faintly confused, already moving on, already treating it like a minor technical hiccup instead of whatever it actually was.

I stand there with a shirt in my paws, listening to a stranger apologize for the abrupt cut. Something in my chest that I don't have words for either sits there being uneasy about it for a lot longer than the segment lasted.

I go back to packing.

---

I wake up, blinking.

Mr. Kenzo is standing right next to me. I don't know when he got there.

"You went somewhere," he says.

I look at the whiteboard. The diagram. The arc rising from Nakuet toward ASI-06A.

"I know what this is," I say. "I think."

He waits.

"I don't know enough of it. But I know what it is. I was writing about it. Before. Someone called me to talk about it, and, uh. I was writing a book."

"Do you remember the call?"

"Most of it."

"Your name."

I look at him.

"She was about to say it. Something with an E?"

Mr. Kenzo looks at the whiteboard. Then back at me.

"We know," he says. "The tube."

I already know. I just hadn't connected it yet. Vasek. E.

"E. Even? Eith? Eshan. Ethyn." They all come out strange, like trying on coats I haven't worn in years and finding one that still fits. "Ethyn Vasek."

He nods once.

"Hope you remember mine soon," he says.

Neither of us says anything for a moment.

"The cockpit," I say.

"Yes."

---

The ladder is easier the second time. Not easy. Easier.

He's ahead of me again. I don't rush it. My legs are getting the idea.

The hatch at the top is the same one that turned him away twenty minutes ago. He stops below it and looks at me. I climb past him, which is a strange thing to do on a ladder, but he moves aside without being asked.

I get to the top and look at the panel beside the hatch.

IDENTIFY TO PROCEED.

"Ethyn Vasek," I say.

A pause. Longer than I'd like.

Then the AI says:

"Dr. Vasek."

ARACCSIM. It's the first time it's said anything to either of us that wasn't a number, a vital sign, or an order to eat. The name comes out strange, pulled from somewhere else in whatever library this thing keeps, a flat statement-voice dropped into a sentence built for questions. Like someone recorded my surname once, in one register, and the machine is stuck using that clip no matter what it's trying to say with it. Its voice is smooth and slightly synthetic otherwise, with the quality of something very capable that has no intention of letting you forget it. "Identity confirmed. Who is your co-pilot? Please state their name."

The hatch doesn't click.

I look down at him. Kenzo looks up at me.

"That's, uh. Weird," I say.

"It knows us, but we don't know ourselves," he says.

"Apparently."

I push on the hatch.

"Can you open the hatch? I trust him," I say. "The engineer. Engineer Kenzo."

"Mr. Kenzo's identity is partially confirmed. First name required."

I look back down. He's still there.

"It needs your first name," I say.

A pause from below.

"I don't remember it."

I look at the screen next to the door.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "What's the software engineer's first name?"

"That information is available to confirmed crew only."

"I am confirmed crew."

A pause.

"Jinzhou," it says. "Mr. Jinzhou T. Kenzo."

I look back down the ladder at Kenzo. At Jinzhou. He's looking up at me with an expression that suggests he already suspected this would go well.

"Jinzhou," I say.

He tries it.

"Jinzhou Kenzo."

The hatch clicks. I climb up first and take the left seat. He climbs through and takes the right.

The console in front of me is not, it turns out, entirely in a language I can read.

Most of it tracks. Screens, readouts, things labeled in the same plain lettering as everything else on this ship. But a strip of switches runs along the lower edge of my side of the panel, worn smooth at the edges like they get used constantly, and the text stenciled above them isn't anything I recognize. Tall narrow characters, an unfamiliar rhythm to the letters, though I can tell they're letters and not symbols. Latin-derived, that much is certain. More out of habit than expectation, I mouth the words.

It doesn't mean anything. It's a sound with no content behind it.

Jinzhou glances over, follows where I'm looking, and doesn't say anything about it. I notice that, without knowing yet why it matters.

"What is the purpose of this mission," ARACCSIM asks.

I open my mouth. Close it. Something surfaces, slow and certain, like the name did.

"To stop Nakuet from dimming," I say. "To save Wask-ki from freezing."

A pause. Longer than the question should need.

"Partial confirm. Dr. Vasek, you are cleared as secondary pilot. Do you vouch for Mr. Kenzo as co-pilot." Then nothing. No countdown, no prompt, nothing to fill the gap, only the patient quiet of something built to stop talking once it's asked a real question and wait however long it takes for a real answer.

"Yes," I say.

A beat, and then: "Jinzhou T. Kenzo." His full name arrives in a different register from the sentence around it, warmer, slightly higher, as if it was pulled from a separate recording made on a better day by someone in a better mood and grafted on before the machine remembered to go flat again. "Co-pilot access granted. Welcome aboard, you two."

He moves through the screens without comment on ARACCSIM, which I've already decided has a personality, and a slightly smug one at that.

"Talking to this thing is like talking to a god damn Spell'n'Speak," I say. "I hate it."

"It can hear you."

"I'm aware. I'd like it noted that I was polite about it."

"Noted," ARACCSIM says, in what I'm fairly sure is the voice of a different, much friendlier machine.

Neither of us says anything for a moment. The screens scroll quietly through what I can only imagine are star maps, system readouts, trajectory data, a zoomed image of something I assume is Nakuet in the upper left corner. None of it is in English.

I look at it.

Then I look at it properly.

"Pass me a, uh... darn, what's it called. A stylus. Pass me a stylus," I say.

Jinzhou looks around, finds a stylus in a slot in his armrest, and passes it over. I use it to work the screens, trying to figure out where we are.

"What are you doing," he says.

"Checking, uh, something."

"What."

"Give me a second. Hm."

I switch the screen to show the star we're orbiting. There are spots on its surface, darker patches moving slowly across the face. I time them. Watch them cross. Check again.

The numbers seem familiar but are still wrong.

"Rotation period," I say. "What's Nakuet's rotation period."

Jinzhou pulls up a secondary screen. Reads it. "Twenty-seven, twenty-five days, give or take."

I look at my numbers. Look at the spots.

"That star's rotation period is about, mmn, thirty-two days."

Different number. Different star. I'd like that to be a typo.

Possibility one: I've forgotten how to divide. I check. I haven't, which is the worst news I've had all day, and the day has had competition. Possibility two goes to later-Ethyn, who can deal with it.

A loud gulp. Mine, apparently. I hadn't planned that.

I pull up the navigation screen. The star map is centered on a point labeled ASI-06A. Asimov-06-A, sitting right in the middle, with a trajectory line running from somewhere far to the left all the way to where we are now.

There's a label in the top right corner too, and I get about half a second of hope that it's going to tell me something before I realize it's in the same unreadable lettering as the switches.

"Yeah, no. Shoot. I can't read that," I say, to nobody in particular, since the screen and I are apparently not on speaking terms.

"Jinzhou. Read that."

He looks at it for a second, longer than it should take someone who actually knows what it says.

"Current system," he says, slow, like he's translating it as much for himself as for me. "Asimov. Distance from Nakuet system. Six point four light years."

"Okay, uh. I don't believe you," I say, to him now instead of the screen.

Jinzhou says nothing else. He just watches me do the math. I do it twice, in case the first time was a mood.

I pull up the stellar readout for the star on screen. All the details needed.

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Dr. Vasek."

"What system are we in?"

"Stephfren Asimov Memorial System, designation ASI-06A. You are currently inbound within the Asimov system. The approximate time to Asimov-09D is about one week."

I look at Jinzhou.

He looks at me.

"This isn't the Nakuet system," I say. "It really isn't."

"No," he says. "It is not."

"Amazing," I say.

*END OF CHAPTER ONE*
