"What is three plus four?"

I chatter my teeth three times. It's the only thing my body will give me, and it doesn't count as an answer. Fair. It's all I've got.

"Incorrect."

I don't try again. Partly because I can't, partly because whoever's asking can wait. I'm doing something important. I don't know what it is, but I'm in the middle of it.

"What is three plus four?"

Same voice, same delivery, down to the half-second hitch before "three," like two recordings glued together by someone who had somewhere better to be. It's a computer. A computer is getting under my fur and I'm not even awake yet. Fantastic start. Somebody give this operation an award.

"Augh," I manage. It comes out as a choked screech, a sound I'd apologize for if anyone were around to hear it.

"Incorrect."

It wasn't incorrect. My vocal cords just didn't get the memo. I'd like to file a complaint with whoever's in charge of vocal cords. I suspect it's me, which complicates the paperwork.

I try to open my eyes. Nothing. I try harder. One of them twitches.

Progress. I'll take it.

"What is three plus four?"

"Seven." Cleaner. Still not great.

"Correct. What is your name?"

I open my mouth.

Nothing comes out.

It isn't that I can't speak. My vocal cords and I have reached an understanding. I just don't have an answer, which is a strange thing to learn about yourself. The part of me that knows my name has stepped out for a smoke break and taken the keys.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation required."

Wait.

Something cradles the back of my head, precisely and gently, which I find deeply unsettling. Nothing that gentle has ever wanted anything good from me. I'm lowered back down.

HISS. THUNK.

I pass out for a while. Rude, honestly, to knock a guy out mid-thought.

---

The second time is easier.

My eyes open without a fight. The ceiling is white and very close, and two robotic arms hang from it. I'm immediately unhappy about them, and I don't know why. Some part of my brain that has lost my name but kept all of its opinions is looking at those arms and saying no, absolutely not, those are for surgery or dismemberment, and neither is my preferred hobby.

"Paw movement detected," the computer says. "Remain still, Dr." The last word comes out clipped, cut a half-beat short, as if a name was meant to follow it and nothing arrived to fill the gap.

I wasn't aware my paws were moving. Apparently they were, without consulting me. Typical. Even my paws don't tell me things.

The arms come down and remove things from me that I didn't know were attached. It's unpleasant and it feels wrong. They're fast, at least. I'll give them that, and nothing else.

Two tubes remain when they're done. Catheter and butt tube. I have several questions about that and I'm going to ask none of them. Some questions you take to your grave. Ideally a distant one.

I'm wearing something. I only notice now that the arms have stopped being the most alarming thing in the room. It's translucent and peach-toned, soft plastic that clings without quite touching, with a hood still pulled up over my head like I fell asleep mid-costume party and nobody woke me for the reveal. FRONT is stamped near my collar, in case I forget which way I'm supposed to face. Round port covers sit over my chest and thigh, where, presumably, things were plugged into me for longer than I want to think about. Wires trail off the cuffs toward my socks, thin and pale and disconnected, clearly used to going somewhere important.

Amazing. What a start to a life.

I say it flat, out loud, and it sounds practiced. Apparently that's my word for things going badly. Good to know something about me survived the nap.

I sit up. My arms feel like noodles, though they feel better than they should after a nap that probably ran longer than a year. Which means somebody has been exercising me in my sleep, and I would like to know who, and under what level of consent.

The hood's still up. I push it off and it falls around my shoulders in a soft translucent puddle. My feet are wrapped in something pale and ribbed that splits my toes apart individually, which strikes me as an aggressively specific design choice for footwear. Someone sat in a meeting and pitched toe socks for a spaceship, and someone else said yes. I'd like to meet both of them. Separately.

Wait. Spaceship? I don't know that. I don't know anything. My brain has started guessing out loud and I haven't approved any of it.

There are six beds here including mine. Oval, sitting in cubbies set into the wall, three on each side of a fairly square, tall room. Five other people. I can't see much of them from here. I don't look too hard.

The arms hover nearby. Watching.

I decide, against all available evidence, that I'm ready to stand up.

I am not ready to stand up.

My legs hold for exactly as long as it takes to notice they're holding, and then the floor arrives a lot faster than I expected. I go down in a heap, one ear folded under me, one arm tangled in a fold of the suit that has chosen this moment to become uncooperative fabric instead of clothing.

A word comes up from somewhere. I send it back down. I couldn't tell you why, only that a voice I can't place would have had something to say about it.

"Body movement detected," the computer says, entirely unbothered that I'm getting acquainted with the floor.

"I noticed," I say, into the floor.

"For your continued safety, please remain on the medical platform until muscle function has been restored."

"That would have been considerably more useful thirty seconds ago," I say, still into the floor.

The arms don't help. I get the sense they've run the numbers and decided I'm not in danger, just embarrassed, and this ceiling treats those as two very different categories.

I lie there a moment longer than necessary, out of spite.

"What is your name?" the computer asks.

I open my mouth.

I don't know.

"Dr.," I try. It's what it called me. It seems like a safe bet.

"Incorrect. What is your name?"

It feels like a trick question, given the circumstances. I think about it seriously, which is harder than it sounds when your brain is running at maybe thirty percent and the other seventy percent is still in bed.

Nothing.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation req-"

"Don't." I say it slowly. "Give me a second."

The computer gives me a second. More than one, actually. I can't tell if that's mercy or a processing delay. I'll take either.

Still nothing.

The arm moves.

"Oh, come on. I would like to formally register a compl-"

HISS. THUNK.

---

Third time. At this point it's less a wake-up and more a recurring appointment.

I wake up and all the tubes are gone. That's a win. My dignity is back where it belongs, roughly. I can see better, too. I think I'm wearing glasses.

Why do I know that?

I touch them. Wire frames, a little crooked. My paw knows exactly where they sit and exactly how to push them up, and it does, twice, without asking me. So I'm a guy who pushes his glasses up. That's one fact. I'll build the rest of a person around it later.

There's a window at the far end of the room. I missed it the first two times, which tells you how much attention those two times had to spare. It's round, reinforced, edged in the same brushed metal as everything else. It looks dark from here, but not the way a wall is dark.

I get up before I've decided to. My legs handle it worse than my brain does. I cross the room mostly by leaning on beds that aren't mine. The beds don't seem to mind, and nobody's around to judge my technique.

I put a paw on the glass.

Stars.

Not many, and not the kind you see from a hill outside a city with the lights turned down. These have nothing behind them. No air to blur them, no horizon to give them scale, just black and points of white so sharp they look like they'd cut you. There's no haze. It just goes.

I count about two dozen.

So that's settled, then. My brain was right about the spaceship. I'd like it to know that I'm not happy for it.

I stand there longer than I should, given I haven't mastered standing. Just me and the reflection of a very confused vulpes in reinforced glass, with two dozen stars behind him. He looks like he could use a nap. He's had about a year of one.

I count to sixty, to give my brain something to do that isn't panic. Then I count to sixty again.

Nothing else moves. Not past the window. Not in the room. The arms have folded themselves up against the ceiling like they're done with me for now, and the five beds behind me haven't made a sound since I woke up.

Not one sound.

Not a cough, not a sheet shifting, not a single snore, and I refuse to believe that out of five people, nobody snores.

I turn around slowly, and it occurs to me, for the first time, that I am the only one standing.

*END OF CHAPTER ONE*
