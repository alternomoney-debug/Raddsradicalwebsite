"What is three plus four?"

I chatter my teeth three times. It's the only thing my body's willing to offer up right now, and it doesn't seem to count as an answer, which, fair, I suppose, but it's genuinely all I've got.

"Incorrect."

I don't answer properly. Partly because I can't, partly because whoever's asking can wait. I hope. I'm doing something important. I don't know what it is but I'm doing it.

"What is three plus four?"

Same voice. Exact same delivery, down to the half-second hitch right before the number three, like the sentence was built out of two different recordings glued together and nobody bothered smoothing the seam. Computer. A computer is getting under my fur before I'm even conscious. Fantastic start. Truly. Somebody give this operation an award.

"Augh," I manage, which comes out as more of a choked, distressed screech than an actual word, a sound I'd apologize for if anyone were around to hear it. Seems the rest of me isn't cleared to speak yet either.

"Incorrect."

It wasn't incorrect. My vocal cords just didn't get the message yet. I'd like to file a complaint with whoever's in charge of vocal cords.

I try to open my eyes. Nothing happens. I try harder. One of them twitches.

Progress. I'll take it.

"What is three plus four?"

"Seven." Cleaner this time. Still not great.

"Correct. What is your name?"

I open my mouth.

Nothing comes out.

Not because I can't speak. I can speak, apparently, my vocal cords and I have just reached an understanding. I just don't have an answer. Which is a genuinely strange thing to discover about yourself, that the part of you that knows your own name has stepped out for a smoke break.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation required."

Wait.

Something cradles the back of my head with a precision that I find deeply unsettling. I'm lowered back down.

HISS. THUNK.

I pass out for a while. Maybe a few hours. Rude, honestly, to knock a guy out mid-thought.

---

The second time is easier.

My eyes open without a fight. The ceiling is white and very close and has two robotic arms hanging from it that I am immediately unhappy about. I don't know why I'm unhappy about them specifically. Something primal, maybe. Some part of my brain that hasn't forgotten its own name is looking at those arms and going, no, absolutely not, those are bad, those are for surgery or dismemberment and neither of those is my preferred hobby.

"Paw movement detected," the computer says. "Remain still, Dr." The last word comes out clipped, cut a half-beat short, like there was meant to be a name slotted in after it and nothing arrived to fill the space.

I was not aware my paws were moving. Apparently they had, though, without consulting me first. Typical.

The arms come down. They remove things from me that I didn't know were attached to me, which is unpleasant. Felt wrong. They work fast at least. I'll give them that.

Two tubes remain when they're done. Catheter and butt tube.

I'm wearing something. I only notice it now that the arms have stopped being the most alarming thing in the room. Translucent, peach-toned, some kind of soft plastic that clings without actually touching, a hood still pulled up over my head like I fell asleep mid-costume-party and nobody bothered waking me for the reveal. Little printed markings run down one side, FRONT stamped somewhere near my collar in case I ever forget which way I'm supposed to be facing, small round port covers over my chest and thigh where, presumably, things were plugged into me for longer than I want to think about. Wires still trail off the cuffs and down toward my socks, thin and pale, disconnected now but clearly used to going somewhere important.

Amazing. Genuinely. What a start to a life.

I sit up. My arms feel like noodles. Granted they feel better than they should for a nap that probably took longer than a year.

The hood's still up. I reach back and push it off without thinking about it, more instinct than decision, and it falls around my shoulders in a soft translucent puddle. My feet, when I look down at them, are wrapped in something pale and ribbed that splits my toes apart individually, which strikes me as an aggressively specific design choice for footwear, and I decide not to examine that thought any further right now.

There are six beds here including mine. Oval, wall mounted, three on both sides of this fairly square, tall room. Five other people. I can't see much of them from here. I don't look too hard.

The arms hover nearby. Watching.

I decide, against every available piece of evidence, that I am ready to stand up.

I am not ready to stand up.

My legs hold for exactly as long as it takes to register that they're holding, which is to say not very long at all, and then the floor arrives considerably faster than I expected the floor to arrive. I go down in a heap, one ear folded flat under me, one arm still tangled in a fold of the suit that apparently decided now was the moment to become uncooperative fabric instead of clothing.

"Body movement detected," the computer says, entirely unbothered by the fact that I am currently becoming acquainted with the floor.

"I noticed," I say, into the floor.

"For your continued safety, please remain on the medical platform until muscle function has been restored."

"That information," I say, still into the floor, "would have been considerably more useful thirty seconds ago."

The arms don't move to help. I get the distinct sense they've already run this exact calculation and determined I'm not actually in danger, just embarrassed, which are apparently two very different categories as far as this ship's ceiling is concerned.

I lie there for a moment longer than strictly necessary, mostly out of spite.

"What is your name?" the computer asks.

I open my mouth.

I genuinely do not know.

"Dr.," I try.

"Incorrect. What is your name?"

This whole thing feels like a trick question given the circumstances. I think about it seriously for a moment, which is harder than it sounds when your brain is apparently running on maybe thirty percent of its usual capacity.

Nothing.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation req..."

"Don't." I say it slowly. "Give me a second."

The computer gives me a second. More than one, actually. I'm not sure if that's mercy or a processing delay. Either way I'll take it.

Still nothing.

The arm moves.

"Oh come on."

HISS. THUNK.

---

Third time.

I wake up again, and all of the tubes are gone. That's a win. I also can see better than before. I think I have glasses on.

Why do I know that.

There's a window at the far left end of the room. I didn't notice it the first two times, which tells you something about how much of my attention those two times actually had to spare. It's round, reinforced, edged in the same brushed metal as everything else here. It looks dark from where I'm sitting, but not the way a wall is dark.

I get up before I've decided to. My legs handle it worse than my brain does. I make it across the room mostly by leaning on beds that aren't mine, which I've decided is fine, since the beds don't seem to mind and nobody's around to judge my technique.

I put a paw on the glass.

Stars.

Not very many, nor the kind you see from a hill outside a city with the lights turned down. The kind with nothing behind them. No air to blur them, no horizon to give them scale, just black and points of white so sharp they look like they'd cut you if you got close enough. There's no gradient to it. No haze. It just goes.

I only count about two dozen.

I don't know where I am yet. I don't need to know the specifics to know, with total physical certainty, that it isn't anywhere I've woken up before. Somewhere in my chest, a small, unhelpful voice observes: well, that's certainly one way to start a day.

I stand there longer than I should, given that standing is still a thing I haven't fully mastered.

Nobody else moves. Not the shapes still lying in the other beds, not anything past this window, not anything I can hear. Just me and the reflection of a very confused vulpes in a piece of reinforced glass with two dozen stars behind it.

I count to sixty, mostly to have something to do with my brain that isn't panicking. Then I count to sixty again.

Something ticks, somewhere behind me. Metal against metal, small, rhythmic, gone before I've fully turned to find it.

I don't move for a second. My heart's doing something it doesn't need to be doing this early into being alive again.

It comes again. A footstep, maybe, except it doesn't sound like a footstep exactly, more like something being set down carefully and then picked back up, over and over, patient, unhurried, from somewhere I can't locate in a room I don't have a floor plan for yet.

My head feels strange. Thick, almost, a pressure behind the eyes that wasn't there a minute ago, and my first genuinely coherent thought since waking up is that something is wrong with the air in here, that whatever's cycling through this room is doing something to me that the computer hasn't bothered mentioning, slow poisoning dressed up as recovery, my own brain quietly shutting down floor by floor while a machine reads me vocabulary tests.

I put a paw against the wall to steady myself, which does nothing, because the wall isn't the thing that's unsteady.

The sound comes a third time. Closer.

I turn too fast, catch myself on the edge of the nearest bed, and my brain, apparently deciding this is the moment to be genuinely useful for once, supplies an entire imagined obituary in about half a second, something about carbon monoxide, something about how you're not supposed to feel it happening, something about how by the time you notice you're already most of the way gone.

Then I actually look at where the sound is coming from, and it isn't the air, and it isn't my brain shutting down floor by floor.

It's row two, bottom bed. The arms are working on someone down there. Unhooking things. Moving with the same brisk indifference they used on me, which I now realize I've been hearing for the last several minutes without once considering that they might be doing it to somebody who isn't a corpse.

The canine sits up.

Not dramatically. Not all at once. He gets about halfway there, stops, considers the situation, and then finishes the job, and I stand across the room with my heart trying to leave through my throat and think, with total clarity:

Oh. There's another one of us.

He looks over. Sees me standing there staring at him like he's a ghost, which, in fairness, is roughly what I'd been treating everyone in this room as for the last ten minutes.

"Hi," he says.

It comes out flat, and slightly hoarse, and entirely unbothered, and it is the single best sound I have ever heard in my life.

The arms lower him down to the floor when his legs don't hold. He lets them. Ends up flat on his back, staring at the ceiling like there isn't a wall right there he could be looking at instead.

I slide down and sit next to him. My heart takes its time coming back down from wherever it went.

Neither of us says anything for a while.

There's a logo on the wall panel beside us. ARGON INDUSTRIES / LSI DIVISION. I watch his paw find it without him looking. Traces the letters once. Stops.

"Why do I remember them," he says. Not a question. Just a thing that came out.

Something flickers behind his eyes. Whatever it is, he doesn't let it finish.

I get up. Offer a hand to the mystery person. He takes it.

"I'm the engineer, I assume, based on the title I've been called so far." He says it in a very monotone voice, the vocal equivalent of a shrug.

"No clue what I am. Working on it."

---

The engineer looks at me for a solid minute but says nothing, then walks over to a console across the room. One line of amber text on a dark screen.

LSI-ASM&C / STANDBY

The engineer looks at it for a moment.

"We should actually figure out where we are," he says. He seems very blunt about everything, the way some people are just built without the option for softening a sentence.

"I was going to do that."

"I know. Hence why I'm saying we should."

He looks over to the console, clicks on something, and starts reading the whole thing out loud.

I appreciate that. My eyes work but my brain is still negotiating with the rest of me, and negotiations, I've decided, go faster when someone else does the reading.

The engineer starts from the top.

"Long Shot Initiative. Vanguard MK1." He pauses. Just reads it. "Welcome aboard. You are awake."

In the bottom corner of the screen a small notification blinks into existence.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. READY TO SWITCH WITH LSI-ASM&C.

I look at it. Look back at him. He hasn't noticed.

"If you are reading this, your comatose state is over and you're awake. By now you have likely met your crew, had a conversation or two, and are starting to piece things together. This program is here to help with the rest."

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. SWITCHING. PREPARING HANDOVER.

"Below are your crewmates. You probably already know who they are. This is just to confirm it."

He reads it. Flat. Informational. A man reading a repair manual who happens to find every repair manual useful.

"Your Pilot is a large framed Canis Lupus. Male. Methodical. If he hasn't already assessed every system on this ship since waking up, he will shortly."

I look over at row one. Three beds stacked on the left wall. The one at the top, big framed. Hard to tell much else from here.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. HANDOVER IN PROGRESS.

"Your Doctor is a slight Feline Serval. Female. Certified biologist. If something was wrong with any of you since waking up, she has already clocked it."

Middle bed. I can see her from here. She looks like she's sleeping.

They all look like they're sleeping.

"Your Mechanical Engineer is a stocky Canine. Male. Hardware, systems, anything physical on this vessel. Keeps things running. He is probably already annoyed about something. This is normal."

Bottom bed, row one. Stocky. Canine. I'll take their word for it.

"Your Software Engineer is a lean Canine. Male. Quiet. Gets things done without mentioning it."

The engineer stops for half a second.

"Yup, that's me." Then he keeps going without breaking stride. "Your Lead Scientist is a Canid Vulpes. Female. Multiple doctorates. You will know her by the way she talks about the Phengoclept like it personally wronged her."

I push my glasses up my nose, mostly out of solidarity for whoever this woman turns out to be. I already like her.

"Your Secondary Scientist is a near-sighted Amber Vulpes. Male. Mid-twenties. More doctorates than is strictly reasonable for his age. If he has his glasses, he is fine."

The engineer stops reading.

I'm an Amber Vulpes. I have glasses. I woke up in row two. The suit and its little port covers tell me the rest of what I need to know about the tube situation, which I am not thinking about any further, out of respect for myself.

Secondary Scientist. Alright. That works. Modest ambitions, but I'll take employment I apparently already have over unemployment I definitely don't remember applying for.

A.R.A.C.C.S.I.M. NOW OPERATING LSI VESSEL.

Then nothing. Just sits there humming in the background like it's been waiting the whole time and has no particular feelings about finally being acknowledged.

The engineer finishes the last two lines.

"By now this should all sound familiar. If it doesn't, give it time. It will come back." A beat. "If you recognise yourself, good. If you recognise someone else, find them."

Neither of us says anything.

"Good luck out there."

I look at row one again. The pilot at the top, the medic sleeping in the middle, the other engineer at the bottom. Sleeping. All of them are just sleeping in row one. And the lead scientist in the middle bed of the row we just woke up from.

The engineer is already looking at something else. There's a ladder on the far wall, running straight up to a hatch in the ceiling.

I stare at it longer than I should.

I know this ship. I know where that ladder goes. The corridor, the lab, the cockpit. I know all of it without knowing how I know it.

Except it's wrong. The ladder should be on the floor. My brain keeps trying to correct it and keeps failing, filing an appeal with a court that has no jurisdiction here.

He puts his paw on the first rung.

One of the arms drops from the ceiling and taps him on the shoulder. He looks at it. It's holding a toothpaste tube. He takes it without comment, reads the label, and sets it aside for a second.

"I wonder what's to eat," I mumble, before the other arm is already in front of me.

"Eat," says ARACCSIM.

"I'm fine right now! Uh, no, not hungry!"

"Eat."

"I said I'm fine!"

"Eat."

I look at the tube. I look at the arm. The arm does not blink because it doesn't have eyes, which somehow makes it more intimidating, not less. At least a person could be worn down by a good stare.

"Fine."

I take it. The label reads:

VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST
MEAL: 1/3 / DAY: 1

I stare at that for longer than I probably should.

Vasek. E.

I push my glasses up. Squeeze the tube. Something akin to gravy and potatoes hits my taste buds and my throat. I chug it in about four seconds. I'm not going to pretend it wasn't the best thing I've ever eaten.

But.

I hand the tube back.

The engineer is already eating his. No ceremony, no argument. Tube up, tube down, tube in his pocket. I watch him read his label the same way I read mine. He doesn't react. Files it away somewhere and looks back at the ladder.

"What does yours say," I ask.

He holds it up without looking at me.

KENZO, J. / SOFTWARE ENGINEER
MEAL: 2/3 / DAY: 1

Day one.

We both already know what it means, and neither of us says it, which I've decided is the correct way to handle information you'd rather not have.

He puts his paw on the first rung again. This time the arm stays put.

I follow him up.

The ladder is about fifteen feet. It takes longer than it should. My arms are fine. My legs are fine. Everything works the way it's supposed to. Every rung is a small argument I win by a narrow margin, and I'm choosing to interpret each victory as a personal triumph, because at this point I need the wins.

He's already through the hatch by the time I reach the top.

---

The lab isn't shaped like a room. It's a shaft. Narrow, tall, going up further than I can see clearly right now and down to a metal grate a few feet below me, the kind of thing you'd see rolling down over a storefront somewhere, except here it's doing double duty as a floor and a door, and I've decided not to think too hard about which one it's actually supposed to be. Below the grate, more of the shaft continues, research equipment bolted along the walls all the way down. Above me, further up than I'd like to climb without a good reason, more shelving, more storage, everything strapped and latched like whoever built this place assumed the whole ship might tip sideways at any moment and wanted a fighting chance against gravity when it did.

Right in front of me, at whatever currently counts as eye level, an oven and a sample fridge, both mounted on some kind of gimbal rig that keeps them level no matter which way the ship's pointing. Small mercies. At least dinner rotates with the craft.

I pull myself off the ladder and through the hatch, and the room does something to my sense of up and down that I don't have a word for yet.

My brain tries to tell me the ladder should be on the floor again. I tell my brain to relax. My brain does not relax. My brain, it turns out, has opinions of its own and does not care what I think about them.

He's already at the next ladder. Of course he is.

"Cockpit's up there?" I ask.

"A bit further but I'll go check."

I look around the lab while he climbs. Microscope. Autoclave. Sample fridge. Oven. I know what everything is. I know how to use all of it. Useful information that tells me almost nothing about who I am, which feels like a very specific kind of unfair. I get to keep the skills but not the person who earned them.

There's a whiteboard on the far right wall, mounted ninety degrees off, so whatever's written on it runs top to bottom instead of left to right.

"It's locked," he says from up the ladder.

I look up. He's at the hatch, not moving.

"Locked how."

"Says to state my name."

"Can you?"

"Kenzo. J. Kenzo." A pause. "Nope."

"Try the full thing."

"I don't know the full thing." Another pause, longer.

"So?"

"I can't get in with just my last name."

He climbs back down. No frustration in it, just recalculating. He lands and looks around the lab the same way I did, systematic, left to right.

"Logs," he says.

"Yeah."

We split without discussing it. He goes for the console on the far table. I walk toward the whiteboard.

It's covered in handwriting, all of it running sideways. I tilt my head to read it. Not neat, not messy, somewhere in between. Someone wrote this quickly but carefully, like they wanted it to be readable by someone who wasn't them.

At the top, underlined twice:

Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP

Below that, a diagram. An arc, rising from a point I assume is Nakuet, curving outward and downward toward something labeled ASI-06A. The arc widens at the end like a funnel. Annotations everywhere. Wavelength readings. Density estimates. A note in smaller writing that says not visible, IR detection only, see attached data.

I stare at the diagram.

Something moves in the back of my head.

---

The desk is mine. I know it because of the coffee cup in the corner and the stack of papers I've been meaning to file for three weeks but haven't. The screen in front of me is halfway through a document I've been writing for longer than I want to admit.

The phone is on speaker.

"So the arc itself," I say, typing. "It's not moving toward the system or away from it?"

"Neither. Sort of." Her voice is clear, unhurried, the voice of someone who has already had this exact conversation with three other people today and is now having it a fourth time as a courtesy. "It is fixed relative to Nakuet. Which is what makes it interesting. Everything else in the system moves. This does not."

"And the Phengoclept is what's causing it."

"The Phengoclept is the arc. Or rather, the arc is what the Phengoclept leaves behind when it migrates. Think of it like the Day-Star rockets. You always see an arc from them."

I type that down. A behaviour. I underline it.

"That's not detailed enough for a study guide."

"It's not detailed enough for me either," she says. "That's why I'm giving you twenty minutes instead of five."

I lean back. Outside my window it's early morning, still grey, heavy snow, same apartments as always, same city that's been slowly, patiently freezing for as long as I've been alive in it.

"Y'shari-line," I say. "That's what they're calling it."

A pause on her end. "I've heard that. I don't love it."

"It's accurate."

"It's my name on something I don't fully understand yet." Flat. Not looking for sympathy, just stating a fact she'd rather not have to state. "I'm going to be the thing people hear when they think of the space dots for the rest of my career, whether I've earned that or not."

I don't push on it. There's a version of me that would, that would poke at the discomfort until it turned into something usable for the book, but I've learned by now that Y'shari answers questions on her own schedule or not at all.

"Alright. I'm writing a study, not an article about your name. So the baseline understanding, for the book, is: someone picks this up and they know nothing. What do I tell them?"

She thinks for a moment. I can hear her thinking, the quality of silence that means she's actually working through it rather than stalling.

"Tell them Nakuet is losing something it shouldn't be able to lose. Tell them we found the thing taking it. Tell them we don't know why it's taking it or where it's going or whether it knows we're here."

I type all of that.

"Well, we already know that," I say.

"You know the part I've told you. I know considerably more than that, most of which isn't for a study guide."

That lands somewhere I don't examine too closely. It's not the first time she's drawn that particular line between what I'm allowed to have and what she actually knows, and it won't be the last, and I've never once stopped to ask myself why she keeps taking these calls at all if there's this much of it I'm not cleared for.

"Not very good for a book," I say instead.

"It's the book you're qualified to write." Not unkind. Just precise. "I have a meeting in a few minutes. I'll catch you around."

The line goes dead.

I'm folding a shirt I don't remember owning into a bag I don't remember packing, the TV going in the corner with the sound turned low, blue light flickering over the wall, on for the noise more than for anything I'm actually watching.

"And for anyone just joining us," the anchor says, her voice pitched slightly too bright for whatever's actually on the paper in front of her, "scientists are calling it simply the Y'shari-line, named for the researcher who first identified it two years ago. In plain terms, it's a faint band of light stretching out from Nakuet, and like every other line of its kind that's ever been observed, it's been getting steadily brighter while the star it's attached to gets steadily dimmer."

I keep folding. Half listening. The kind of listening you do to something on in the background, present without being present.

"Today, for the first time, a probe is close enough to actually sample whatever's producing it," the anchor continues, "and in just a few minutes, we're expecting the first images back from its onboard instruments. Scientists have told us this could tell us, for the first time, exactly what's out there."

I stop folding.

Not because of anything she said. Because of the name in the corner of the screen, small, in the guest-expert graphic under a live video feed, a Kanadian research office backdrop behind her.

Y'shari.

Same voice I was just on the phone with. Same everything, except now she's not talking to me, she's talking to the entire world, calm, composed, doing the thing she does where she sounds certain even when I happen to know for a fact she isn't.

"The probe's onboard microscope should give us our first real look," she's saying, to the anchor, to the cameras, to however many people are watching this the way I'm halfway watching it right now. "But let's back up, because I know a lot of viewers are just hearing about this today."

"Please," the anchor says, grateful for the redirect.

"What we've mapped so far is a pattern," Y'shari says, and something in her settles once she's actually talking about the work instead of performing for a camera, a steadiness I recognize even secondhand through a television speaker. "Every star we've observed with a line like this shows the same behavior. The line appears, the star dims a little, and then the line gets brighter and the star dims a little more. Every one of them. We haven't found a single one that stopped."

"And where is that," the anchor asks.

"In our own system, the line runs from Nakuet toward the second planet out, Aluk'. Hot, thick atmosphere, exactly where something might go to breed." She pauses, choosing the next part carefully. "That's the working theory, at least. Something is feeding on Nakuet's output, converting it, storing it, and eventually carrying it off to reproduce there. What we're hoping this probe tells us, for the first time, is what that something actually looks like up close."

"And the concern," the anchor says, "for people watching at home."

"The concern," Y'shari says, "is that ours has been doing exactly what all the others did for two years now, which either means we're earlier in the curve than the models say, or."

She doesn't finish the sentence.

She stops.

Somewhere off camera, not quite quiet enough for the microphone to miss it, a voice says something that isn't meant for broadcast, a short, sharp "oh, come on" followed by a second voice, younger, considerably less composed, blurting out something about the number on a screen nobody watching at home can see. Neither of them is talking to Y'shari. Neither of them seems to remember there's still a live feed running.

Not a dramatic stop, on her end. Not one built for television. Something behind her attention shifts, off camera, a monitor or a feed I can't see, whatever's actually coming through on the other side of that microscope, and whatever it is, it takes the composure right off her face in about half a second, replaces it with something I don't have a name for yet and will spend a very long time later trying to find one for.

"I'm sorry," she says, already half turned away from the camera, "I have somewhere else I need to be."

The feed cuts back to the anchor, faintly confused, already moving on to something else, already treating it like a minor technical hiccup instead of whatever it actually was.

I stand there with a shirt in my hands, listening to a stranger apologize for the abrupt cut, and something in my chest that I don't have words for either sits there being uneasy about it for a lot longer than the segment itself lasted.

I go back to packing.

ARACCSIM. First time it's said anything to either of us that wasn't a number, a vital sign, or an order to eat, and the name comes out strange, pulled from somewhere else in whatever library this thing keeps, a flat statement-voice dropped into the middle of a sentence built for questions. Like someone recorded my surname once, in one register, and the machine's been stuck using that same clip regardless of what it's actually trying to say with it. Its voice is smooth, slightly synthetic otherwise, with the quality of something that is very capable and has no intention of letting you forget it. "Identity confirmed. Who is your co-pilot? Please state their name."

The hatch doesn't click.

I look down at him. Kenzo looks up at me.

"That's, uh. Weird," I say.

"It knows us, but we don't know ourselves," he says.

"Apparently."

I push on the hatch.

"Can you open the hatch? I trust him," I say. "The engineer. Engineer Kenzo."

"Mr. Kenzo's identity is partially confirmed. First name required."

I look back down. He's still there.

"It needs your first name," I say.

A pause from below.

"I don't remember it."

I look at the screen next to the door.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "What's the software engineer's first name?"

"That information is available to confirmed crew only."

"I am confirmed crew."

A pause.

"Jinzhou," it says. "Mr. Jinzhou T. Kenzo."

I look back down the ladder at Kenzo. At Jinzhou. He's looking up at me with an expression that suggests he already suspected this was going to go well.

"Jinzhou," I say.

He tries it.

"Jinzhou Kenzo."

The hatch clicks. I climb up first and take the left seat, then he climbs through and takes the right seat.

The console in front of me is not, it turns out, entirely in a language I can read.

Most of it tracks. Screens, readouts, things labeled in the same plain lettering as everything else on this ship so far. But there's a strip of switches along the lower edge of my side of the panel, worn smooth at the edges like they get used constantly, and the text stenciled above them isn't anything I recognize, tall narrow characters, unfamiliar rhythm to the letters even where I can tell they're letters and not symbols. It's Latin derived, that's for sure. More out of habit than any expectation, I mouth out the words I don't understand.

It doesn't mean anything. It just sits there, a sound with no content behind it.

Jinzhou glances over, follows where I'm looking, and doesn't say anything about it, which I notice and file away for later without knowing yet why it matters.

"What is the purpose of this mission," ARACCSIM asks.

I open my mouth. Close it. Something surfaces, slow and certain, the way the name did.

"To stop Nakuet from dimming," I say. "To save Wask-ki from freezing."

A pause. Longer than the question should need.

"Partial confirm. Dr. Vasek, you are cleared as secondary pilot. Do you vouch for Mr. Kenzo as co-pilot." Then nothing. No countdown, no prompt, nothing to fill the gap, the specific patient quiet of something that's been built to stop talking entirely once it's asked a real question, and wait however long it takes for a real answer.

"Yes," I say.

A beat, and then: "Jinzhou T. Kenzo." His full name arrives in a different register than the sentence around it, warmer, slightly higher, like it was pulled from a separate recording made on a better day by someone in a better mood, and grafted on before the machine remembers to go flat again. "Co-pilot access granted. Welcome aboard, you two."

He moves through the screens without comment on ARACCSIM, which I've already decided has a personality, and a slightly smug one at that.

Neither of us says anything for a moment. The screens scroll quietly through what I can only imagine to be star maps, system readouts, trajectory data, a zoomed image of something that I assume is Nakuet in the upper left corner. It's not in English.

I look at it.

Then I look at it properly.

"Hand me something," I say.

Jinzhou looks around, fumbles to pull a stylus from a slot in his armrest, hands it to me. I use it to work with the screens, trying to figure out where we are.

"What are you doing," he says.

"Checking, uh, something."

"What."

"Give me a second. Hm."

I switch the screen to show the star we're orbiting. There are spots on the surface of the star, darker patches moving slowly across the face of it. I time them. Watch them cross. Check again.

The numbers seem familiar but are still wrong.

"Rotation period," I say. "What's Nakuet's rotation period."

Jinzhou pulls up a secondary screen. Reads it. "Twenty-seven, twenty-five days, give or take."

I look at my numbers. Look at the spots.

"That star's rotation period is about, mmn, thirty-two days."

A loud gulp. Mine, apparently. I hadn't planned that.

I pull up the navigation screen. The star map is centered on a point labeled ASI-06A. Asimov-06-A, sitting right in the middle with a trajectory line running from somewhere far to the left all the way to where we are now.

There's a label in the top right corner too, and I get about half a second of hope that it's going to tell me something before I realize it's in the same unreadable lettering as the switches under the screen.

"Yeah, no, I can't read that," I say, to nobody in particular, since apparently the screen and I are not on speaking terms.

"Jinzhou. Read that."

He looks at it for a second, longer than it should take someone who actually knows what it says.

"Current system," he says, slow, like he's translating it out loud as much for himself as for me. "Asimov. Distance from Nakuet system. Six point four light years."

"Okay, uh. I don't believe you," I say, to him now instead of the screen.

Jinzhou says nothing else. Just watches me do the math on what he just told me.

I pull up the stellar readout for the star on screen. All the details needed.

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Dr. Vasek."

"What system are we in?"

"Stephfren Asimov Memorial System, designation ASI-06A. You are currently inbound within the Asimov system. The approximate time to Asimov-09D is about one week."

I look at Jinzhou.

He looks at me.

"This isn't the Nakuet system," I say. "It really isn't."

"No," he says. "It is not."

*END OF CHAPTER ONE*
