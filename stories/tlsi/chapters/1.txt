"What is three plus four?"

No.

Sleeping.

...

"What is three plus four?"

Something's talking. Far away. Then really close.

"Mgmsnmn," I say. "Jdh. Pft."

"Cognition assessment failed. Waiting."

Wuh. Huh? What?

Failed what? Who's- where's-

It's dark. My eyes are shut. I know that much. I tell them to open. They don't. I tell my paws to move. They don't either. Nothing does. I'm stuck in here.

My heart's going. I can hear it in my ears. Way too fast.

Okay. Okay okay okay.

"What is three plus four?"

Three. Plus four. I know this. I know this one. It's...

Seven. It's seven. I try to say seven.

"Sehhhv."

Terrible. That was terrible.

"Incorrect."

No, I said- that was-

"What is three plus four?"

I push everything I have into my mouth.

"Seven."

"Correct."

Okay. One thing. I did one thing. My heart slows down a little.

"What is your name?"

My name. My name is.

My name.

I open my mouth and there's nothing to put in it. It should be right here. It's always right here. Where did it go?

My heart speeds up again.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation required."

"Nnno. Wai-"

Something cradles the back of my head and lowers it, very gently.

HISS. THUNK.

---

I wake up again. Slower this time. It comes back in pieces. Light first, red through my eyelids. Then sound: a fan, somewhere. Then my own breathing, which is apparently handling itself.

I open my eyes. Both of them. First try. Progress.

"What is three plus four?"

Oh, this again. But my head's clearer now. Clear enough to be petty.

"Ten," I say.

"Incorrect."

"In base seven," I say. "It's ten in base seven. That's- I'm right."

"Incorrect. What is three plus four?"

So it doesn't think. It has one answer on file and it's checking me against it. Good to know. Also good to know: an hour ago I couldn't say "seven," and the first thing I did with a working mouth was pick a fight about number bases.

What kind of person does that?

"Seven. Sorry. Seven."

"Correct."

The ceiling is white and close. A ring of flat lights. Two robot arms hang from the center, each one ending in a bundle of tools. Clamps. Something with a needle. Some things I can't name and don't want to.

"Paw movement detected," the computer says. "Remain still, Dr."

It stops there. "Dr." and then nothing, like it's leaving a blank for me to fill in.

Still, that's new information. I'm a doctor. A doctor of what? I run a quick test. Name a bone. Femur. Name another one. Uh. Okay, I'm not a medical doctor. Some other kind.

The arms come down.

"Oh. Hi. Hello. Okay, what are you- okay. We're doing this. Yep."

They're fast and precise. A line comes out of my arm. A sensor patch comes off my chest. A long, thin tube slides out of my nose, which is a feeling I'll be trying to forget for the rest of my life.

That was a feeding tube. In through the nose, down to the stomach. It's the gentlest way to feed someone who can't eat for themselves, and you only bother with it if they're going to be out for a while. Weeks. Months, maybe.

Huh. How long have I been here?

When the arms pull back, two tubes are left. A catheter, and one other. You can guess where. Of all the tubes to leave in, they picked those two.

I lift my right arm and let it fall. Then the left. Heavy, but they work. I do a few more reps. It's the only thing I can do, so I'm going to be great at it.

Wait. Look at that arm.

It's muscular. Not huge, but healthy. That's wrong. If I've been on a feeding tube long enough to lose my name, my muscles should have wasted down to nothing. Muscle you don't use goes away. It's one of the most dependable facts in biology.

So someone's been using mine for me. I look at the arms. Electrical stimulation, maybe, or physically moving my limbs. Either way, they've been giving me workouts while I slept.

"Thank you?" I tell them. "I think? Is that- is it weird to thank you? It feels weird."

They don't respond. Probably for the best.

I check myself over. I'm wearing a thin, translucent, peach-colored suit, one piece, with a hood that's still up over my ears. The word FRONT is printed at the collar (in case I got confused). There are round port caps over my chest and right thigh. Fine wires run from the cuffs down to my feet, where they connect to a pair of socks.

Toe socks. Each toe has its own little sleeve.

Why toe socks?

Someone chose those. You don't end up with individually wired toe socks by accident. So what would you measure at someone's toes? Temperature and circulation, most likely. Toes are the farthest point from the heart. When blood flow goes bad, they're the first to know. If you had a patient lying still for months, you'd want an early warning. And if you were really thorough, you'd want one for every toe.

Someone was very worried about my feet. I don't know who, but I like them already.

My claws are short and neat, too. All twenty. Somebody's been trimming them. Claws grow around three millimeters a month (I don't know how I know that), and none of mine are chipped or overgrown, so either I've only been here a week or the arms do manicures. Given the feeding tube, I'm betting on manicures.

I push the hood back. My ears pop up. That feels incredible. Best thing that's happened so far, though the competition isn't stiff.

Six beds in all. Three on each wall, set into oval cubbies, in a tall, square room. Mine is the top one. Five other shapes under blankets and hoods. Other people. I'm not alone. I'll take that.

Next step: stand up.

Sitting goes fine. Swinging my legs over goes fine. Standing goes fine for about one second.

Then I'm on the floor. One ear folded under my head, the suit twisted around my arm.

"Fudge!"

Fudge?

That's my word? I just face-planted onto a metal floor and I went with "fudge"? I've apparently got a doctorate. I know bigger words than that. I can feel them in there. They're just... off-limits. Like somebody told me not to say them a long time ago, and I actually listened. I must have been that kind of kid.

I don't remember who told me. But I bet they'd be proud of the fudge.

"Body movement detected."

"Yep. Yeah. I was there."

"For your safety, please remain on the medical platform until muscle function is restored."

"That would've been really helpful about five seconds ago."

The arms hover over me, but they don't help. I'm not hurt, I'm just on the floor, and apparently floor isn't their department.

"What is your name?" the computer asks.

Okay. Concentrate. It called me Dr. Maybe if I start with that, the rest will follow, like the second line of a song you only half know.

"Dr...."

Nothing. I glance down at my socks.

"Dr. Toes," I say.

"Incorrect."

"Yeah. That one's on me."

"What is your name?"

Kitchen. Light switch. Wall.

"I don't know," I say.

It sounds a lot worse out loud.

"Response unsatisfactory. Sedation req-"

"No, no, wait. One second. Just give me one second."

It gives me about five. I use all of them.

Nothing.

The arm moves.

"Oh, come on, you didn't even-"

HISS. THUNK.

---

Third time.

I'm back in bed. The suit's been straightened out. Both remaining tubes are gone (thank you, robot arms, for taking those while I wasn't watching).

Everything's sharper now. I reach up and find out why. Glasses. Wire frames, bent slightly on the left. My paw pushes them up the bridge of my nose without me asking it to, like it's done that ten thousand times.

So I wear glasses, and I push them up. It's not a name, but it's another fact. I'm starting a collection.

There's a dark panel beside my bed, shiny enough to work as a mirror. I lean over. A vulpes looks back at me. Amber fur, white chest. Male. Mid-twenties, I'd guess. He looks tired, which is just insulting, given how much sleep he's had.

"Hi," I tell him. "I'm, uh. Dr."

He doesn't look impressed.

There's a window at the far end of the room. Round, thick glass, metal frame. I somehow missed it twice. To be fair, I was busy being sedated.

I get up slowly and keep a paw on the bed frames the whole way across. It takes a while. I make it.

The glass is cold.

Stars.

Not many. I count twenty-four. And something about them is off. It takes me a second to work out what.

They aren't twinkling.

Stars twinkle because of air. Pockets of warm and cool air bend starlight on its way down to the ground, and the stars shimmer. No air, no twinkle. These are perfectly still. Pinpricks.

So there's no air between me and them.

So I'm not on a planet.

"Okay," I say. "Okay. Okay, that's- okay."

I'm in space. This is a spaceship. I'm on a spaceship, in a plastic suit and toe socks, and I don't know my own name.

I should be panicking. I'm not, and I'm not sure why. Maybe it's too big. Like hearing a number with so many zeroes it stops meaning anything.

But twenty-four stars is too few. With no atmosphere in the way, I should be seeing thousands. A dark-adapted eye can pick out a few thousand stars even from the ground. So either my eyes are terrible (possible; I do wear glasses), or there's too much light in here.

It's the light. My pupils are adjusted for a bright room. Full dark adaptation takes twenty or thirty minutes, and I'm not standing here that long. I cup my paws around my face, press them against the glass, and give it two minutes.

I count to sixty. Then again.

Thirty-seven stars. Hypothesis confirmed. I'd high-five somebody if there were anybody to high-five.

Huh.

It's quiet in here. Really quiet. A fan somewhere. A low hum under the floor. The arms are folded up against the ceiling.

And behind me, nothing.

I've been up and moving around for a while now. I fell out of bed. I yelled "fudge" at a volume I'm not proud of. Nobody stirred. Nobody coughed. Nobody rolled over. And with five people in one room, somebody should be snoring.

I turn around.

I'm the only one standing.

---

"Hello?"

It comes out small. I clear my throat and try again.

"Hello. Hi. Um. Anybody?"

Smaller, somehow.

Nobody answers. The room hums. Five beds, five shapes under five see-through hoods, and not one of them so much as twitches an ear.

Okay. They're asleep. I was asleep too, until recently. The difference is that the computer kept poking me, and nobody's poking them. So I'll poke them.

I start with the closest one. Middle bed, my row, right under mine. A vulpes, a little smaller than me, paws folded on her chest like somebody arranged them that way. The hood hides most of her face. What I can see of it looks peaceful.

"Excuse me," I say. "Ma'am? Hi. Sorry. I don't know your name. I don't know mine either, so, we're even. Could you, um. Wake up? If that's okay?"

I put a paw on her shoulder. Gently, the way you'd wake a stranger on a train.

Nothing.

I shake her a little harder. Her suit makes the same soft plastic crinkle mine does. That's the only sound.

"Deep sleeper," I say. "That's fine. Lots of people are deep sleepers."

The bottom bed in my row has a canine in it. Lean, curled a little on his side. I say hello to him. I tap the frame. Nothing.

Row one is across the room. I get there by going from bed frame to bed frame. Top bed: a wolf. Huge. The cubby looks like it was built for somebody smaller and he got it anyway. I can't reach his shoulder without climbing, so I knock on the edge of the berth like it's a door.

"Sir? Hi. Hello. Big guy. You look like somebody who knows what's going on. Could you, would you mind knowing it at me?"

He would mind, apparently.

Middle bed: a serval, slight, ears squashed flat under her hood in a way that can't be comfortable. Bottom bed: a stocky canine with a jaw like he's about to argue with somebody. I say hello to both of them. Then louder. Then a third time, at a volume I'm not proud of.

Nothing.

I put a paw on the frame of the serval's cubby. It's cool. Cooler than mine felt, I think, though I wasn't exactly taking measurements in mine.

Okay. That makes sense. Comas slow everything down. Heart rate, breathing, metabolism. A slower body makes less heat. Cool is fine. Cool is expected.

That's a good explanation, and I like it, so I stop there. I could check more. I know how to find a pulse. I don't.

"Computer," I say to the ceiling. "Hi. Hello. Please wake them up."

A chime. Two bright notes, like a doorbell at a birthday party.

"Crew wake schedule is not available."

"Okay. That's, okay. I'm not asking for the schedule. I'm asking you to wake them up. Now. Instead of whenever the schedule is."

"Crew wake schedule is not available."

"Then who has it? Can I talk to whoever has it? The, the manager? Of the beds?"

"Please remain calm."

"I AM CALM! THIS IS ME BEING CALM!"

I'm yelling at a ceiling. I know that yelling at a ceiling doesn't work. Knowing that and not doing it turn out to be two separate skills, and I only have the first one.

I go back to the vulpes in my row, because she's closest, and because I've decided, based on nothing, that she's the one who'll know things. A bundle of lines runs from her berth into the wall, the same kind the arms pulled out of me. They plug into a panel. The panel has buttons. I don't know what they do. I'm about to find out.

An arm unfolds from the ceiling and puts itself, very politely, between my paw and the panel.

"Please step away from the medical platform."

"Move."

"Please step away."

"I'm trying to help her!"

"Please step away."

It doesn't push me. It doesn't grab me. It just stays there, patient and immovable, like a crossing guard made of surgical steel. And something in me that's been sitting very still since I found out I don't have a name stands up and loses it.

There's a paper cup on the ledge by the berth. Empty. I throw it at the arm. It bounces off without a sound.

There's a roll of medical tape. I throw that too. It hits a joint and rolls under the bed.

I'm out of things to throw, so I pull off a sock. This takes a while. Every toe has to be negotiated with individually. Then I throw it at the arm as hard as I can.

It catches on the wrist joint and hangs there.

A pale ribbed toe sock, dangling off a surgical robot.

The arm seems to consider this. Then it folds itself back up into the ceiling, sock and all, like the matter's been settled and I've made my position clear.

"YEAH," I yell after it. "THAT'S RIGHT!"

I'm not sure what I'm agreeing with. I sit down on the floor.

---

I stay down there a while. My breathing slows. My heart slows. Up on the ceiling, my sock sways a little.

Okay. Inventory. One suit. One sock. One pair of glasses. Five people who won't wake up. One computer that won't help. One robot arm that now owns half my socks.

And one more thing, which has been bugging me since I threw the cup.

It fell wrong. Too fast. I threw it up and sideways, and it came down quicker than my arm expected, like something yanked it. Same with the tape. Same with me, when I fell out of bed.

Things are heavier here. I can't tell how much. But my body has a lifetime of opinions about how fast stuff falls, and this floor disagrees with every one of them.

I could test it. Drop something, time the fall. I don't have a stopwatch, though. I could count "one-one-thousand," but that's only good to about half a second, and a cup falls off a ledge in less than that. Useless. I need a clock.

So I file it. Heavy floor. It goes on the list of Things That Are Wrong, which is getting long.

Here's the thing about the list, though. I don't know what any of it means. That's the bad part. It's also, weirdly, the okay part. I'm upset. I yelled. I threw a sock. But I'm upset the way you get when you don't know what's happening and you can't stop it. That's distress.

Panic is different. Panic is when you know exactly what's happening, and you still can't stop it.

Right now I've only got the first one. I'd like to keep it that way.

---

The other arm comes down. Not the sock one. It's holding a tube, like a toothpaste tube, right in front of my face.

"Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat."

"I just threw a sock at your coworker. I'm not really in a snacking, I don't feel-"

"Eat."

It doesn't blink. It doesn't have anything to blink with. Somehow that makes it harder to argue with.

"Fine."

I take it. There's a label in plain block capitals.

VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST
MEAL: 1/3 / DAY: 1

Vasek. E.

I stare at that for a long time.

It doesn't ring a bell. It doesn't not ring a bell, either. It's like hearing a song through a wall. You know you know it before you know what it is.

"Vasek," I say out loud.

It fits. Mostly. Like a coat somebody else has been wearing for a while.

I squeeze the tube. Something like gravy and mashed potatoes hits my tongue, and the whole thing's gone in about four seconds. Best thing I've ever eaten. (Sample size: one.)

Oh, and that explains the nose tube. They kept my stomach working the whole time I was under, so it'd be ready for this. That's good planning. I don't know who did the planning, but they're good at it.

"Computer. My name is Vasek."

"Partial confirm. First name required."

"It's E. Something. Something that starts with E."

"Partial confirm. First name required."

"It's on the label. You printed it on the label. You know it, and you're giving me a pop quiz."

No answer. That's how it wins arguments, I'm learning.

Still. Secondary scientist. I'm a scientist! The second one, but still. That's a job. I have a job.

Day one, the label says.

Day one of what, it doesn't say.

---

There's a ladder on the far wall, running up to a hatch in the ceiling.

I know where it goes. The lab. Past that, the cockpit. I know it like you know the house you grew up in, and I don't know how I know it, which is not a fun way to know something.

Something about it is off, too. My brain keeps trying to turn the room on its side, like that ladder ought to be lying flat on the floor and I ought to be walking along it. I don't know why. That goes on the list.

I go up with one sock on and one sock off. It's about fifteen feet.

Fifteen feet. I think in feet for short distances. That's a clue. A small one. I'll take any size.

Every rung is a fight, and I win each one by a very small margin.

LAB A, says the stencil beside the hatch.

It isn't a room. It's a shaft. Narrow and tall, going up farther than I can see, with a metal grate a few feet below the hatch, the roll-down kind you'd see over a shop window at night. Here it's the floor. Below the grate the shaft keeps going, research equipment bolted along the walls all the way down. Above me, shelving and storage, everything strapped and latched, like whoever built this expected the ship to tip over at some point and wanted everything to survive it.

Right in front of me are an oven and a sample fridge, both mounted on gimbals so they stay level no matter which way the ship points. Microscope. Autoclave. Pipettes. Incubator. Fume hood. I know what everything is, and I know how to use all of it.

So I kept the skills and lost the person who learned them. I don't remember agreeing to that trade.

There's a whiteboard on the far wall, mounted sideways, so the writing runs top to bottom. I tilt my head. The handwriting is quick but careful. Somebody wrote this for somebody who wasn't them.

At the top, underlined twice:

Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP

Below that, a diagram. An arc, rising from a point I somehow know is Nakuet, curving out and down toward something labeled ASI-06A. The arc widens at the end like a funnel. Notes everywhere. Wavelengths. Density estimates. Smaller writing in the corner: not visible, IR detection only, see attached data.

I know this diagram. I've seen it a hundred times. I think I've drawn it.

Something moves in the back of my head.

---

The desk is mine. I know because of the coffee cup in the corner and the stack of papers I've been meaning to sort for three weeks. The screen in front of me is halfway through a document I've been writing for longer than I want to admit.

The phone is on speaker.

"So the arc itself," I say, typing. "It's, uh, not moving toward the system or away from it?"

"Neither. Sort of." Her voice is clear and unhurried. She's had this exact conversation with three other people today and she's having it a fourth time as a courtesy. "It is fixed relative to Nakuet. That is what makes it interesting. Everything else in the system moves. This does not."

"And the Phengoclept is what's causing it."

"The Phengoclept is the arc. Or rather, the arc is what the Phengoclept leaves behind when it migrates. Think of the Day-Star rockets. You always see an arc from them."

I type that down. A behaviour. I underline it.

"That's not detailed enough for a study guide."

"It's not detailed enough for me either," she says. "That's why I'm giving you twenty minutes instead of five."

I lean back. Outside my window it's early morning, still grey, heavy snow, same apartments as always. Same city that's been slowly freezing for as long as I've lived in it.

"Y'shari-line," I say. "That's what they're calling it."

A pause on her end. "I've heard. I don't love it."

"It's accurate."

"It's my name on something I don't fully understand yet." Flat. Not fishing for sympathy. Just a fact she'd rather not have to state. "I'm going to be the thing people think of when they think of the space dots, for the rest of my career. Whether I've earned it or not."

I don't push. Y'shari answers questions on her own schedule or not at all.

"Okay. So, I'm writing a study guide, not an article about your name. Baseline. Somebody picks up this book and knows nothing. What do I tell them?"

She thinks. I can hear her thinking. It's the kind of silence that means she's working it through, not stalling.

"Tell them Nakuet is losing something it shouldn't be able to lose. Tell them we found the thing taking it. Tell them we don't know why it's taking it, or where it's going, or whether it knows we're here."

I type all of that.

"Well, we already know that," I say.

"You know the part I've told you. I know considerably more than that, and most of it isn't for a study guide."

That lands somewhere I don't look at too closely. It's not the first time she's drawn a line between what I'm allowed to have and what she actually knows. I've never once asked why she keeps taking these calls, if there's this much I'm not cleared for.

"Not very good for a book," I say instead.

"It's the book you're qualified to write." Not unkind. Just precise. "I have a meeting in a few minutes. I'll catch you around, E-"

The line goes dead.

I'm folding a shirt I don't remember owning into a bag I don't remember packing. The TV's going in the corner with the sound turned low, more for the noise than anything else.

"And for anyone just joining us," the anchor says, her voice a little too bright for whatever's on the paper in front of her, "scientists are calling it simply the Y'shari-line, named for the researcher who first identified it two years ago. In plain terms, it's a faint band of light stretching out from Nakuet, and like every other line of its kind that's ever been observed, it's been getting steadily brighter while the star it's attached to gets steadily dimmer."

I keep folding.

"Today, for the first time, a probe is close enough to actually sample whatever's producing it, and in just a few minutes we're expecting the first images back from its onboard instruments."

I stop folding.

Not because of anything she said. Because of the name in the corner of the screen. Small, in the guest-expert graphic, under a live feed from a Kanadian research office.

Y'shari.

Same voice I was just on the phone with. Except now she's talking to the whole world. Calm. Composed. Doing the thing where she sounds certain, even when I happen to know she isn't.

"What we've mapped so far is a pattern," she's saying, and something in her settles once she's talking about the work instead of performing for a camera. "Every star we've observed with a line like this shows the same behavior. The line appears, the star dims a little, and then the line gets brighter and the star dims a little more. Every one of them. We haven't found a single one that stopped."

"And where is that," the anchor asks.

"In our own system, the line runs from Nakuet toward the second planet out, Aluk'. Hot, thick atmosphere, exactly where something might go to breed." She chooses the next part carefully. "Something is feeding on Nakuet's output, converting it, storing it, and eventually carrying it off to reproduce. What we're hoping this probe tells us, for the first time, is what that something actually looks like up close."

"And the concern," the anchor says, "for people watching at home."

"The concern," Y'shari says, "is that ours has been doing exactly what all the others did, for two years now. Which either means we're earlier in the curve than the models say, or."

She doesn't finish.

Somewhere off camera, not quite quietly enough, a voice says a short, sharp "oh, come on," and a second voice, younger and a lot less composed, blurts out something about a number on a screen nobody at home can see.

It isn't a dramatic stop. It's not built for TV. Something behind her eyes shifts toward a monitor I can't see, toward whatever's coming through that microscope, and in about half a second it takes the composure right off her face and replaces it with something I don't have a name for. I'll spend a long time looking for one.

"I'm sorry," she says, already half turned away. "I have somewhere else I need to be."

The feed cuts back to the anchor, who looks faintly confused and is already moving on.

I stand there holding a shirt. Something in my chest stays uneasy for a lot longer than the segment lasted.

I go back to packing.

---

I'm back in the lab, blinking at a whiteboard.

"E," I say. She was about to say it. She always did that when she was in a hurry. First letter only, like the rest of my name was paperwork she'd get to later. "E. Eli? Ezra? Ethan? No. Close, though. Ethyn."

It's like trying on a coat I haven't worn in years and finding out it still fits.

"Ethyn Vasek."

I say it again, louder. "Ethyn Vasek! That's me! I'm Ethyn Vasek!"

I pump my fist. Nobody sees it. I don't care. I have a name. I'm a person with a name now.

I say it one more time, to the whiteboard, since the whiteboard is the only thing on this ship that's helped.

---

The ladder keeps going up past the lab, to another hatch. My paws are on the rungs before the rest of me has voted.

There's a panel beside the hatch.

IDENTIFY TO PROCEED.

I'm ready for this one.

"Ethyn Vasek," I say, and I'll admit I'm a little smug about it. "Dr. Ethyn Vasek."

A pause. I wait for the click. I'd also accept confetti.

"Identity confirmed. Who is your co-pilot? Please state their name."

Oh.

"I don't, I don't have one. They're asleep. Everybody's asleep. It's just me."

"Cockpit access requires two confirmed crew."

"There's one confirmed crew. Me. I'm right here. I'm confirming."

"Cockpit access requires two confirmed crew."

I rest my forehead on the hatch. It's cool. It doesn't care about me at all. Fair enough. I wouldn't either.

I climb back down.

---

Off one side of Lab A there's a short neck, a passage barely longer than a doorway, with another shaft at the other end of it. LAB B, the stencil says. There's a console on the far table in there.

It wakes up when I touch it. No password. Menus, dozens of them, and all of it in a language I can't read. Tall, narrow letters. An unfamiliar rhythm to the words. I'm pretty sure it uses the same alphabet I do, which means I can sound it out and still have no idea what I'm saying. I sound out the first menu anyway. It's a noise with nothing behind it.

But not everything is words.

Some of the icons are pictures. One of them is a little camera. I press it, because the other option is pressing things at random, and the last time I did that I lost a sock.

A grid of camera feeds fills the screen. Most show black, or bits of hull, or stars. One shows a star.

Not a point. A disc. Filtered down so it doesn't fry the sensor. A pale gold circle in the middle of the frame with a soft glow around it. The feed has an arrow on it pointing forward. Arrows are arrows in every language, thank goodness. We're facing it. We're heading toward it.

Huh. Heading toward it.

The heavy floor.

Oh! It's not gravity. It's us. The ship's pushing toward that star, steady, all the time, and the floor's pushing up into my feet because of it. Like an elevator starting to go up, except it never stops starting. That's why the cup fell too fast. Nothing's pulling it down. The floor is coming up to meet it.

I cross one thing off the list. It feels great. It lasts about four seconds.

Because, okay. It's a star. Stars are fine. We have one at home. We have a perfectly good star at home called Nakuet, and I know a lot about it, because apparently I wrote a book about it. And I'm looking at a star right now, and the simplest conclusion is that this is Nakuet, and we're somewhere in our own system, and everything is fine.

There are spots on it.

Darker patches on the face. Sunspots. I lean in. There's a bar along the bottom of the feed, a timeline, and when I drag it backward the picture changes. It keeps an archive. Frames going back days. Each one has a timestamp, and numbers are numbers in every language too.

I know how to use this.

Sunspots ride along on the surface as the star turns. So if I time how long one takes to move across, I get the star's rotation period. Stars have fingerprints, sort of. Every one spins at its own rate.

I pick a spot. A big one near the middle, with a smaller one trailing behind it like a duckling. I drag the timeline back until the spot is right at the left edge, just coming into view. There's a marker in a tray by the console. No paper anywhere. So I write the timestamp on the sleeve of my suit. Plastic takes marker. (Fur doesn't. I check.)

Then I drag forward and follow the spot across the disc until it reaches the middle. I write that time down too.

Edge to middle is a quarter turn. Multiply by four. Fudge it a little for the angle. I know how to do this the way I know how to push my glasses up. It's in my paws.

Thirty-two days.

Give or take. Call it thirty-two.

And I know Nakuet's rotation period. Twenty-seven point two five days at the equator. I know it like you know your own phone number, if you know your own phone number. It's on page one of the study guide. It's the first fact I give people, because it's an easy one, and easy ones make people feel like they're going to understand the rest.

Twenty-seven.

Not thirty-two.

Okay. I messed up the math. That's the first possibility, and I really want it to be the right one, so I do it again. Different spot. One near the top, smaller, harder to follow. I track it frame by frame. I write the times on my sleeve, under the first ones.

Thirty-two days.

Third spot. Third set of numbers, running up toward my elbow now. I'm a scientist. A secondary scientist. I'm good at this.

Thirty-two.

I look at the numbers on my sleeve for a long time.

I'd like to say I yelled. I yelled at a ceiling twenty minutes ago because a computer wouldn't wake anybody up. I threw a sock. This is a much bigger problem than a sock, and I don't yell at all. I don't have the breath. Something has sat down on my chest, very calmly, and it isn't getting up.

This is the other one. This is panic.

Because I know exactly what I'm looking at. That's the problem. Everything on the list just slid together into one shape. The stars downstairs that didn't twinkle. The floor that's pushing. The label that says day one. The beds that won't wake up. The star that turns too slow. And the shape is this:

I'm not home.

I'm not anywhere near home.

Stars aren't neighbors. They're islands. Light takes years to cross from one to the next. If that isn't Nakuet, then home is so far behind us that the light leaving it right now, this second, while I stand here with numbers up my sleeve, won't get here until I'm a lot older.

The arc on the whiteboard runs from Nakuet to ASI-06A.

I think I'm at the far end of the arc.

I know it, and I can't stop it, and I can't climb back down the ladder and un-know it. So I stand there and let it be true, because there's nothing else it can be.

And I keep doing the math anyway, because my brain doesn't have an off switch. It only has a dimmer. Thirty-two days. Slower than Nakuet. Slow spinners tend to be older, because stars lose spin as they age. So, an older star. Probably. Somewhere I've never been. Doing star things.

I go to put the marker back in its tray, and my paw lands on the console instead.

Click.

The feed jumps. The gold disc slides off to the right side of the frame, and everything goes dark and red and wrong. The star is a dull blob pushed up against the edge of the screen.

"No. No no no. I didn't do that. Undo."

There is no undo.

I stand there looking at this monitor in awe.

There are so many faint red lines. And off to the side, a large bright one.

I nudge the camera over using a mix of arrow keys and a stick. (Thank you, engineers.)

...

It's the Y'shari-line.

Bright. Red. Beautiful.

It comes in from another star, way off past the edge of the screen, and it runs all the way to this one.

I can't put into words what I'm looking at. But no Ferin was ever supposed to see this.

"Amazing," I say. It comes out very quiet.

The star on the screen keeps turning. Thirty-two days at a time.

---

I don't know how long I stand there.

Long enough for the marker on my sleeve to dry. Long enough to read the numbers so many times they stop being numbers and turn back into ink.

At some point standing isn't enough, and I lie down. On the grate. It's the floor right now, and the floor and I have an understanding. The metal is cool through the suit and presses a neat little grid of squares into my back.

Above me the shaft goes up and up. Another grate, far overhead. Past it, shelving, straps, latches. The underside of everything, all built by people who expected to be here. Through the squares of the upper grate I can see a light on the far wall, blinking green. On. Off. On. Off. It doesn't know anything is wrong. Nothing on this ship seems to know anything is wrong except me.

I count the blinks. I count the squares in the grate above me. Forty across. I don't count the ones under me. I can feel them.

The lines are still up there on that monitor. I can't see them from down here. I can see them anyway.

Not one line. Lots of them. Faint ones everywhere, and the bright one, coming in from some other star to this one.

Huh.

It's like a cell network. Every star is a tower. The Phengoclept don't just eat one star and stop. They hop. Tower to tower. Star to star. This star caught it from a neighbor.

Which means the one at home caught it from somebody too.

I lie there with that for a while. It doesn't feel any better than the sunspots.

It's quiet. Fans. A hum so deep it's more in the grate than in the air. The push. My breathing slows. My heart slows. For a minute nothing is happening, and nothing is going to happen, and I'm lying in a quiet room a very long way from home, and it's almost peaceful.

Almost.

A chime. Two notes, bright and completely wrong for the moment. I know that chime. It's the computer that wouldn't wake anybody up.

"Wakey Wakey protocol initiated," the flat voice says, from everywhere at once. "Waking up crewmember S.Eng. J. T. Kenzo."

"What?-.."

I sit up so fast the grate rings under me.

"What? You, you said there wasn't a schedule. I asked you. I asked nicely, and then I asked with a sock, and you said-"

Something moves at the edge of the shaft.

The arms. Both of them, coming out of Lab C, through the neck and into Lab B, where I am, sliding along a track on the wall I hadn't noticed, folded up small and moving with purpose. One of them still has my sock hanging off its wrist. They pass right by me, close enough to touch, and they don't slow down. They glide on toward the hatch, folding themselves smaller to fit.

I didn't do anything. I'm almost sure I didn't do anything.

I still feel like I should follow them.

I try to get up. My legs have other ideas. I get one knee under me, my other foot finds the gap in the grate, and I go right back down onto my elbow, hard. The grate rings again, louder.

"Okay. Okay, okay, okay."

Second try. Both feet under me, and I'm up, and I'm moving, and then my bare foot comes down on my socked foot and I trip over myself. I didn't know you could do that with only two feet. I catch the ladder more by luck than by aim.

I don't climb down it. No time. I hook my feet on the outside rails and slide, which I'm fairly sure a safety briefing once told me never to do. The rungs blur past. The floor comes up a lot faster than it did at the top. (Of course it does. The floor is coming up to meet me. I just worked that out. I'd like it to stop.)

I hit the medbay floor feet first. Then everything else. My legs fold, I go over backward, and I keep going until I'm flat on my back at the bottom of the ladder with the wind knocked out of me, staring straight up.

Above me, the arms come through the hatch.

They take their time. One, then the other, unfolding out of the opening, sliding down the wall on their track and then swinging up onto the ceiling where they started this whole morning. My sock sways on the wrist of the second one. Neither of them looks down at me. They have somewhere to be.

So do I.

I roll over, get my paws under me, and run. It isn't much of a run. It's more of a lurch with ambition. I cross the room bouncing off the ends of berths that aren't mine and stop at the far side, holding on to the frame.

Row two. Bottom bed. The canine I said hello to. The arms are already working on him, unhooking things, brisk and businesslike, the same way they were with me. S.Eng. J. T. Kenzo, whoever that is.

He sits up.

Not dramatically. He gets about halfway, stops, thinks about it, then finishes the job. I'm standing across the room with my heart trying to climb out of my throat and numbers written all the way up my sleeve, and I think, with total clarity:

Oh. There's another one of us.

He looks over. He sees me staring at him like he's a ghost. Then his eyes go to the arm unhooking his lines, where a single pale toe sock is still hanging off the wrist.

He looks at the sock for a while.

Then he looks back at me.

"Halo," he says.

It comes out flat and hoarse and completely unbothered, and it's the best sound I've ever heard.

*END OF CHAPTER ONE*
