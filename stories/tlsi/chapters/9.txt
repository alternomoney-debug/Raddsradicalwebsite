
The second collector sample is a mess.

Not a bad mess. A busy one. There's way more on this slide than there was on the first. I've been staring at it for forty minutes, and I still can't tell if I'm looking at the most interesting thing I've ever seen or a very elaborate headache.

"ARACCSIM. Spectral breakdown on batch two. Again. Full output."

"Running."

Same graph as the last three times. Same curve. Same peaks. And the same two little bumps sitting in the readout that have no business being there.

Okay. Let's be methodical about this.

The Phengoclept cells are there. Good. Twenty-two point seven zero six micron emitters, nanogram mass, right where they should be. They're doing their thing with the total indifference of something that's been doing it since before anybody on Wask-ki had a word for "thing."

So that's the Phengoclept. What are the bumps?

I go back to the slide image and zoom in. Then I zoom in some more.

Huh.

There's something else on here.

Lots of something elses, actually. Scattered all over the field of view. No clumping. No pattern. They're not hanging around the Phengoclept, they're not avoiding it, they're just... there.

Dust? No. Dust is junk. Dust comes in every shape and size you can imagine. These are all roughly the same size. And they have structure. Insides. Layers.

Structure doesn't happen by accident at this scale.

What are you?

"Jinzhou."

"What."

"Stop whatever you're doing and come look at this."

A pause. Footsteps. He leans over my shoulder.

"Those are not Phengoclept," he says.

"Nope."

"Not the same shape. Not the same size." He leans closer. "Could be contamination. From us. From the collector."

"That's what I thought too. Look at the color."

He looks.

I wait. I'm good at waiting when I already know what somebody's about to see. (I'm terrible at it every other time.)

"Too clean," he says. "Contamination is random. These have structure." He points. "Blue at the membrane. Red in the middle. Green running through both. That is not random."

"Nope. It isn't."

"What is it?"

"I don't know yet!" It comes out way too cheerful. I can't help it. I pull the spectral breakdown up next to the image. "But look. Whatever it is, it's not emitting anywhere near twenty-two point seven zero six. It's absorbing there."

Jinzhou goes still. Not his regular still. His thinking still.

"Absorbing," he says.

"Absorbing."

Okay. Why does that matter? Think it through, Ethyn. Slowly.

When a Phengoclept cell spends its energy, it spits it out as light. Twenty-two point seven zero six microns. Every time. That's its whole thing. It's how it moves, it's how it dumps what it stored.

Now. If something out here soaks up light at exactly that wavelength. Not close to it. Exactly it.

That's not a coincidence. Nature doesn't hand out coincidences that specific. That thing is built to catch what the Phengoclept puts out.

Or to catch the Phengoclept.

Oh.

Oh!

The bathtub. The drain. I've been hunting for the drain for two days, and it's been sitting on the same slide as everything else, being blue and red and green at me.

"That would mean," Jinzhou starts.

"Something that eats at the same wavelength the Phengoclept emits," I say. I'm talking too fast. "Living in the same place. In a system where the Phengoclept population hasn't grown in two years. Jinzhou. Jinzhou!"

"Then this is it," he says.

"This is probably it," I say, because I've been wrong before, and getting loud about a conclusion before you've checked it three times is how you end up wrong in public.

"This is it," he says again. Same flat voice he uses for everything. Somehow it hits harder than if he'd yelled.

I push my glasses up.

"Yeah," I say. "This is it."

Then comes the part of science nobody puts on a poster.

I run the spectral breakdown six more times. Same answer. Jinzhou goes through the reference library looking for anything known that absorbs between twenty-two and twenty-three microns. It's a short list. Almost nothing alive has any reason to care about light at that wavelength.

The list has one hit that matters. And it's not even an organism. It's a theoretical paper. Published about four years before we left. It asks, basically: if something evolved to eat Phengoclept, what would it have to look like?

Wait.

I know this paper.

I read it when it came out. I thought it was clever. I thought it was also completely unprovable. So I put it in a folder on my desktop called MAYBE SOMEDAY, which is where I put things I like but don't believe.

I look at the author line.

Bejors wrote it.

I'm sitting in her lab. Her whiteboard is about ten feet behind me. And her name is on the screen, at the top of a paper describing the thing I just found.

I pull up her figures next to my slide. Transparent membrane. Check. Coloring on the inside from whatever's left over after it digests. Check. Absorbs Phengoclept emissions directly and stores the energy, the same trick the Phengoclept does with starlight. Check. Check. Check.

She described it. Years ago. From math. Before anybody had ever seen one.

She was right!

She was right, and I filed it under maybe.

"Someday is soon," she told me once, in a demo bay, with marker all over her paws. I didn't believe her then either. She would have been completely unbearable about this. I would have let her.

And there's something else about her, sitting just out of reach. A number she asked me to check once. I can feel the shape of it. I can't get it to come up.

"Ethyn," Jinzhou says.

"One second," I say. "I'm, uh. One second."

I open a new document. It needs a name, so I give it one. Asimov, plus cell. ASICELL. It's not what I'd call it in a formal write-up. It's what I'm calling it right now, because things you've found deserve a name, even a bad one.

At the top I type:

PRIMARY THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK: B. BEJORS, PHD. SECONDARY RESEARCHER: E. VASEK.

I look at that. Then I change my line to SECONDARY SCIENTIST, because that's my actual job, and because she'd have made fun of me for "researcher."

Her name goes first. It's her idea. All I did was find it.

"Done," I say.

"Okay," Jinzhou says, and doesn't ask.

More waiting.

"Spectral profile of the collected organism is consistent with the theoretical Phengoclept predator model within four point three percent. This is within expected variance for a theoretical model predicting an unobserved organism."

"Within four point three percent. Yes."

Four point three percent! For a guess made from scratch, about something nobody had ever seen, from six point four light-years away. That's not close. That's a bullseye with a little wobble.

I look at Jinzhou. He's already looking at me.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Is there anything in the mission data about this? About finding something like this?"

This pause is shorter. Like the answer was already sitting near the front.

"Protocol Seven was locked pending biological confirmation. Biological confirmation has now occurred."

It's long. Somebody back in Argon's LSI division had a lot of ideas about what to do if we found exactly this. The header says MISSION PROTOCOL SEVEN VARIATION-C: EXTENDED ATMOSPHERIC COLLECTION PROCEDURE.

Jinzhou reads faster than me. He's three sections in before I'm done with page one.

"There's a probe," he says.

"I'm getting there!"

I catch up to him. He's right. There's a full schematic. A sealed collection probe, reinforced for pressure, on a cable that runs back to the ship. Rated for the heat and pressure at the top of a gas giant's atmosphere. Sample chambers, filters, sensors. You drop it into the clouds, it grabs whatever's down there, you reel it back in.

Okay. Why would they bother with a probe if we already have a collector?

Because of the numbers. The collector skims. It gets a pinch. The first batch told us something was there. The second batch told us what it was. But you can't build a solution for a whole planet out of what fits in a vial. You need a real sample. A lot of it.

"We need the probe," I say.

"You need the probe," Jinzhou says.

"I need the probe."

The protocol is very specific about where the probe has to go. The Asicell should be thickest a hundred to two hundred meters below the top of the cloud layer, in the first dense band of methane and ammonia under the haze. Below that, there's nothing for a Phengoclept-eater to eat. Above it, there's not enough of it to matter.

It's also very specific about what getting there costs.

The probe is on a cable. The cable is on the ship. So for the probe to reach a hundred meters into the clouds, the ship has to come down close enough for the cable to reach. Way closer than safe orbit.

The protocol has a word for how much closer. The word is "significant." Nobody uses the word "significant" when the news is good.

"How close?" I ask.

Jinzhou checks the table and reads me the number.

I read it myself, because I didn't like it the first time.

"Yes."

"Yes."

Somebody had to believe in this enough to spend money on it. Somebody who'd read a paper about an organism nobody had ever seen and thought, no, that's real, build the hardware.

I wonder if she designed the thing that's going to catch her own organism.
