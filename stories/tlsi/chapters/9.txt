Day twenty-three, and the ship smells different.

Not bad. Just different. It smells like two people who've been working on the same problem long enough that the problem smells like them now. Lab chemicals. Recycled air. Whatever the food processor does when it heats things. I stopped noticing it around day fifteen. I only notice now because I walked in from the hallway and caught it fresh.

I put my tray down next to the microscope. Lab A, same as always.

The batch two sample is logged, filed, and backed up. It's already the most documented microbe in the history of a species that has been documenting microbes for a few hundred years. And I'm about to document it some more.

Why? Because tomorrow we drop below the Stephfren line, and there's nothing to do until then except get ready. And getting ready means knowing everything I can possibly know before we go down there and find out what I missed.

Jinzhou's in Lab C. I can hear him. Not talking, which is normal. Just the small clicks of tools being set down by somebody who treats equipment like it has feelings. He's been in there since before I woke up. Did he sleep? I'd ask, but the answer would be "yes," in a tone that ends the conversation.

I sit down and get to work.

---

Here's something I found on day nineteen that Bejors' paper didn't have.

I was running a population count on the batch two slide. Fourth time. (I like to be sure. You've met me.) And I was checking it against the Phengoclept count from the same slide.

And, huh. The two numbers moved together.

Wherever there was a little clump of Phengoclept, there was a little crowd of Asicell nearby. Not perfectly. Not every time. But way too often to be chance.

Coincidence? I ran it three more times. Nope. Not a coincidence.

So here's the question. How does a predator find its lunch in a cloud the size of a continent?

It follows it!

The Asicell tracks the Phengoclept. Probably by the same light. Twenty-two point seven zero six microns. The Phengoclept uses that light to get around. The Asicell uses it to find the Phengoclept. One organism's headlights are another organism's dinner bell.

Bejors would have hated "dinner bell." She'd have used it in a talk anyway.

Her paper described how the Asicell eats, what it absorbs, how it digests. It didn't describe this, because she only had math. I have an actual sample. That's not a knock on her. It's just what you get when somebody finally hands you the real thing.

I write it down. Twice. In two different places. The server room has made me paranoid about losing things, and I'd rather be the guy who wrote it down twice than the guy who lost it once.

PRIMARY FRAMEWORK: B. BEJORS. TRACKING BEHAVIOR ADDENDUM: E. VASEK.

I look at that for a second. It's the first time in three weeks I've put my own name on something without moving it to the bottom. It feels right. It also feels a little like stealing. Which is silly. I'm going with right.

I push my glasses up and keep going.

---

The EVA suits live in the airlock at the cockpit end, hanging on a rack. They look like they're waiting for something. They are. They've been waiting for about seven years.

I pull the first one down around midmorning.

Oof. It's heavy. Why is it this heavy?

Two options. One, I lost more muscle than I thought in six years of stasis. Two, this is a different class of suit than the ones I trained in. Very briefly. In a crash course crammed into the three days before launch. (I remember those three days in pieces. Most of the pieces are me sweating.)

Probably both. I'm going to blame the suit.

The checklist is printed inside the cabinet door. Laminated! Somebody sat at a desk and thought, "What if one of them has to check a suit by himself, six light-years from anybody who knows how?" And they laminated it.

Whoever you are, laminator, I love you. In a bureaucratic way.

I go down the list. Seal integrity. Oxygen, main and backup. Heaters, because the temperature outside the hull is a number I looked up once and then decided never to think about again. Thruster pack, which I'm not planning to use and would like to keep not using. Comms. Tether points. Helmet lock. Gloves.

Suit one passes everything. I log it, hang it back up, grab suit two.

Suit two has a scuff on the left forearm. A little one. It's not in any of the pre-launch inspection records. So it happened in transport or assembly, and nobody wrote it down.

Is it a problem? I look closer. It's paint, not material. Cosmetic. Not structural. Okay. I'll write it down myself.

"ARACCSIM."

"Dr. Vasek."

"Suit two has a scuff, left forearm, cosmetic. Not in the records. I'm logging it. Can you confirm it passed the structural checks?"

"Confirmed. Structural integrity of suit two is within all operational parameters."

"Thank you."

A pause. The longer kind. I've figured out that one means it's going to fetch a recording instead of just saying the next line.

"You are welcome," it says.

I've started saying thank you just to hear which recording it picks. Today's is warm. Yesterday's sounded like a bank.

---

Jinzhou finds me on suit three.

He doesn't say anything at first. He leans against the rack and watches me work for a minute. There's grease on his left paw that he hasn't wiped off. For Jinzhou, that means he got into something complicated and decided checking on me was more important than cleaning up.

"Probe?" I ask, without looking up.

"Cable first," he says. "Three hundred meters. I checked every coupling."

"Every one?"

"Every one. By paw." He says it like a fact, not a brag. "The cable is fine."

"And the probe?"

"Sample chambers sealed. Pressure sensors reading correctly. Insulation intact." He pauses. "The winch is the part I am not completely happy with."

I look up.

"Not unhappy," he says, before I can ask. "It works. I tested it under load. But it sat in storage eight years, and the motor draws a few percent more power than spec."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it works. Meaning under normal conditions it is fine. Meaning if something goes wrong and we need to reel the probe in fast and hard, it will do it, but it will run hot."

Huh. Okay. Why does a few percent matter?

More power in means more heat. Fine. On a planet, a hot motor just sits there and cools off, because air carries the heat away. But the winch is in the payload bay. There's no air moving in there. Out here, heat only leaves by glowing it off, which is slow. So every hard pull adds heat, and the heat stays. It stacks up.

"Not a problem unless it becomes a problem," Jinzhou says.

"So we don't let it become a problem."

"Correct."

I go back to my checklist. He stays leaning on the rack.

"How is the science," he says.

"Good! Really good, actually. The Asicell tracks the Phengoclept. By the light, I think. It uses the same signal to find its food that the Phengoclept uses to get around."

He's quiet for a second.

"That is elegant," he says.

From anybody else, that's a compliment. From Jinzhou, that's falling out of his chair. I'm writing down that I was there for it.

"It is," I say. "It also means the collection window really matters. The probe has to sit down there long enough for the Asicell to find it in real numbers. Not just a couple."

"How long."

"Longer than the protocol's minimum. Shorter than however long it takes the atmosphere to start doing interesting things to the ship." I hang up suit three and grab suit four. "ARACCSIM's been running approach simulations. Ask it for the new dwell time when you go back up."

He nods and pushes off the rack. Then he stops.

"Ethyn."

"Yeah."

He looks at me a second longer than he usually looks at things.

"Sleep tonight," he says. "Real sleep. In the bed."

"I'll try."

"Do not try. Do it."

He goes back to Lab C. I go back to my list.

---

Suit four is Bejors'.

I know before I check the name inside the collar. I can't say how. The cut of it, maybe. It's sized for a Canid Vulpes, a little different through the shoulders than my Amber Vulpes fit. I check the collar anyway.

BEJORS, B. / LEAD SCIENTIST.

I run the whole checklist on it. Every line. Seals through gloves, off the laminated card. She'd have wanted it done all the way, and she'd have complained the whole time. So I do it all the way, and I complain the whole time, quietly, to the suit.

It passes.

I log it. I hang it back exactly where it was. Then I just stand there for a moment with my paw on its shoulder. There's nothing left to check. It just takes me a second to decide to stop.

She packed this herself. Before the launch, before whatever part of my life I still can't find, she had a list of her own. Things to bring. And she packed a suit she knew she'd be wearing six point four light-years from home, to go look for something she'd already figured out on paper.

What was she thinking about, packing it?

I take my paw off the shoulder and go check suit five.

---

By the time I get back to Lab A, the afternoon has gone somewhere without asking me. The lights are drifting toward amber, the hour before ARACCSIM dims everything for night.

I pull up the approach simulation. It's thorough. I expected that. It's also quietly terrifying in a few places. I expected that too. I just hoped it would be less true once I saw the real numbers.

Okay. Let's go through it like a person.

The Stephfren line is the lowest a ship like ours is supposed to orbit Asimov-09D. Below it, the air starts to drag on the hull. Not deadly right away. It's just there. And it grows. To fight it, we have to keep thrusting, which means spending fuel to stay somewhere we're not supposed to be.

Fine. Expensive, but fine. Next problem: what stops us from cooking?

The Phengomotors.

I read this part three times, because it sounds like a joke. Below the line, ARACCSIM runs the motors slow. So slow they don't push us forward at all. They just brake. Each cell leaves the plate, flies a few meters ahead of the hull, dumps everything it's carrying, and burns. A wall of tiny, controlled bangs just in front of us, soaking up the heat so the hull doesn't have to.

So the heat shield is made of the thing we came here to study. Dying on schedule. Again.

And the simulation says a cell misfires about once every ninety minutes. Not zero. A cell that misfires doesn't burn where it should. It goes past us. And anybody who watched Kalen's steel cube turn into a puddle knows what a cell does when it finally lets go.

Okay. Next problem. The cable.

ARACCSIM modeled the cable four different ways. In three of them, the drag on the cable is fine. We can afford it. In the fourth, we can't, and the ship starts losing altitude faster than the word "manageable" can keep up with.

ARACCSIM named that one CONFIG 4. I'd have called it something with a lot more capital letters.

So what causes config four? I go through it piece by piece. Cable too long. Dropping too fast. A gust at the wrong time. I write a note next to each one. Avoid this. Make up for that. Watch the other thing and call it early.

Then I close the simulation and go get dinner. Looking at it a third time won't change the numbers. I checked. That was the third time.

---

The food's better than week one. Not good. Better. Whatever the processor does to the later meals, they're less aggressively beige. I eat at the Lab A bench, because I always eat at the Lab A bench, and eating anywhere else now would feel weird. That's either comfort or a habit specific enough to worry a psychologist. I've decided not to find out which.

Jinzhou eats in the cockpit. I can hear his tray click down on the armrest from the hallway. He does that sometimes. He watches the readouts while he eats, the way some people read. He doesn't really sit still without something to look at.

I think about the Asicell following the light. The winch running hot. Config four, and the gap between calling it early and calling it too late.

I think about Bejors, packing her suit.

At some point I notice I've finished eating. So the food was probably fine. My brain just had other plans.

---

I work until ARACCSIM dims the lights to seventy percent. That's its way of saying "you should stop" without actually saying it. Then I work forty more minutes, because the density model is almost done and I want to close it properly, not halfway through a thought.

When I finally close it, I don't go to the dorms.

I go to the cockpit.

Not to work. I told myself I'm not going to work, and for once I mean it. I sit in the left seat. It's been mine since day one.

Asimov-09D is on the main screen. It's always on the main screen. Brown and blue and huge. The blue shows up better now than when we first got here, because I've had three weeks to learn which filter shows what, and this one is the best for the methane bands.

Tomorrow we go below the Stephfren line. Tomorrow the probe goes down on the cable. Jinzhou flies the approach from here. I run the probe from the payload bay. ARACCSIM runs everything else, like always. And we find out whether all these numbers add up to something we live through.

I look at the planet for a long time.

It doesn't look back. It's a planet. It just turns, slow and patient and enormous, the way it's been turning since long before anybody here had a word for anything.

The Asicell are down there. A hundred to two hundred meters under the cloud tops, in the ammonia and the methane and the dark. Eating Phengoclept. Keeping a whole star alive, and not knowing a thing about it.

Bejors described them. She built the whole thing out of math and a good gut and a paper she finished four years before any of this happened to us. She was right about all of it.

She asked me to check a number once, and I said I'd look at it after. I don't remember if I ever did.

Tomorrow I go find it.

I sit with the planet a little longer. Then I turn off the screen and go to bed.

Real bed. In the dorms. With a pillow. Like a person who is definitely not about to lower a trash bag into a gas giant.

Jinzhou wins this one. I'm not telling him.

*END OF CHAPTER NINE*
