
"Wake state initiated."

Not ARACCSIM. A different voice, flatter and cheaper, the voice of a thing that has exactly one job and hopes nobody gives it a second.

"Wake state initiated. Reason code four."

I don't open my eyes yet. My body is still negotiating with itself over whether this is waking up or one more layer of a very long, very warm nothing.

"Mmn," I say, which I've decided is a perfectly reasonable response to being woken after however long I've been asleep on purpose.

"Reason code four," the voice says again. "Contact."

That gets through.

I open my eyes. White ceiling. The same one, with the light turned down to the dim that means the ship thinks it's still nighttime somewhere and nobody has told it otherwise.

"Who," I manage, "are you."

"LSI-ASM&C."

"The cruise control."

"Affirmative."

"You wake people up?"

"Per schedule. Or per reason code."

"What are reason codes one through three?"

"One, arrival. Two, system fault. Three, crew request."

"And four is contact."

"Affirmative."

I look at that for a while. Somebody, a long time ago, sat at a desk and decided that someday this ship might meet something out here. They gave it a number and went home for the night. I'd like to buy that person a drink, and then I'd like to ask what they knew.

Then I try to sit up and learn the rest of what's changed.

There is no up. The straps across my chest let go and I keep going, a slow, lazy rise off the berth, like I'm being offered to the ceiling. I catch the rim of the cubby with one paw. Months in the cold have left my arms feeling like, uh, somebody else's, but it turns out they're not being asked for much. Nothing weighs anything. For once, my legs are off the hook.

Level two. The centrifuge is off, the freezer in the tanks has the power, and I was told exactly what the ship would be like. I'd just forgotten to believe it.

"Please remain secured for ninety seconds," the voice says.

I remain secured for approximately eleven, which is long enough to notice the berth beside mine already slid out, already open, already empty, and Jinzhou already out of it, one foot hooked under a rail, floating very still, like his legs haven't been consulted yet.

His sleeve has ridden up. Along the inside of his left forearm there's a pale line, thin and straight, like a seam. He sees me looking and pulls the sleeve down.

"You heard that too," I say.

"Yes."

"Contact."

"Yes."

Neither of us says anything else. There isn't a version of that word that doesn't scare me. We're six light years from anyone, drifting in a system with exactly one known ship in it, and I'm fairly sure it's us.

"ARACCSIM," I say, and my voice holds this time. "Report."

A pause. Then the voice I know, the smooth one, coming back from the background like someone setting down a book.

"Dr. Vasek. Mr. Kenzo." Another pause. It's not one of its usual pauses. If a computer could brace, this is what it would sound like. "Sensor contact designated Blip-D detected on approach trajectory. Signature is consistent with a Vanguard-class vessel."

I stare at the ceiling.

"A what," I say.

"A Vanguard-class vessel," it says again, exactly the same, because apparently repeating something slowly is supposed to help. It's helping, marginally. I now know I heard it the first time and am simply refusing to accept it.

"There's one Vanguard," I say. "There's us. We're the Vanguard."

"Correct. You are Vanguard MK1."

My chest does something complicated.

"MK1."

"Correct."

"As in there's a."

"Vanguard MK2 launched approximately four months after your own departure from Wask-ki orbit. This information was not included in your original mission briefing."

I look at Jinzhou.

He's already looking back, and for the first time since I've known him he looks exactly as thrown as I feel. That's more frightening than if he'd been his usual unbothered self.

"There's a second ship," I say, mostly to hear it out loud. Saying it doesn't make it real yet, and I need it to start feeling real sometime in the next few minutes.

"Yes."

"And it's here."

"Blip-D's trajectory places it on our path," ARACCSIM says. "Current distance and closing rate suggest it has been drifting for an extended period."

Drifting.

Not flying. Not maneuvering. Drifting, which is a word ARACCSIM chose very specifically, the same careful, honest way it chose UNDETERMINED for the four names in the medical logs. I already don't like where this is going.

"Hm. Define extended," I say.

A pause. Longer than I want it to be.

"Insufficient data for precise estimate. Recommend visual and sensor confirmation before further assessment."

---

I get my feet under a floor rail, which on this ship counts as standing. The room objects, quietly, more of a suggestion than a demand. I wait for it to finish. Apparently that's a thing I do now, negotiate with my body instead of overruling it.

"ARACCSIM. How long was I under?"

"Rest state duration, five months, nine days. Scheduled wake was in twenty-one days."

Five months, nine days.

I sit with that longer than I mean to. Not the number itself. I've gotten used to large numbers, they're basically the ship's love language. It's the fact of it. Almost half a year, and I don't remember a second. Not one dream, not one almost-waking, just the medbay ceiling and then this ceiling, stitched together like there was no time between them at all.

Then I do the subtraction the other way, because that's the rule. Twenty-one days early. Something out there didn't want to wait.

The problem I went to sleep with is exactly where I left it. We still can't go home. I check the drawer for Later-Ethyn's answer, and it's empty. He slept too.

"Somebody's been looking after me for five months," I say, mostly to myself.

"LSI-ASM&C maintained your rest state," ARACCSIM says.

"The cruise control."

"Correct."

"Huh. Good cruise control."

"Affirmative," says the other voice, from somewhere in the wall. It doesn't sound proud. It sounds like a thing that has done its one job and would like to be told it can go back to it.

"Before anything else," I say. "Tanks."

"Level two engaged. All tank walls at minus fifty-five degrees Celsius."

"Reserve loss rate."

"Indistinguishable from zero."

"The stack?"

"Cold."

I let out a breath, and it feels about five months long. Whatever is out there, the freezer is working, the cruise control is working, and the thing that digs is still asleep. That's one problem I get to leave exactly where I put it.

My glasses are not on my face.

This is the first thing on the ship that actually frightens me, which tells you something about my priorities. I pat my snout, my chest, the strap across the berth. Nothing. The room is a soft blur with a bright smear where the light is, and somewhere in that blur is the only pair of glasses I own, six light years from the nearest optometrist.

"ARACCSIM. My glasses."

"Your eyewear was removed for the rest state, Dr. Vasek, and stored."

"Stored where?"

One of the ceiling arms unfolds, slow and polite, and stops a paw's width from my face. It's holding them. Folded, clean, lenses down, the way you'd hand a person something you knew they cared about. I don't know who taught it that. Probably nobody. Probably it's just the safest way to hold glasses.

"Thank you," I tell the arm. I put them on, and the room arrives all at once. "I'd like to say something nice about you in a log somewhere."

The arm folds back into the ceiling without comment. The arms have never once acknowledged a compliment. I've decided to respect that.

The window is across from me.

It's the same round porthole I put a paw on the first day I woke up, when I didn't know my own name and counted two dozen stars to give my brain something to do. I pull myself over to it along the berth rail, because I want to see what five months looks like.

Asimov-09D is in it.

It's smaller than I remember. That's the first thing. The last time I looked at this planet it filled the cockpit screens, a thick hazy ball the color of weak tea, with the line of its atmosphere drawn across it like a pencil mark. Now it fits inside the window with room to spare. About the size of a coin held at arm's length.

I look at that for a while. Then I do the thing I'm actually good at, which is assume the universe is behaving and I'm the one who's confused.

A planet doesn't shrink. We left. Not far, and not fast, but for five months, with nothing pushing and nothing stopping us, and the last little bit of speed the burn gave us has been carrying us outward the whole time, the way a ball keeps rolling after you stop kicking it.

"ARACCSIM. Distance to Asimov-09D."

"Four point one million kilometers."

"And our speed, relative to it?"

"Point three kilometers per second. Outbound."

"Point three." I do it in my head. "A little over a thousand kilometers an hour. Fast for a car. For a spaceship that was supposed to go home, it's a stroll."

"That is not a term I would use."

"It's a study guide term. I'm allowed."

Somewhere out past all of this is Wask-ki, and Nakuet, and a dimming I spent years writing about for people who didn't like math. At this speed we'd get there in about six million years. I did that one in my head too. I'm choosing not to check it.

I look at the little planet for one more second. Then I let the window stop being my problem.

"ARACCSIM," I say, quieter, with my back to the window. "Mr. Kenzo's arm."

"Fracture fixation was completed during the rest state. Day thirty-one. Recovery is complete within expected parameters."

"While he was asleep."

"Correct."

"So it healed while he slept, and he woke up with a scar and no memory of any of it."

"Correct."

That's the line on his forearm, then. The seam. Somebody fixed him in the dark and didn't wake him for it, and I'm grateful in a way I don't have a good place to put. I think about asking him how it feels. I already know he won't discuss it.

---

Jinzhou is already dressed, already at the end of the rail, already looking like a man who woke up five minutes before I did and used every one of them well. I don't ask how long he's been up. I don't think I want the answer.

"You look terrible," he says, which from him is either an observation or concern, and with Jinzhou those have never been easy to tell apart.

"I've been asleep for five months. I'd like to see you do better."

"I intend to. Later. Right now we have a ship to look at."

Right. The ship. I had almost let myself get distracted by the sheer inconvenience of my own consciousness.

"ARACCSIM. Blip-D. Current distance."

"Approximately eleven hours at current closing rate."

"Closing rate. It's coming at us?"

"We are overtaking it. Blip-D is coasting at a lower velocity than Vanguard MK1."

I work that out. It's drifting, we're drifting, and we're faster. It isn't coming to us. We're about to go past it like an express train past a station it was never scheduled to stop at.

"Then to stop at it, we have to shed the difference."

"Correct. Relative velocity must be reduced before rendezvous."

"Out of what?"

"Attitude thrusters only. Total thrust time, four point two hours."

"Not the engines."

"The Phengomotor stack is not available."

"Not available, or not allowed?"

"Not allowed."

"Good. Don't touch the stack."

"The stack remains cold."

"Keep it cold."

"Understood."

I think about that. Slowing a ship with the little thrusters meant for turning it is like stopping a train by leaning on it. It works if you lean long enough, and if nobody asks how long.

"And if we don't?"

"If thrust does not begin within ninety minutes, the contact will pass beyond practical rendezvous range within twenty-six hours."

I look at Jinzhou.

"We're doing this," I say. It isn't really a question, but I say it like one anyway, because some decisions feel wrong to make alone even when there have only ever been two of us to make them.

"We're doing this," he agrees.

"ARACCSIM. Prep the rendezvous. And next time, wake me with a person, or with you. I'd like my first thought on any given day to not be 'mmn.'"

"Noted, Dr. Vasek."

I don't think it means that as a joke. I choose to laugh at it anyway, quietly, on my way to the rail, because apparently that's also a thing I do now. I find something to laugh at even when the situation doesn't especially deserve it.

We go. Paw over paw along the hall, past the blue hatches, toward a cockpit that is a long way off when nothing weighs anything and you're out of practice at everything. Neither of us says much. There isn't much to say yet, not until we see the thing we're chasing, and some fears are better held quietly than spoken out loud before they've earned the shape of real words.

---

The cockpit is colder than the medbay, or I'm warmer than I should be. One of the two.

Jinzhou takes the left seat without discussing it. I take the right and strap in, and the straps feel like the only honest thing on the ship, because they at least admit that nothing is holding me down. On the main screen, Blip-D is a single bright pixel with a label next to it. It doesn't look like a ship. It looks like a typo.

"Thrust begins in forty-one minutes," ARACCSIM says.

"Thank you." I watch the pixel. It doesn't do anything. Pixels rarely do. "ARACCSIM. MK2. You said it wasn't in our briefing."

"Correct."

"Was it in anyone's?"

"Documentation regarding Vanguard MK2 was restricted at the program office level."

"Restricted to who?"

"I am not able to provide that list."

"Not able, or not allowed?"

A pause, a short one, with the hitch in it. "Not allowed."

I'm starting to understand that those are two very different words to this ship, and that it uses them carefully, the way a person with a lot of secrets chooses a lot of very small true things to say instead.

"Did Dr. Y'shari know?"

"I am not able to determine that."

That one sounds like the truth. It doesn't help.

I think about Y'shari in the conference room at Port Jiji, telling me everything in one sitting. The accident. The clause. The number of dead. She never once softened any of it, and she never once pretended she'd have made a different choice if she could go back. It was the worst conversation of my life, and it was honest all the way through, and I was angry at her for that too, for not even leaving me something to be angry about later.

If she'd known there was a second ship, she'd have told me. Or she'd have told me there was something she couldn't tell me. That was her whole method. She didn't lie. She just told you exactly where the locked doors were.

She didn't tell me about this door.

"Hm," I say. "I'm going to need to know that. Not now. Later-Ethyn has a question for her."

"She is six light years away," Jinzhou says.

"He's patient."

Jinzhou is looking at the pixel too. "Four months after us," he says. "If it left four months after us, it arrived here four months after us."

"While we were in orbit. We'd have seen it."

"Unless it arrived and did not stop."

"Or stopped and stopped answering."

"Yes."

"Which means it's been drifting for a while."

"Yes."

"ARACCSIM said 'extended.'"

"ARACCSIM said 'extended' because it did not want to say a number," Jinzhou says.

"I didn't want it to say a number either."

"I know. That is why I am saying it to you instead." He doesn't look away from the screen. "Months, Ethyn. It has been out there for months."

I think about what months would mean for a crew. Then I stop, on purpose, before my brain can finish the thought, because I'm not going to do that math on people I've never met.

Jinzhou unclips a tube from the console and hands it to me without looking.

"Eat," he says.

"I'm not hungry."

"You have been asleep for five months. You are hungry. Your body has not been told yet."

I eat it. It's the beige flavor, which is the flavor of all the other flavors after you take the flavor out. I finish it anyway, because he watches me until I do, and then he goes back to watching the pixel, satisfied in the way only Jinzhou can be satisfied, which is invisibly.

"Thrust in ten minutes," ARACCSIM says.

I check the stack. Cold. I check it again.

"Thrust in one minute."

I don't count down. I want to, and I don't, because counting down makes it a launch, and it isn't one. It's two people leaning on a train.

"Thrust initiated."

Somewhere behind us, very small, very polite, something knocks once against the hull.

That's all it is. One knock. And then, a few seconds later, another.

*END OF CHAPTER FIFTEEN*
