
The bar smells like fryer oil and spilled beer and somebody's cheap cologne, and none of that matters because the soup is good and the company is better. There's a game on the screen above the taps that nobody's watching. Somebody's phone keeps buzzing on the table two seats down and nobody claims it.

"Transit was delayed. It sucked."

"Heard." A page turns. "Soup's still bad, by the way."

"That's why I went to the market."

He looks up then. Looks at the bag. Back to the book. Cracked spine, dog-eared past the point of being precious about it, read enough times that the plot doesn't matter anymore, just the act of being in it again.

"What'd you get."

"Actual soup. Among other things."

"Mind if I bum a can off ya."

"I don't."

He puts the book down, face-first, spine up, the universal signal of someone who fully intends to come back to it. "I'll get bowls, then."

Bowls. Two of them, mismatched, one chipped at the rim, set down without a word because that's just how it goes between us. No production. Just up, bowls, done. The soup's not great. The company more than covers for it. Somewhere behind the bar a radio's playing something neither of us could name if asked, low enough to be furniture rather than music.

---

"Sixty marks," he says, not looking up from the paperwork on the workbench between them. Metal shavings everywhere, some tool half-disassembled at his elbow. "You're not getting out of this one."

"I don't even remember what for."

"You will. Or you won't. Either way you owe me."

He's grinning when he says it. That's the part that matters. The number doesn't matter. The grin does, and so does how he goes right back to the tool afterward, like the debt's settled just by being said out loud and collecting was never the point.

---

Bejors at a whiteboard, chalk dust on her paws, gesturing at something with total conviction, believable before it's understood.

"The Phengoclept doesn't care about our timelines," she says. "That's the mistake everyone keeps making. It's not waiting for us to catch up."

"Neither are you."

"Someone has to not wait."

I'm laughing before I decide to. Head tipped back. Eyes closed. Helpless, and it arrives before permission does. She keeps talking through it, doesn't even pause for the laugh, just builds the next point on top of the one that caused it, because for her the two things were never separate. The joke was the argument. The argument was the joke.

---

A card, handmade, block letters uneven like a child who has only just learned the shapes. DR MAMA. A drawing underneath that might be a stethoscope or might be a snake, hard to tell, doesn't matter, because it was made with both paws and no doubt at all about what it was for.

She's holding it like it might break. Turning it over once, twice, checking something she already knows is fine.

"They're getting good at their letters," she says, mostly to herself.

There's nobody else in the room for her to be saying it to. She says it anyway.

---

A field somewhere. Tall grass, gone gold at the tips, right before it turns. One arm slung around a shoulder, peace sign up, not posed, thrown because the camera happened to be there and being happy happened to be easy that day, easy enough that nobody thought to make it mean anything more than it already did.

He's laughing at something off-frame. You can tell even without hearing it. A laugh that takes over the whole face.

---

A cafeteria. Fluorescent light, a tray half-finished, a fork paused halfway to a mouth that's already grinning at whatever someone off-camera just said. It's not a big moment. It's not supposed to be. That's what makes it land: the ordinary weight of a joke good enough to interrupt a meal.

---

A group photo, later, all of them in matching tracksuits, the ship behind them looking smaller than it has any right to look given what it actually is. Somewhere in the back, a head shorter than everyone else, trying to read something on a clipboard and failing to look at the camera at all.

An arm slung around that shoulder from the side. A grin aimed straight at the lens that says the photo was his idea, and it probably was, and it probably wasn't the first one he'd talked everyone into that week.

"Get in the shot properly," he says, off to the side, to someone who clearly isn't listening. "Come on. One good one."

Nobody remembers if the photo turned out how he wanted. It exists now as it exists, which might be better anyway.

---

"You have detailed intelligence on literally everyone," someone says, except that's wrong, that's not this memory, that's a different room, a different year, and it doesn't belong here and it surfaces anyway because nothing in here is sorting itself properly, the static bleeding sideways into rooms it has no business touching.

---

Rooftop. City below, unfamiliar, lit gold with late light that makes everything look like it means something whether or not it actually does.

She's looking at the camera.

I'm looking at her.

Neither of us knew, until we saw the photo, that it was being taken at all. That's the whole reason it works. Nobody was performing anything. It just happened, and somebody caught it happening, and now it exists forever exactly as it actually was.

---

The soup's gone. The bar's loud. Somebody's laughing two tables over about something that has nothing to do with any of this.

"You're back," he says. Doesn't look up from the book.

"Yeah."

"Good."

That's the whole conversation. That's all it needed to be. Some friendships run on volume, constant talking, constant checking in. This one never did. This one ran on somebody getting bowls without being asked.

---

A tool cage, later, some other day, cluttered like a workspace someone is actually using instead of keeping tidy for inspection. He's got a panel open on something, paws moving without looking, the economy of someone who's done this exact motion a thousand times.

"You gonna help or you gonna stand there."

"I'm supervising."

"That's not a real job."

"It's the job I've assigned myself."

He doesn't look up. Just holds a paw out, palm up, and waits, and the wrench gets placed in it before either of them says another word. Some things you learn to anticipate without being told. This was one of them.

---

A whiteboard, again, later, more crowded now, annotations stacked on annotations in handwriting that's gotten faster and messier as the ideas outpace the time it takes to write them down.

"You're not going to like this part," she says.

"Tell me anyway."

"The model breaks down past a certain density. I don't know why yet."

"Then we find out why."

"That's what I said an hour ago."

"Say it again. I like hearing you say it."

She looks at me for a second, the look of someone deciding whether that was a joke, and then goes back to the board without answering, which is somehow an answer.

---

Quieter, this one. No whiteboard. No argument to win. Just a hallway, late enough that the building's mostly emptied out, the lights gone to half so somebody, somewhere, is saving on the electric bill.

She's sitting on the floor outside the containment lab, back against the wall, knees pulled up, staring at nothing in particular.

"You should be home," she says, not looking over.

"So should you."

"I don't have anywhere better to be."

"Neither do I, apparently."

She doesn't laugh at that. Doesn't need to. Just shifts over a few inches to make room on the floor, which is an invitation, and doesn't say anything else for a while, and neither do I, and somehow that's the whole point of it.

---

Static, for a while. Not silence. Static. The texture of a signal that's present but not resolving into anything, a radio between stations, a memory that hasn't decided which shape to take yet.

Then, slower this time, more reluctant, like something being dragged up from a depth that doesn't want to give it back:

A field.

A cafeteria tray, forgotten mid-bite.

A group photo, a shoulder, an arm slung sideways, don't remember if it turned out.

A rooftop, gold light, a photo neither of them knew was happening.

A whiteboard, crowded, handwriting getting faster than the thoughts behind it.

A hallway floor, late, shared silence, no words needed.

A tool cage, a wrench passed paw to paw without looking.

A card. DR MAMA. Block letters.

A bowl of soup, mismatched, one chipped.

Sixty marks and a grin, and a debt neither of them ever really meant to collect.

None of it in order. None of it needing to be. It just keeps surfacing and receding, like tide, indifferent to whether anyone's watching, indifferent to whether anyone's ready for it to stop.

---

Something changes.

Not a memory. Not a fragment. Something underneath the static, low at first, easy to mistake for part of the same texture if you're not paying attention.

It doesn't stay background.

It gets a shape. A rhythm. Not a voice. Not quite. Something more mechanical than that, insistent in a way none of the memories have been, cutting through the field and the rooftop and the whiteboard like a paw through water, displacing everything around it without asking permission.

The static thins.

The field pulls back first, the gold light going flat and grey at the edges, the grass losing its color before it loses its shape. Then the cafeteria, the fork frozen mid-air dissolving into nothing, the tray and the table and the fluorescent hum all going with it. Then the hallway floor, the shared silence unraveling into something with actual texture again, cold instead of comfortable. Then the whiteboard, the crowded annotations losing their edges, losing their meaning, becoming just marks on a surface that isn't there anymore, that maybe was never really there to begin with, not here, not now.

The alarm is not distant anymore. It has a rhythm to it now, insistent, evenly spaced, the cadence of something designed to be impossible to sleep through.

I try to open my eyes. They don't. The thing holding me down is heavier than an alarm, and warmer, and it has my left arm.

The alarm resolves into words.

"Contaminant detected."

Then another voice, closer, the same flat recorded calm.

"Mr. Kenzo. Remain in rest state. Treatment is incomplete. Dr. Vasek is being woken."

I try to say his name. I get as far as the first letter.

"Rest well, Mr. Kenzo."

The static comes back, softer than before. Somewhere in it a paw is held out, palm up, and a wrench is placed in it, and nobody says another word about it.

I stay under.

*END OF CHAPTER TWELVE*
