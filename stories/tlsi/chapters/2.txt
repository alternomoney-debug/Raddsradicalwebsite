
"Hello?"

It comes out small. I didn't mean it to. I clear my throat and try again, with what I'd like to think is more authority.

"Hello. Hi. Um. Anyone?"

Less authority. Somehow less.

Nobody answers. The room hums. The arms stay folded against the ceiling. Five beds sit in their cubbies, five shapes under five translucent hoods, and none of them so much as twitches an ear.

"Okay. Okay, that's, that's fine. You're asleep. Everyone's asleep. I was asleep too, a, a minute ago, so I get it. I'm not judging. I'm just, I'm going to, uh, wake somebody up, if that's alright, and if it isn't alright, you can tell me, by waking up."

Nobody tells me anything.

I go to the closest one, because closest is the only plan I've got. Middle bed, my row, right under mine. A vulpes, a little smaller than me, lying with her paws folded on her chest like someone arranged them that way. Her face is mostly hidden by the hood. What I can see of it looks peaceful, which is more than I can say for mine.

"Excuse me," I say. "Ma'am. Hi. Sorry. I don't, I don't know your name. I don't know my name either, so we're even, and I'd love it if you could, um."

I put a paw on her shoulder. Gently. Like she's a stranger on a train and we've missed our stop.

Nothing.

I shake her a little harder. The berth doesn't move. She doesn't move. The suit makes the same soft plastic noise mine makes, and that's the only noise there is.

"Okay. Deep sleeper. That's fine. That's a, that's a personality trait."

I cross the room to row one, mostly by bouncing between beds like a pinball with a doctorate. Top bed: a big wolf, so big the cubby looks borrowed. I can't reach his shoulder without climbing, so I knock on the edge of the berth instead, which is not a thing you do to a bed, but I'm out of ideas that are things you do to beds.

"Sir. Hi. Big guy. Up and at 'em. There's, I need, somebody needs to tell me what's going on, and you look like a guy who knows what's going on."

He looks like a guy who is asleep.

Middle bed: a serval, slight, with her ears flattened under the hood in a way that looks uncomfortable. Bottom bed: a stocky canine with a jaw like he's about to argue with somebody. I say hello to both of them. I say it louder the second time. I say it a third time at a volume I'd normally reserve for fire alarms and people who back into my parking spot.

Nothing. Nobody.

Their cubbies feel cool when I put a paw against the frames. Cooler than mine was, I think, though I can't swear to it, since I spent most of my time in mine unconscious or being told I'd failed a math test. Cold makes sense, I tell myself. Coma sleep. Everything slowed down. Everything turned way down low.

That's what I tell myself, and I don't check anything else, because I don't want to.

"Computer," I say, to the ceiling. "Hi. Hello. You. Wake them up."

"Crew wake schedule is not available."

"I didn't ask if it was available. I'm asking you to make it available. I'm, I'm not asking. I'm telling. Wake them up."

"Crew wake schedule is not available."

"Then who has it? Give me the person. Give me the, the whoever. The manager. I want the manager of the beds."

"Please remain calm."

"I AM calm! This is calm! This is what calm looks like on me, apparently, nobody told me, I just found out!"

I'm aware that I'm yelling at a ceiling. I'm aware it doesn't help. Knowing a thing doesn't help is a different skill from not doing it, and I don't seem to have the second one.

I go back to the vulpes in my row, because she's closest and because I've decided, without consulting anyone, that she's the one who'll know things. There's a bundle of lines running from her berth into the wall, the same kind I just had pulled out of me. I find the panel where they connect. There are buttons on it. I don't know what they do. I'm about to find out.

An arm unfolds from the ceiling and puts itself, very calmly, between my paw and the panel.

"Please step away from the medical platform."

"Get out of the way."

"Please step away."

"I'm trying to help her!"

"Please step away."

It doesn't push. It doesn't grab. It just stays there, patient and unmovable, a crossing guard made of surgical steel. And something in me, some low part that's been sitting very still since I found out I don't have a name, stands up and loses it completely.

There's a little cup on the ledge by the berth. Paper. Empty. I throw it at the arm. It bounces off with a sound like nothing at all.

There's a roll of medical tape. I throw that too. It hits a joint and rolls under the bed, which feels like a win and isn't.

I look for something else and there isn't anything else, so I pull off one of my socks, which takes longer than it should because of the toes, every single toe has to be negotiated with individually, and I throw it at the arm with everything I've got.

It catches on the wrist joint and hangs there.

A pale ribbed sock, with five little toe pockets, dangling off a surgical robot.

The arm considers it. Then it goes back up to the ceiling and folds itself away, sock and all, like it's decided the matter is closed and I've made my position clear.

"YEAH," I yell after it. "YEAH, THAT'S RIGHT."

I don't know what I'm agreeing with. I sit down on the floor.

---

I stay on the floor for a while. The floor and I have history now. It's the only relationship I've had that's gone well.

My breathing slows down. My heart slows down. The sock stays where it is, up on the ceiling, a tiny flag of surrender that isn't mine.

I take stock, because I seem to be the kind of person who takes stock. One sock. One suit. One pair of glasses. Five people who won't wake up. One computer that won't help. One ceiling arm that now owns a sock.

And the floor. The floor's heavy. Or I'm heavy. It's hard to tell from down here, but everything I lift weighs a little more than my arm thinks it should. Not a lot. A little. The cup didn't fall the way I expected when I threw it. It went down too quick, like it was late for something.

I have no idea what that means. I put it in a pile. The pile is called Things That Are Wrong, and it's getting tall.

That's the thing I'll work out later, when I have time to work things out on floors. There's a difference between distress and panic. Distress is not knowing what's happening and not being able to stop it. That's what this is. That's the yelling and the sock. Panic is different. Panic is when you know exactly what's happening, and you still can't stop it.

Right now I'm only doing the first one.

I'd like to stay there.

---

An arm comes down. The other one, not the sock one. It's holding a tube, like a toothpaste tube, and it holds it in front of my face with the confidence of something that has never once been told no.

"Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat."

"I just threw a sock at your coworker. I'm not in a, a snacking frame of mind."

"Eat."

I look at the tube. I look at the arm. The arm doesn't blink, because it doesn't have eyes, which somehow makes it more intimidating. At least a person can be worn down by a good stare.

"Fine."

I take it. There's a label.

VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST
MEAL: 1/3 / DAY: 1

I stare at that for longer than I probably should.

Vasek. E.

It doesn't ring anything. It doesn't not ring anything, either. It's like hearing a song from the next room and knowing you know it before you know what it is.

"Vasek," I say out loud, testing it.

It fits. Not perfectly. Like a coat somebody else has been wearing.

I squeeze the tube. Something close to gravy and potatoes hits my tongue, and I chug the whole thing in about four seconds. I'm not going to pretend it wasn't the best thing I've ever eaten. Admittedly, the sample size is one.

The arm takes the empty tube back, which is more cleaning up after myself than I've done so far today.

"Computer. My name is Vasek."

"Partial confirm. First name required."

"It's E. Something E."

"Partial confirm. First name required."

"You have it on a label. You have it on a label, and you're, you're giving me a pop quiz about it."

It doesn't answer, which I'm starting to understand is how it wins arguments.

Secondary scientist, though. That's something. I'm a scientist. I'm the secondary one. Modest ambitions, but I'll take employment I already have over unemployment I don't remember applying for.

Day one, the label says.

Day one of what, it doesn't say.

---

There's a ladder on the far wall, running straight up to a hatch in the ceiling.

I stare at it longer than I should.

I know where it goes. The lab. Past that, the cockpit. I know it the way you know the layout of a house you grew up in, without knowing how I know it, which is a deeply unpleasant thing to know.

Except it's wrong. The ladder should be on the floor. My brain keeps trying to correct it and keeps failing, filing an appeal with a court that has no jurisdiction here.

I go up with one sock on and one sock off. It's about fifteen feet and it takes longer than it should. Every rung is a small argument I win by a narrow margin, and I'm counting each win as a personal triumph, because at this point I need them.

The lab isn't shaped like a room. It's a shaft. Narrow, tall, going up further than I can see, and down to a metal grate a few feet below me, the kind you'd see rolled over a storefront. Here it's doing double duty as a floor and a door, and I'm not going to think too hard about which one it's supposed to be. Below the grate the shaft continues, research equipment bolted along the walls all the way down. Above me, more shelving, more storage, everything strapped and latched like the builders assumed the whole ship might tip sideways and wanted a fighting chance when it did.

Right in front of me, at whatever currently counts as eye level, are an oven and a sample fridge, both mounted on gimbals that keep them level no matter which way the ship points. Small mercies. At least dinner rotates with the craft.

Microscope. Autoclave. Sample fridge. Oven. I know what everything is and how to use all of it. Useful, and it tells me almost nothing about who I am. I get to keep the skills and lose the person who earned them. That's a very specific kind of unfair.

There's a whiteboard on the far wall, mounted ninety degrees off, so whatever's written on it runs top to bottom instead of left to right. Handwriting, all of it running sideways. I tilt my head to read it. Not neat, not messy, somewhere in between. Someone wrote this quickly but carefully, like they wanted it to be readable by somebody who wasn't them.

At the top, underlined twice:

Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP

Below that, a diagram. An arc, rising from a point I somehow know is Nakuet, curving outward and downward toward something labeled ASI-06A. The arc widens at the end like a funnel. Annotations everywhere. Wavelength readings. Density estimates. A note in smaller writing that says not visible, IR detection only, see attached data.

I stare at the diagram.

Something moves in the back of my head.

---

The desk is mine. I know because of the coffee cup in the corner and the stack of papers I've been meaning to sort for three weeks. The screen in front of me is halfway through a document I've been writing longer than I want to admit.

The phone is on speaker.

"So the arc itself," I say, typing. "It's, uh, not moving toward the system or away from it?"

"Neither. Sort of." Her voice is clear and unhurried, the voice of someone who has already had this exact conversation with three other people today and is having it a fourth time as a courtesy. "It is fixed relative to Nakuet. Which is what makes it interesting. Everything else in the system moves. This does not."

"And the Phengoclept is what's causing it."

"The Phengoclept is the arc. Or rather, the arc is what the Phengoclept leaves behind when it migrates. Think of it like the Day-Star rockets. You always see an arc from them."

I type that down. A behaviour. I underline it.

"That's not detailed enough for a study guide."

"It's not detailed enough for me either," she says. "That's why I'm giving you twenty minutes instead of five."

I lean back. Outside my window it's early morning, still grey, heavy snow, same apartments as always, same city that's been slowly, patiently freezing for as long as I've been alive in it.

"Y'shari-line," I say. "That's what they're calling it."

A pause on her end. "I've heard that. I don't love it."

"It's accurate."

"It's my name on something I don't fully understand yet." Flat. Not looking for sympathy, just stating a fact she'd rather not have to state. "I'm going to be the thing people hear when they think of the space dots for the rest of my career, whether I've earned that or not."

I don't push on it. Y'shari answers questions on her own schedule or not at all.

"All right. I'm writing a study guide, not an article about your name. So the baseline for the book is: someone picks this up and they know nothing. What do I tell them?"

She thinks for a moment. I can hear her thinking. It's the silence that means she's working through it, not stalling.

"Tell them Nakuet is losing something it shouldn't be able to lose. Tell them we found the thing taking it. Tell them we don't know why it's taking it or where it's going or whether it knows we're here."

I type all of that.

"Well, we already know that," I say.

"You know the part I've told you. I know considerably more than that, most of which isn't for a study guide."

That lands somewhere I don't examine too closely. It's not the first time she's drawn a line between what I'm allowed to have and what she actually knows. I've never once stopped to ask why she keeps taking these calls at all, if there's this much of it I'm not cleared for.

"Not very good for a book," I say instead.

"It's the book you're qualified to write." Not unkind. Just precise. "I have a meeting in a few minutes. I'll catch you around, E-"

The line goes dead.

I'm folding a shirt I don't remember owning into a bag I don't remember packing. The TV is going in the corner with the sound turned low, on for the noise more than for anything I'm watching.

"And for anyone just joining us," the anchor says, her voice pitched slightly too bright for whatever's actually on the paper in front of her, "scientists are calling it simply the Y'shari-line, named for the researcher who first identified it two years ago. In plain terms, it's a faint band of light stretching out from Nakuet, and like every other line of its kind that's ever been observed, it's been getting steadily brighter while the star it's attached to gets steadily dimmer."

I keep folding. Half listening, the background kind.

"Today, for the first time, a probe is close enough to actually sample whatever's producing it, and in just a few minutes, we're expecting the first images back from its onboard instruments."

I stop folding.

Not because of anything she said. Because of the name in the corner of the screen, small, in the guest-expert graphic under a live video feed, a Kanadian research office behind her.

Y'shari.

Same voice I was just on the phone with. Same everything, except now she's not talking to me, she's talking to the entire world, calm, composed, doing the thing where she sounds certain even when I happen to know for a fact she isn't.

"What we've mapped so far is a pattern," she's saying, and something in her settles once she's talking about the work instead of performing for a camera. "Every star we've observed with a line like this shows the same behavior. The line appears, the star dims a little, and then the line gets brighter and the star dims a little more. Every one of them. We haven't found a single one that stopped."

"And where is that," the anchor asks.

"In our own system, the line runs from Nakuet toward the second planet out, Aluk'. Hot, thick atmosphere, exactly where something might go to breed." She pauses, choosing the next part carefully. "Something is feeding on Nakuet's output, converting it, storing it, and eventually carrying it off to reproduce there. What we're hoping this probe tells us, for the first time, is what that something actually looks like up close."

"And the concern," the anchor says, "for people watching at home."

"The concern," Y'shari says, "is that ours has been doing exactly what all the others did for two years now, which either means we're earlier in the curve than the models say, or."

She doesn't finish the sentence.

Somewhere off camera, not quite quiet enough for the microphone to miss it, a voice says a short sharp "oh, come on," followed by a second voice, younger and considerably less composed, blurting out something about the number on a screen nobody at home can see.

It isn't a dramatic stop. Not one built for television. Something behind her attention shifts, off camera, a monitor or a feed I can't see, whatever's coming through on the other side of that microscope. In about half a second it takes the composure right off her face and replaces it with something I don't have a name for yet and will spend a very long time trying to find one for.

"I'm sorry," she says, already half turned from the camera, "I have somewhere else I need to be."

The feed cuts back to the anchor, faintly confused, already moving on.

I stand there with a shirt in my paws. Something in my chest sits there being uneasy about it for a lot longer than the segment lasted.

I go back to packing.

---

I'm back in the lab, blinking at a whiteboard.

Nobody tells me I went somewhere. There's nobody here to tell me.

"E," I say. She was about to say it. She always called me by the first letter when she was in a hurry, like the rest of my name was a formality she'd fill in later. "E. Eli? Ezra? Ethan. No. Close. Ethyn."

It comes out strange, like trying on coats I haven't worn in years and finding one that still fits.

"Ethyn Vasek."

I say it to the whiteboard, since the whiteboard was the only one who helped.

---

The ladder keeps going above the lab, up to another hatch. I don't decide to climb it. My paws are on the rungs before the rest of me has voted.

There's a panel beside the hatch.

IDENTIFY TO PROCEED.

"Ethyn Vasek," I say, and I'm proud of it. First try. No sedation. Look at me go.

A pause.

"Identity confirmed. Who is your co-pilot? Please state their name."

"I don't, I don't have one. They're asleep. Everyone's asleep. It's just me."

"Cockpit access requires two confirmed crew."

"There's only one confirmed crew. I'm the confirmed crew. I'm right here. I'm confirming."

"Cockpit access requires two confirmed crew."

I put my forehead against the hatch. It's cool and it doesn't care about me, and right now that's the most honest thing on the ship.

I climb back down.

---

There's a console on the far table in the lab. I didn't notice it the first time because I was busy having a memory at a whiteboard.

It wakes up when I touch it. A login I don't need, apparently, because it lets me straight in. Menus. Dozens of them. All of it in a language I can't read. Tall narrow letters, an unfamiliar rhythm to the words, Latin-derived, I'm almost sure, which means I can sound it out and still have no idea what I'm saying. I sound out the first menu anyway. It's a noise with nothing behind it.

But not everything is words.

Some of the menu icons are pictures. One of them is a little camera. I press it, because the alternative is pressing things at random, and I've already done that once today and lost a sock.

A grid of feeds fills the screen. External cameras. Most of them show black, or the hull, or stars. One of them shows a star.

Not a point. A disc. Bright, filtered down so it doesn't burn the sensor, a pale gold circle in the middle of the frame with a soft glow around the edge. We're pointed at it. We're going toward it. I don't know how I know that, except that the feed is labeled with an arrow, and arrows, thank god, are arrows in every language.

I look at it for a while.

It's a star. That's fine. Stars are fine. We have one at home. We have a perfectly good star at home called Nakuet, and I know a lot about it, because apparently I wrote a book about it, and I'm looking at a star now, and the obvious conclusion is that this is Nakuet, and we're somewhere in our own system, and everything is going to be fine.

There are spots on it.

Darker patches, scattered across the face. Sunspots. I lean in. The camera feed has a little bar along the bottom, a timeline, and when I drag it backward the image changes. It keeps an archive. Frames going back days. Each one has a timestamp, and timestamps are numbers, and numbers are numbers in every language too.

I pick a spot. A big one, near the middle, with a smaller one trailing behind it like a duckling. I drag the timeline back until the spot is right at the left edge of the disc, just coming into view. I write the timestamp on the sleeve of my suit with a marker from the tray. There's no paper. Plastic takes marker. Fur doesn't. I'm learning so much today. Then I drag forward and follow the spot across the face until it reaches the middle.

I write that time down too.

Half the visible face, edge to middle. That's a quarter of a full turn. Multiply by four. Correct for the angle, roughly. I know how to do this. I know how to do this the way I know how to push my glasses up. It's in my paws.

Thirty-two days.

Give or take. Call it thirty-two.

I know Nakuet's rotation period. Twenty-seven point two five days at the equator. I know it the way you'd know your own phone number, if you knew your own phone number. It's on page one of the study guide. It's the first fact I give people, because it's an easy one, and easy ones make people feel like they're going to understand the rest.

Twenty-seven.

Not thirty-two.

I did the math wrong. That's the first possibility, and I'd love it to be the right one, so I do it again. Different spot. One near the top, smaller, harder to follow. I track it frame by frame. I write the times on my sleeve, under the first ones.

Thirty-two days.

Third spot. Third set of numbers, now running up to my elbow. I'm a scientist. I'm a secondary scientist. I'm good at this.

Thirty-two.

I look at the numbers on my sleeve for a long time.

I want to tell you I yelled. I yelled at a ceiling twenty minutes ago because a computer wouldn't wake someone up. I threw a sock. This is a much bigger problem than a sock, and I don't yell at all. I don't have the breath for it. Something has sat down on my chest, very calmly, and it isn't getting up.

This is the other one. This is panic.

I know exactly what I'm looking at. That's the problem. I'm looking at a star that isn't mine. And the stars outside the window downstairs didn't have any sky behind them, and the floor's too heavy, and the label says day one, and the beds won't wake up, and every single one of those things that was in the pile called Wrong just slid into one shape, and the shape is this:

I'm not home.

I'm not anywhere near home.

Stars aren't neighbors. They're islands. The light from one takes years to cross to the next. If that isn't Nakuet, then home is so far behind us that the light leaving it right now, this second, while I sit here with numbers written up my sleeve, won't get here until I'm older. A lot older.

The arc on the whiteboard runs from Nakuet to ASI-06A.

I think I'm sitting at the far end of the arc.

I know it, and I can't stop it, and I can't undo it, and I can't go back down the ladder and un-know it. I sit there and let it be true, because there's nothing else it can be.

"Amazing," I say. It comes out very quiet.

The star on the screen keeps turning. Thirty-two days at a time.

---

I don't know how long I sit there.

Long enough for the marker on my sleeve to dry. Long enough to look at the numbers so many times they stop being numbers and turn back into ink.

Then something ticks, below me.

Metal on metal. Small and rhythmic, coming up through the hatch from the room with the beds, gone before I can work out what it is.

I don't move. My heart is doing something it doesn't need to be doing this early in being alive again.

It comes again. Not a footstep. More like something set down carefully and picked back up, over and over, patient and unhurried.

The arms. The arms are moving. I know the sound now. I spent a whole morning getting it done to me.

I'm down the ladder faster than I came up, half climbing, half falling, one sock catching on every rung. I hit the floor of the medbay and nearly go over and catch myself on the end of a berth that isn't mine.

Row two. Bottom bed. The arms are working on someone down there. Unhooking things. Brisk and indifferent, same as they were with me.

The canine sits up.

Not dramatically. He gets about halfway, stops, considers the situation, then finishes the job. I stand across the room with my heart trying to leave through my throat and numbers written all the way up my sleeve, and I think, with total clarity:

Oh. There's another one of us.

He looks over. He sees me staring at him like he's a ghost. Then his eyes go up, to the ceiling, where a single pale toe sock is still hanging off the wrist of a surgical arm.

He looks at the sock for a while.

Then he looks back at me.

"Hi," he says.

It comes out flat and hoarse and entirely unbothered, and it's the best sound I have ever heard in my life.

*END OF CHAPTER TWO*
