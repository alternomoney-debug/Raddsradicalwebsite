Day Nine, and the arm doesn't hand me a tube.

I notice before I understand why. It comes down the same way it always does, precise, unbothered, and instead of the familiar squeeze pack it's holding something with actual weight to it. A tray. Sealed lid, condensation on the inside like it's been sitting somewhere warm, which for a ship that's spent the last week feeding me gravy through a straw feels borderline provocative.

I stare at it. I am aware, distantly, that I am drooling slightly, which is not a dignified thing to notice about yourself but here we are.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Is this a joke."

"No."

"This isn't a tube."

"Correct. Nutritional transition to solid rations begins Day Nine per standard protocol. Digestive readiness confirmed."

I peel the lid back like it might argue with me. It doesn't. Something that I think is rice. Something that resembles a baked potato and fry bread. Something I genuinely don't have a category for, filed under later along with everything else I'm not equipped to process right now, but it looks better than the tube goop by an embarrassingly wide margin, and my stomach makes an executive decision on the matter well before my brain gets a vote.

I rip into it.

Jinzhou's already got his open. Genuine smile on him too, which for Jinzhou is roughly the emotional equivalent of a parade, and he picks up the little collapsible fork like a man who's decided I'm eating a bit too feral for the occasion and would prefer not to be associated with it.

"You're not going to comment," I say, mouth mostly full.

"On the food?"

"On the fact that we've been drinking gravy through a straw for eight days and now there's rice, and you're just, what, eating it. Calmly. Like a person."

"I noticed." He chews. Unhurried. "I'm choosing not to make it a thing. I don't waste breath on useless comments."

"That's a very you way to handle this, I suppose."

"It's food, Eat it. Ethyn.."

I eat it. It's good, solid and warm ish, and after eight days of tube paste it might as well be the finest meal prepared by anyone anywhere in the history of anyone preparing anything. I go through it faster than I mean to.

"Slower," Jinzhou says, not looking up. "Your stomach's been asleep for five years. It doesn't know what speed to eat at yet."

"You're one to talk. You're on your second bite in four seconds."

"I'm pacing myself. You're inhaling."

I slow down. Mostly because he's right, which is annoying, and partly because the alternative involves finding out the hard way in a small enclosed spacecraft with limited plumbing, and I'd rather not gamble with my guts on Day Nine of knowing I have guts.

"Can I ask you something," I say, once the tray's mostly empty.

"You're going to regardless."

"The fruit bars. Day one. We probably weren't supposed to eat those either, were we."

He considers it for a few moments, the way he considers most things, like he's checking the math before committing to an answer out loud.

"First of all: Body's coming out of five years of medically induced stasis. Second of all: Digestive system hasn't processed solid food in that long. We've been eating from gravy tubes. Random unlogged snack, no nutritional profile on file, no idea what preservatives are in it." A pause. "Yeah. Probably should not have eaten that."

"We didn't get sick."

"We got lucky."

"Comforting."

"I try."

I look at the empty tray. At the little stenciled label on the corner, VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST / MEAL: 1/5 / DAY: 9, same font, same everything, except now it means something different than it did eight days ago. Eight days ago it meant you exist, apparently. Now it just means breakfast, which is honestly a promotion.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "How much food do we actually have on this ship."

"Provisioning was calculated for six crew members across a projected ninety day mission window, plus fifteen percent contingency reserve."

I do the math out loud, mostly because doing math out loud is apparently a habit I had before I had a name to go with the habit.

"Six people, ninety days, fifteen percent extra. That's." I stop. "That's a lot of food for two people."

"Correct."

"How much is 'alot'?"

"At standard individual rationing for two crew members, current stores would last approximately three hundred and ninety days."

Jinzhou looks up from his tray for the first time since the conversation started, which is how I know the number actually landed for him too.

"Say that again," he says.

"Approximately three hundred and ninety days."

"That's just over a year," I say.

Something in my chest loosens before I've even finished the sentence. A year of food. For two people. That's not a countdown, that's breathing room, that's the first genuinely good number I've heard since I woke up not knowing my own name.

"If rationed further," ARACCSIM continues, unprompted, the way it does when it has more to say and no particular interest in whether you wanted it, "stores could be extended significantly beyond that figure. Reduced caloric intake, extended meal spacing, and rationing as though for four crew members rather than two would yield an estimated seven hundred to eight hundred days of supply."

Nobody says anything for a second.

The relief is still sitting there, half formed, and then it isn't, because I've done the math on the math, and so has Jinzhou, I can tell by the way he's gone very still over his tray.

Provisioning was for six. Six people eat six people's worth of food. That's how you get to a year for two of us at the numbers ARACCSIM's giving. Unless the food was never actually split six ways to begin with.

Unless four people stopped eating a long time before we woke up.

"Why four," I ask anyway, because I need to hear it said out loud, even if I already, uh, know. "Why would you ration like there's four of us."

"Contingency planning is a standard protocol recommendation. It is not a statement regarding current crew status."

"That's a very careful way of not answering the question."

"It is!" Jinzhou chimes in, but there's no weight behind it. He's not eating anymore. He's looking at his tray like it's become a different tray than it was thirty seconds ago, which I understand, because mine has too.

Neither of us says the actual thing. El'kian's rations. Aria's. Nelson's. Bejors'. Eight hundred and ninety something days of meals that never got eaten, sitting in a store room somewhere on this ship, because four people went into that dormitory and didn't come back out of it.

That's what a year of food for two people means. Not a mystery. A number of people who aren't here to eat their share.

"Two thousand one hundred days," Jinzhou says eventually, quieter than before.

"I know."

"We don't have two thousand one hundred days of food. Not even close."

"I know that too."

"So either the food problem solves itself before then," he says, "or we're not planning on being here that long."

I don't have a good answer for that, so I don't give one. I put the tray in the little dishware slot by my bed, and it hums and does something to it I don't ask about, and I sit there for a second longer than I need to, thinking about a year of meals that belonged to four people who never got to have them.

We spend the rest of the morning doing the thing we should have done on day one, which is actually map the ship instead of learning it one traumatic room at a time, a system I would like to formally retire.

It's Jinzhou's idea, mostly. He's got a notepad from somewhere, actual paper, and he's drawing the ship in cross section with the flat, unhurried confidence of someone who's either done this before or was simply born knowing how to make a floor plan look correct on the first try, which honestly tracks.

"Medical bay," he says, drawing a box on the left. "Lab." A box next to it. "Cockpit." A box at the far end. He taps the pen against the page. "Straight line. Three rooms."

"There's more than that."

"I know. That's why I'm drawing it."

We go through together this time, no ladder races, no one person finding things without the other one knowing, a rule I've decided we're implementing retroactively and enforcing from here on out. The lab looks different in the daylight that isn't daylight, less like a crime scene and more like a room two people are actually allowed to be in now. The whiteboard's still there. Bejors' handwriting. I don't look at it for very long, on account of not being emotionally prepared to before noon.

"There," Jinzhou says.

He's found a hatch I walked past twice already without registering it as a hatch, tucked into the lab wall beside the old ladder, which now runs flat along the floor like a railing nobody asked for, flush enough with the paneling that it reads as decoration until you know to look for the seam.

"How'd you spot that."

"I wasn't looking at the whiteboard."

Fair. Extremely fair, actually.

The hatch doesn't have a lock. No ID panel, no dual key, nothing, just a manual latch, same as most of the others, and it gives easily, like it's used to being opened by people who aren't thinking too hard about it.

Behind it: a narrow vertical shaft, shorter than the others, maybe eight feet, servicing rungs bolted into one wall. Cool air comes up out of it, a different temperature than the rest of the ship. Strange, I think, filing it under things to be strange about later.

"Ladies first," Jinzhou says. He's starting to get a sense of humor. That's nice, actually, genuinely, I'm choosing to be touched by it.

"You found it."

"You're smaller. Less likely to get stuck."

"That's not the flattering reason I was hoping you'd give."

I go first anyway.

The room at the bottom of the shaft is not large. It doesn't need to be. Three walls of it are rack mounted server towers, floor to ceiling, humming with a low, steady tone that I decide to interpret as friendly rather than ominous, mostly because I've had enough ominous for one week. The air is noticeably colder in here, dry enough it makes the fur on my head and paws want to lie flat.

A small stenciled plate on the nearest rack reads: VANGUARD ARCHIVE / PRIMARY STORAGE / LSI MK1.

"Huh," Jinzhou says, which from him is roughly equivalent to another person falling to their knees in awe.

There's a terminal built into the wall across from the racks, a proper one, not just a screen, with a physical keyboard and a chair bolted to a rail in front of it. I sit down before I decide to. Jinzhou stands behind me, reading over my shoulder, which I've started to understand is just how he participates in things, quietly, from slightly above and behind, like a very literal guardian angel.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "What is this."

"Vanguard Archive. Primary long term data storage for the vessel. Redundant offline storage for corrupted local drives, or extended mission duration beyond original parameters."

"Extended mission duration," I repeat.

"Correct."

I tap the terminal awake. It takes a second, longer than I expect from something this cold and this quiet, and then the screen fills with a directory tree that does not end where I expect it to end. It keeps going. Folders inside folders inside folders, labeled in a shorthand I don't fully recognize but can mostly guess at.

MEDIA_ARCHIVE_FULL. REFERENCE_LIBRARY_MIRROR. PERSONAL_STORAGE [6]. MISSION_DATA. TECHNICAL_SPEC.

"How much is on here," I ask.

Jinzhou leans past me, taps a few keys with the unbothered efficiency of someone who has apparently done this exact motion a thousand times in a life he doesn't remember living. A summary window pops up. Total capacity. Used space.

He reads the number out loud. Then reads it again, slower, like the first read might have been wrong, which I appreciate, because it means I'm not the only one having trouble with it.

"That can't be right," I say.

"It's right."

"That's not, uh, that's not a normal amount of storage for a two person science mission. That's a suspicious amount of storage."

"It's not for the mission." He scrolls. "REFERENCE_LIBRARY_MIRROR. This isn't mission data. This is everything."

I look closer. He's right. It's not a science archive, or not only a science archive. It's an entire mirrored copy of what looks like the sum total of a civilization's written knowledge, encyclopedias, digitized libraries, some kind of full internet backup with a timestamp on it from before any of this happened to us, video files by the tens of thousands, audio, books, maps, music.

"They gave us the whole internet," I say, mostly to myself, "in case we forgot how to use a fork."

"In case we forgot everything," Jinzhou says. "Not just the fork."

I sit with that for a second. Six people, sent six point four light years from home with no guarantee of ever seeing it again, and someone back at Argon LSI division sat down and thought, what if they wake up and don't remember any of it, and then backed up all of the species onto a hard drive and bolted it into the wall.

That's either the most thoughtful thing I've ever heard of, or the saddest. I haven't decided which yet. Possibly both. Possibly it's allowed to be both, actually, now that I think about it.

"PERSONAL_STORAGE," Jinzhou reads. "Six folders."

I look at the screen. VASEK. KENZO. BEJORS. EL'KIAN. ARIA. NELSON. Same six names as the lockers. Same six names as the airlock log.

Neither of us moves to open one.

"We should probably," I start.

"Not yet," Jinzhou says.

"Not yet," I agree, faster than I mean to, relieved that he said it first so I didn't have to be the one deciding we're not ready.

I close the directory before I can think too hard about why my own folder, sitting right there, three feet away, one click deep, feels like the scariest thing on the entire ship, scarier than the airlock, scarier than the dead crew, scarier than the number two thousand one hundred.

"Later," I say.

"Later," he agrees.

We back out of PERSONAL_STORAGE and don't discuss it again.

MISSION_DATA is safer ground, or at least it feels that way, right up until it doesn't, which is turning into something of a pattern for this ship.

It's not organized the way I'd organize it, which either means someone else built this archive or past me had genuinely terrible filing habits, a possibility I am not ruling out, given the state of the papers in my own locker. Raw sensor logs. Spectral readings going back years, cross referenced against systems I don't recognize by name yet. A folder just called PHENGOCLEPT_OBS with what has to be thousands of files in it.

"This is going to take a while," Jinzhou says.

"This is going to take the rest of the trip."

"Good thing we have the rest of the trip."

I open one of the files more or less at random. It's a spectral reading, timestamped years before either of us woke up, taken from somewhere that isn't Asimov, isn't Nakuet, isn't anywhere I recognize by name. A wavelength signature. Annotations in a hand I don't recognize either, clinical, precise, nothing like Bejors' whiteboard scrawl.

"This isn't even our data," I say. "This is somebody else's fieldwork. Predates the mission."

"Reference material."

"A lot of reference material."

I keep scrolling. The scale of it starts to actually land somewhere around the two hundredth file. Whoever built this ship didn't just send two, then six, people six point four light years on a hunch. They sent years of institutional knowledge with them, pre loaded, redundant, backed up against something nobody wanted to say out loud they were expecting.

"They didn't think we were calling home," I say.

Jinzhou doesn't answer right away.

"No," he says eventually. "I don't think they did either."

It's Jinzhou who finds the actual work, in the end, the thing that gets us out of the server room and back into our own bodies instead of staring at a screen full of other people's data until our eyes give out.

"The collector," he says.

"What collector."

"Diagnostic list. Day one. Asimov Payload Sample Collector." He's already climbing back up the shaft. "It's been running passively since we came into orbit. Nobody's looked at what it's actually collected."

That gets me moving faster than anything else has all day, including breakfast.

The collector interface is back in the lab, a small subsystem panel I'd walked past three times without registering, next to the microscope station. According to the log, it's been quietly doing its job this whole time, skimming trace particulates out of Asimov-09D's upper atmosphere on a slow automated cycle, filtering them into a sealed sample chamber nobody's opened yet.

"There's a sample waiting," I say, reading the log. "Has been for two days."

"Get it."

I do. The extraction's simple enough, a sealed vial that clicks free of the chamber with a small hiss of released pressure, contents invisible to the naked eye, which is exactly the problem.

I sit down at the microscope. It's a beautiful piece of equipment, or it would be, if it turned on.

"It's not reading anything," I say.

Jinzhou comes over, looks at the display, which is showing exactly nothing, a dark screen where an image should be.

"Powers off," he says. "Or the plug to the thing isn't seated right. Could be either."

"Can you fix it?"

"Probably."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not a no either." He's already got a panel open on the base of the microscope, small tools appearing from somewhere on his person the way they seem to for him, methodical, unbothered, the same energy I assume the logs from when we woke up was talking about.

"Give me a minute."

I get up and walk away, giving him about four minutes alone. More than asked, honestly, but I've decided it's the polite thing to do. He works with the quiet, total focus of someone who genuinely finds this relaxing, which I'm starting to suspect he does, tracing a line, checking a connector, muttering something about a wire being crossed with another. I don't understand most of what he's doing. I don't need to. That's sort of the point.

"There," he says, eventually. "Try it now."

I look through the eyepiece. The display flickers, resolves, and suddenly there's something there, a field of shapes too small and too strange to be dust, structured in a way raw particulate matter has no business being structured.

"Jinzhou."

"What."

"I need you to look at this."

He looks. For a long moment he doesn't say anything, which from him is its own kind of reaction, possibly the loudest one he's given me all day.

"That's not rock," he says.

"Yup, I'd assume so."

"That's not anything I'd call rock."

"Yeah, I wouldn't call this rock either," I say, and I can hear my own voice doing the thing it does when I've stopped being tired and started being someone who cares about a problem, a fairly annoying vocal register I've noticed I have. "I think this is Phengoclept trace material. Actual material, from out here, not a culture grown back home off one lucky probe. I think this is the first sample anyone's ever pulled out of this system."

Jinzhou looks at the display. Then at me. Then back at the display.

"Well," he says. "Get to work, then."

"You're not going to help?"

"I fixed the microscope. That's the help." He pulls a stool over anyway, sits down next to me, close enough that his shoulder's nearly against mine, and doesn't get up. "Tell me what I'm looking at, though. In order. I want to keep up."

So I do. I start at the beginning, the way I would if I were writing it down for a book nobody's asked me to write yet, and Jinzhou listens the way he does everything, quiet, exact, filing it away somewhere I can't see, and for a little while neither of us thinks about the food, or the folders we didn't open, or the three hundred and ninety days ticking down somewhere above our heads.

We work until the lights dim for ship's night, and neither of us notices until they do.

*END OF CHAPTER FOUR*
