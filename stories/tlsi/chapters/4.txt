
Day nine, and the arm doesn't bring me a tube.

I notice before I understand why. It comes down the way it always does, precise and unbothered, but instead of the usual squeeze pack it's holding something with actual weight to it. A tray. Sealed lid. Condensation on the inside, like it's been sitting somewhere warm.

I stare at it. I'm aware, distantly, that I'm drooling a little. Not a dignified thing to notice about yourself. Here we are.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "Is this a joke?"

"No."

"This isn't a tube."

"Correct. Nutritional transition to solid rations begins Day Nine per standard protocol. Digestive readiness confirmed."

I peel the lid back like it might fight me. It doesn't. Something I think is rice. Something like a baked potato, and a piece of fry bread. Something I don't have a category for. All of it looks better than tube paste by an embarrassingly wide margin, and my stomach makes the decision well before my brain gets a vote.

I dig in.

Jinzhou's already got his open. He's smiling. An actual smile. For Jinzhou that's roughly a parade. He picks up the little folding fork like a man who has noticed I'm eating a bit feral and would like to not be associated with it.

"You're not going to say anything?" I say, mouth mostly full.

"About the food?"

"About how we've been drinking gravy through a straw for eight days and now there's rice, and you're just, what, eating it? Calmly? Like a person?"

"I noticed." He chews. Slowly. "I do not make it a thing. I do not waste breath on useless comment."

"That's a very you way to handle this."

"It is food. Eat it, Ethyn."

I eat it. It's good. Solid and warm, more or less, and after eight days of paste it might as well be the best meal in history. I go through it faster than I mean to.

"Slower," Jinzhou says, without looking up. "Your stomach is sleeping six years. It does not know the speed yet."

"Has been asleep," I say. "Your stomach has been asleep."

He looks at me over his tray for a long moment.

"I know what I said," he says.

I go back to my rice. Fair.

"You're one to talk," I say. "That's your second bite in four seconds."

"I am pacing. You are inhaling."

I slow down. Mostly because he's right, which is annoying. Partly because the other option is finding out the hard way, in a small spaceship, with limited plumbing. Somewhere in the back of my head, a voice I can't place tells me to chew. I chew.

"Can I ask you something?" I say, once the tray's mostly empty.

"You will ask anyway."

"The fruit bars. Day one. We probably weren't supposed to eat those either, were we?"

He thinks about it. He thinks about most things like that, checking the math before he says anything out loud.

"First: body is coming out of six years of medically induced stasis. Second: digestive system has not processed solid food in that long. We are eating from gravy tubes. Random snack, not logged, no nutritional profile, no idea what preservatives." A pause. "Yes. Probably we should not have eaten that."

The technical words come out of him perfectly. It's the little words in between he still has to go and find.

"We didn't get sick."

"We got lucky."

"Comforting."

"I try."

I look at the label on the corner of my tray. VASEK, E. / SECONDARY SCIENTIST / MEAL: 1/3 / DAY: 9. Same font, same everything. Eight days ago, that label told me who I was. Now it just means breakfast. That's a promotion, honestly.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "How much food do we actually have?"

"Provisioning was calculated for six crew members across a projected ninety-day mission window, plus fifteen percent contingency reserve."

I do the math out loud. I've apparently had that habit since before I had a name to attach it to.

"Okay. Six people times ninety days. Five hundred and forty person-days. Plus fifteen percent. That's, uh, eighty-one more. Six twenty-one, total." I stop. "Six hundred and twenty-one days of food, for one person. Split between two of us..."

"Approximately three hundred and ten days," ARACCSIM says, "at standard individual rationing for two crew members."

"Three hundred and ten," I say. "Yeah. I was getting there."

Jinzhou looks up from his tray for the first time, which is how I know the number landed for him too.

"Say again," he says.

"Approximately three hundred and ten days."

"That's most of a year," I say.

Something in my chest loosens before I've even finished saying it. Most of a year of food. That's not a countdown. That's room to breathe. It's the first good number I've heard since I woke up.

Then I look at the math again. The six. The two.

So does Jinzhou. I can tell, because he's gone very still.

Ninety days of food, packed for six people. Two of us eating it. It stretches to most of a year because four of the six aren't here to eat.

He's not eating anymore. He's looking at his tray like it turned into a different tray in the last thirty seconds. I get that. Mine did too.

Neither of us says it. El'kian's meals. Aria's. Nelson's. Bejors'. Ninety days each, sitting in a storeroom somewhere on this ship, because four people went to sleep in that medbay and didn't wake up.

That's what most of a year of food means. Not a lucky surplus. A count of people who aren't here to eat.

"Two thousand one hundred days," Jinzhou says eventually. Quieter.

"I know."

"We do not have two thousand one hundred days of food. Not close."

"I know that too."

"So the food problem solves itself," he says, "or we are not here that long."

I don't have a good answer, so I don't give one. I put the tray into the little dish slot by my bed. It hums and does something I don't ask about. I sit there a second longer than I need to, thinking about months of meals that belonged to four people who never got to have them.

---

We spend the rest of the morning doing what we should have done on day one: actually mapping the ship, instead of learning it one awful room at a time.

It's Jinzhou's idea, mostly. He's got a notepad from somewhere, real paper, and he's drawing the ship in cross section with the calm confidence of someone who's either done this before or was born knowing how to make a floor plan look right on the first try.

"Medical bay," he says, drawing a box. "Lab." A box next to it. "Cockpit." A box at the far end. He taps the pen on the page. "Straight line. Three rooms."

"There's more than that."

"I know. That is why I draw it."

We go through together this time. No ladder races. Nobody finding things without telling the other one. I've decided that's a rule now, effective retroactively. The lab looks different now that it's sideways and we're both allowed to be in it. Less like a crime scene. The whiteboard's still there. Bejors' handwriting. I don't look at it for long. Not before lunch.

"There," Jinzhou says.

He's found a hatch I walked past twice without registering. It's tucked into the lab wall beside the old ladder, which now runs along the floor like a railing nobody asked for. The hatch sits so flush with the panels that it looks like decoration, until you know to look for the seam.

"How'd you spot that?"

"I was not looking at the whiteboard."

Fair.

No lock on it. No ID panel. No dual key. Just a manual latch. It opens easily, like it's used to being opened by people who aren't thinking too hard about it.

Behind it is a narrow shaft, short, maybe eight feet, with rungs bolted to one wall. Cool air comes up out of it. Colder than the rest of the ship. Cold air out of a hatch is either a fridge or a very bad idea.

"Ladies first," Jinzhou says.

He's starting to make jokes. That's nice. I'm choosing to be touched by it.

"You found it."

"You are smaller. Less stuck."

"That's not the flattering reason I was hoping for."

I go first anyway.

The room at the bottom isn't big. It doesn't need to be. Three walls of it are server racks, floor to ceiling, humming a low, steady note. I'm choosing to hear that as friendly. I've had enough ominous for one week. The air is cold and dry. The fur on my paws wants to lie flat.

Cold and dry, for computers. Makes sense. Heat's the enemy of electronics, and dry air means no condensation on the boards. Somebody thought about this room.

A stenciled plate on the nearest rack reads: VANGUARD ARCHIVE / PRIMARY STORAGE / LSI MK1.

"Huh," Jinzhou says. From him, that's roughly falling to his knees in awe.

There's a terminal built into the wall across from the racks. A real one, with a physical keyboard, and a chair on a rail in front of it. I sit down. Jinzhou stands behind me, reading over my shoulder. I've started to figure out that's just how he takes part in things. Quietly. From slightly above and behind.

"ARACCSIM," I say. "What is this?"

"Vanguard Archive. Primary long-term data storage for the vessel. Redundant offline storage for corrupted local drives, or extended mission duration beyond original parameters."

"Extended mission duration," I repeat.

"Correct."

I wake the terminal up. It takes a second. Then the screen fills with a folder tree that doesn't end where I expect it to. It keeps going. Folders in folders in folders, labeled in a shorthand I can mostly guess at.

MEDIA_ARCHIVE_FULL. REFERENCE_LIBRARY_MIRROR. PERSONAL_STORAGE [6]. MISSION_DATA. TECHNICAL_SPEC.

"How much is on here?" I ask.

Jinzhou leans past me and taps a few keys without even looking for them, like he's done this exact thing a thousand times in a life he doesn't remember. A summary window pops up. Total capacity. Used space.

He reads the number out loud. Then he reads it again, slower, like the first time might have been wrong. I appreciate that. It means I'm not the only one having trouble with it.

"That can't be right," I say.

"It is right."

"That's not a normal amount of storage for a science mission. That's a suspicious amount of storage."

"It is not for the mission." He scrolls. "REFERENCE_LIBRARY_MIRROR. This is not mission data. This is everything."

I lean in. He's right. It's not a science archive. Or it's not only one. It's a mirror of what looks like everything anybody's ever written down. Encyclopedias. Digitized libraries. A full backup of the internet, with a timestamp from before any of this happened to us. Video files by the tens of thousands. Audio. Books. Maps. Music.

"They gave us the whole internet," I say, mostly to myself. "In case we forgot how to use a fork."

"In case we forget everything," Jinzhou says. "Not only the fork."

He doesn't look at me when he says it. Of the two of us, he's the one who woke up without his English.

I sit with that. Six people, sent six point four light-years away with no promise of ever coming home. And somebody at Argon sat down and thought, what if they wake up and don't remember any of it? And then they backed up the entire species onto a hard drive and bolted it to the wall.

That's either the most thoughtful thing I've ever heard of or the saddest. Maybe it's allowed to be both.

"PERSONAL_STORAGE," Jinzhou reads. "Six folders."

VASEK. KENZO. BEJORS. EL'KIAN. ARIA. NELSON. Same six names as the lockers. Same six as the airlock log.

Neither of us moves to open one.

"We should probably-" I start.

"Not yet," Jinzhou says.

"Not yet," I agree, way too fast. I'm relieved he said it first, so I didn't have to be the one who decided we're not ready.

I close the folder tree before I can think about why my own folder, right there, one click away, feels like the scariest thing on the whole ship. Scarier than the airlock. Scarier than two thousand one hundred.

"Later," I say.

"Later," he agrees.

---

MISSION_DATA feels safer. Right up until it doesn't, which is turning into a pattern on this ship.

It isn't organized the way I'd organize it, which means either someone else built this archive or past-me had terrible filing habits. I'm not ruling that out, given the papers in my locker. Raw sensor logs. Spectral readings going back years, cross-referenced against systems I don't recognize by name. A folder just called PHENGOCLEPT_OBS with thousands of files in it.

"This takes long time," Jinzhou says.

"This is going to take the rest of the trip."

"Good. We have the rest of the trip."

I open a file more or less at random. A spectral reading, timestamped years before either of us woke up, taken from somewhere that isn't Asimov, isn't Nakuet, isn't anywhere I know. A wavelength signature. Notes in handwriting I don't recognize either. Neat, clinical. Nothing like Bejors' whiteboard scrawl.

"This isn't even our data," I say. "This is somebody else's fieldwork. From before the mission."

"Reference material."

"A lot of reference material."

I keep scrolling. Somewhere around the two hundredth file, the size of it really lands. Whoever built this ship didn't just send six people out on a hunch. They sent years of work along with them. Preloaded. Backed up twice. Backed up against something nobody wanted to say out loud they were expecting.

"They didn't think we'd be calling home," I say.

Jinzhou doesn't answer right away.

"No," he says eventually. "I do not think so either."

---

It's Jinzhou who finds actual work for us, in the end. The kind that gets us out of the server room and back into our own bodies, instead of staring at other people's data until our eyes give out.

"The collector," he says.

"What collector?"

"Diagnostic list. Day one. Asimov Payload Sample Collector." He's already climbing the shaft. "It runs passive since we come into orbit. Nobody looks at what it collects."

That gets me moving faster than anything else has all day. Breakfast included.

The collector panel is back in the lab, next to the microscope station. Small. I've walked past it three times. According to the log, it's been quietly skimming particles out of Asimov-09D's upper atmosphere this whole time, on a slow automatic cycle, and filtering them into a sealed sample chamber nobody's opened.

"There's a sample waiting," I say, reading the log. "Has been for two days."

"Get it."

I do. A sealed vial clicks free of the chamber with a little hiss of pressure. Whatever's inside is too small to see, which is exactly why there's a microscope next to it.

I sit down at the microscope. It's a beautiful piece of equipment. Or it would be, if it turned on.

"It's not reading anything," I say. "Fudge."

Jinzhou comes over and looks at the display. It's showing nothing. Just dark, where an image should be.

"Power is off," he says. "Or plug is not seated right. Could be either."

"Can you fix it?"

"Probably."

"That's not a yes."

"It is not a no either." He's already got a panel open on the base, and little tools have appeared from somewhere on him, the way they seem to. "Give me a minute."

I give him about four. I get up and wander off, because hovering over somebody while they fix things is rude. He works with this quiet, total focus, like he finds it relaxing. I'm starting to think he does. He traces a line. Checks a connector. Mutters something about two wires being crossed. I don't understand most of it. I don't need to. That's kind of the point of having him.

"There," he says. "Try now."

I look through the eyepiece.

The display flickers, settles, and suddenly there's something there.

Little dark shapes. Dozens of them, scattered across the field. Roundish, but not perfect circles. I check the scale bar in the corner. Each one is a few microns across, maybe ten. About the size of a cell.

Dust comes in every size and shape. Sharp edges, flakes, splinters, clumps. These don't. They're all about the same size. All about the same shape. When something comes out of nature in a hundred identical copies, that's not a rock. That's a design. Not somebody's design. Just, the kind of sameness you get when a thing makes copies of itself.

"Jinzhou."

"What."

"I need you to look at this."

He looks. For a long moment he doesn't say anything. From him, that might be the loudest reaction he's had all day.

"That is not rock," he says.

"Nope. I don't think so either."

"That is not anything I call rock."

"I wouldn't call it rock either," I say, and I can hear my voice doing the thing it does when I stop being tired and start caring about a problem. It goes up. It's a little annoying. I can't stop it. "Same size. Same shape. Over and over. I think this is, uh, I think this is Phengoclept. Real Phengoclept, from out here. Not something grown back home off one lucky probe sample. I think this is the first sample anyone's ever pulled out of this system."

Jinzhou looks at the display. Then at me. Then back at the display.

"Well," he says. "Get to work, then."

"You're not going to help?"

"I fixed the microscope. That is the help." He pulls a stool over anyway and sits next to me, close enough that his shoulder's nearly against mine, and he doesn't get up. "Tell me what I am looking at. In order. Slow. I want to keep up."

So I do. I start at the beginning, like I'm writing it down for a book nobody's asked me to write. Jinzhou listens the way he does everything. Quiet. Exact. Taking all of it in. And for a while neither of us thinks about the food, or the folders we didn't open, or the three hundred and ten days ticking down over our heads.

We work until the lights dim for ship's night. Neither of us notices until they do.

*END OF CHAPTER FOUR*
