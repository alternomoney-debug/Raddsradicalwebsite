
## Day One

The phone rings at six AM on a Tuesday. I'm still in bed. I answer it anyway, because I always do, and I have never once in my life learned anything useful from that habit.

"Meet me at the airport parking lot," Y'shari says. "Five PM. Tuesday."

"That's today."

"Yes."

"Y'shari, it's six in the morning."

"Five PM." She says it again, slower this time, spacing the words out like she's reading off a script she half-memorized. "Please."

She doesn't say please. Not to me. Not like that.

"Alright," I say.

The line goes dead. I sit up, bare paws on the cold floor, phone still in my paw, and I stare at the wall for a while without actually looking at anything on it.

I don't get back to sleep. I make coffee I don't drink. I sit at my desk and open the document I've been writing for three weeks and read the same paragraph four times without any of it landing.

At some point the sun comes up. I don't remember deciding to get dressed.

---

The airport parking lot is grey and freezing and smells like exhaust and old cigarette smoke. Y'shari is standing next to a car I don't recognize, in a coat I've never seen her wear. Dark. Unfamiliar cut. Bought recently, in a hurry.

I don't ask.

She presses an envelope into my paw before I've fully stopped walking.

"Don't open it here," she says.

"What is it."

"A ticket. Some others. A number written on a card."

"Y'shari."

"The flight leaves at seven forty-five." She isn't looking at me. She's watching the terminal entrance, scanning it. "When you land, find a payphone downtown. Not your mobile. A payphone. Call the number on the card. Say your name and where you're standing. Someone will come."

"Someone."

"Someone I trust." She finally looks at me straight on. Numbers already run, answer already hated, decision made anyway. "I'm sorry I can't tell you more here. I'm sorry about a lot of things, actually." A pause. "Go home. Pack a bag. Don't tell anyone."

"You're, uh... you're scaring me, Y'shari..."

"Good," she says. "You should be a little scared. It'll keep you moving."

She gets back in the car. She doesn't wave.

I stand in the parking lot a long time after she drives off. Snow starts, thin flakes catching on my fur, melting on contact. The envelope is already in my coat pocket.

I don't remember putting it there.

---

Arktos Airport at seven thirty in the evening is loud enough that being nobody is easy. I buy a coffee and drink it too fast. First one I've finished all day. Technically progress. I find my gate. I sit with the envelope in my lap and don't open it.

The ticket is for Port Jiji. I've been once, years back, a conference on Phengoclept dispersal modeling I mostly remember for the bad hotel coffee. A city that never finished being built and is proud of that fact, cranes still stuck on the skyline, permanent fixtures nobody ever bothered to take down.

The other tickets are stapled together, dated in sequence. Four of them. The card has a number in her handwriting. 51-333-27466.

A government number. A type of number I've only ever seen printed in PSAs, warnings about evacuation routes or contaminated water, the numbers a voice reads out fast at the end of a broadcast when something's actually wrong. Not something I'd expect a colleague to just have sitting around, memorized well enough to write down from memory on the back of a boarding pass.

I decide not to read anything into it. I read quite a lot into it.

I fold everything back into the envelope and board the plane.

---

Port Jiji from the air is a smear of orange light against black harbor water. I land, get my bag, and go looking for a payphone, which takes twenty minutes longer than it should because nobody uses payphones anymore and the one I finally find is wedged behind a bus shelter with a cracked receiver and a sticker for a business that closed years ago.

I call the number. It rings twice.

"Name and location," a voice says. Not a question. A format.

"Ethyn Vasek. Port Jiji central block. Mayson Street near building 1957, behind the bus stop."

"Forty minutes," the voice says, and hangs up.

A vehicle pulls up exactly on time, dark plated, unmarked except for a small LP Defense Solutions decal near the rear window. The window comes down. A raccoon, broad-shouldered, uniform collar too tight for his neck, looks at me.

"Vasek."

"That's me."

"Get in. Name's Wyaine, by the way, in case you were gonna spend the whole drive wondering."

"Ethyn."

"Yeah, I know. Get in."

---

The launch site is three hours outside Port Jiji. I sleep for most of the drive. I wake when the vehicle slows.

I look out the window and stop thinking about postponing anything at all.

The site is so big my brain takes several seconds just to catch up, and even then falls short. The facility sprawls for kilometers in every direction, lit up against the dark like a small city that decided halfway through being a city to become a spaceport instead. Warehouses the size of city blocks. Four VAB towers visible against the sky. Roads wide enough for the kind of vehicles that carry rocket stages, and those vehicles on them, moving slow and deliberate at this hour.

Above it, a cluster of lights drifts with the unhurried patience of low orbit. Rising toward it from a distant pad is a supply rocket and its payload, climbing in a long bright arc into the black.

I watch it until it's just an orange point.

"The Vanguard," Wyaine says. First real thing he's said since get in. "They've been assembling it for eight months. Couple more weeks to go, give or take."

A forklift the size of a building hauls what looks like a thruster housing out of one of the warehouses as we pass, slow and careful, headed somewhere further into the site.

"This is where the Day-Star rockets were built," he says. "For Faraday. You've probably seen it on broadcasts." He glances at me in the mirror. "First time a civilian's been on site past the visitor area, if you're wondering why I keep narrating. Company likes us to talk. Makes the new ones feel less like they're being kidnapped."

"Are you succeeding."

"Little bit. Give it time."

I look at the shape of the Vanguard against the stars. Too far to resolve into anything but light. I know from the briefing packet what it is and roughly how large it is, and none of that information makes it feel real from down here.

"Come on," he says. "They're expecting you in the morning. I'll swing by the dorms around seven, get you fed before they throw you at the lab."

## Day Two

The research lab is a dedicated building on the north end of the site, about the size of one of the smaller warehouses. Argon Industries and LP Defense Solutions logos on the side, plus a third logo I don't immediately recognize. It doesn't look like a place that contains live Phengoclept. It looks like a place that makes components for things that will eventually be in space.

Which is, I suppose, accurate enough.

Wyaine's there at seven exactly, same vehicle, same tight collar, holding two cups of something that's technically coffee.

"Big day," he says, passing one over. "First one's always the worst. After this you'll know where the bathrooms are and it stops being terrifying and starts being just a job."

"Comforting."

"I do my best."

He drops me at the checkpoint to the lab wing. Bejors is waiting there when I arrive. She's been at the site for three weeks already, which I learn from her immediately, unprompted.

"You're late," she says, which means I'm on time and she's been here since before breakfast.

"I got the morning briefing on the way in."

"The raccoon?"

"Wyaine. He's got a name now."

"The raccoon," she says again, unbothered, already walking. I follow.

---

The containment unit is in the center of the main floor, sealed behind four layers of shielding I'm not cleared to touch. Small. About the size of a carry-on bag. Inside, according to readouts confirmed independently by three separate systems every thirty seconds, is a quantity of live Phengoclept culture so small you'd never guess it was dangerous.

Y'shari is at the far end of the room, finishing a call, and turns like she's already three items into a list she means to finish today.

"Ethyn," she says. "Come look at this."

I come look at this.

Up close, the containment unit is less impressive than it looked from across the room, which somehow makes it worse. A thing this small, behind this much shielding, has been eating stars. The readout shows culture count, temperature, containment pressure.

All nominal. All perfectly, infuriatingly nominal.

"It doesn't do much at this scale," Y'shari says. "The culture's too small for meaningful output. You won't watch it eat anything in there. But it's alive. Reproducing. Doing exactly what it does at full scale, just smaller."

"And what it does," I say, "is eat light."

"It metabolizes electromagnetic radiation, mostly infrared and the high-energy output tied to stellar thermal activity. That's why stars dim. They're not losing mass. They're losing output, converted and consumed before it can radiate outward."

"And then it migrates to breed, comes back, and there's twice as much of it."

"Everywhere we've looked." She looks at the unit. "Almost everywhere."

I look at her.

"ASI," I say.

"ASI," she agrees.

We work through the day. Real work, the sort that swallows hours without announcing it, and by the time Wyaine sticks his head into the lab wing to ask if anyone wants a ride back to the dorms, it's well past dark and neither Bejors nor I have noticed.

"You two live in here now?" he asks.

"Apparently," I say.

"I'll come back in an hour. Try to remember you have legs."

## Day Three

The work is good, when it's just the work.

That matters more than it sounds like it should. Research settings don't guarantee the work stays clean, and when it does, you notice it by the absence of the thing you braced for.

Bejors is the best scientist I've ever worked beside. I've told her this exactly zero times, because she'd weaponize it in every methodology argument we have, and we have a lot of them. Good arguments. Half testing each other, half enjoying it.

We're in the middle of one, technically two at once, the afternoon of the incident. I'm arguing with Y'shari about the data. Bejors is three feet away having the same argument from a different angle.

"The growth rate in the third sample batch is higher than the model predicted," I say, scrolling the output. "Not by much. But consistent. Every batch, slightly over."

"Within acceptable variance," Y'shari says.

"I know it's within variance. That's not the point. Consistent deviation isn't random variance, it's a signal the model's slightly wrong about something." I push my glasses up. "I want to know what."

"We don't have time to rebuild the model right now."

"Not asking to rebuild it. Asking to look at where it's off and figure out if it matters."

Bejors swivels her chair around.

"The lab protocol," she says. "The quantity we're feeding the tech team for culture propagation. It's one digit higher than the breeding model specifies. I flagged it in the last review. It's going to cause issues."

"It's within range," Y'shari says.

"It's at the top of the range. One digit more and it's over." Bejors looks at me directly. "Ethyn. The quantity they're working with downstairs. Someone should double-check the transfer amount before the next batch."

I check my screen. She's right, it's at the top of the window, but it's still inside the window, and I've got three other threads open and the model deviation is more interesting to me right now, and the next batch isn't for another six hours.

"I'll look at it after," I say.

Bejors holds my eyes a second longer than she needs to. Then she turns back to her screen.

"Alright," she says.

We keep working.

---

Fifteen hours later, Y'shari puts a paw on the door.

"We're done for the night," she says.

Neither Bejors nor I look up.

"I mean it," she says. "Both of you. Screens off. We can pick this up in the morning."

"We're close to something," I say.

"You've been close to something for four hours. You said that. You were right. It'll still be close in the morning." She crosses the room and physically closes my laptop. Not hard. Like a parent closing a book when the reading light's been on past midnight. "I will have security walk you out if you don't move in the next five minutes."

Bejors saves her work without being asked. I look at the half-finished model on my screen for another few seconds before closing it.

"The predator hypothesis," I say. "If we're right about the population cap at ASI, something is eating them before the return trip. Something in the atmosphere of Asimov-09D."

"I know," Y'shari says. "Write it down when you get to the dorms. We'll look at it in the morning."

"If we're right, that means the predator, whatever it is, can be replicated. Brought back. It's not just interesting, it's."

"The whole ballgame," Y'shari says. "Yes. I know. Morning."

We go.

## Day Four

Wyaine's waiting outside the dorms in the morning with two cups of the technically-coffee and an expression I've started to recognize as him having an idea he hasn't fully talked himself out of yet.

"So," he says, passing one over. "The breeding thing."

"What breeding thing."

"The Phengoclept. You guys keep sayin' you need reliable propagation numbers before the mission timeline actually works, right? Consistent doubling time, no more one batch runs high and the next one runs low. I was thinkin' about it last night. Damn thing wouldn't leave me alone."

Wyaine swears like other people clear their throats. I don't. Swearing in my house wasn't banned, exactly. It was more like wearing a hat to dinner: nobody stopped you, you just got looks.

"You were thinking about xenobiology last night."

"I think about a lot of things last night. Mostly I don't sleep great." He shrugs, unbothered by his own admission. "Anyway. What if the problem's not the culture. What if it's the container."

I stop walking.

"Say that again."

"You've got these standard breeding trays, right, same ones for every batch, same material, same everything. What if the tray itself's inconsistent. Not by much. Enough to matter over enough batches."

It's not a scientist's idea. It's not phrased like one, doesn't come with a hypothesis attached or a model behind it, just a guy who spent all night turning a problem over because he happened to be awake and it happened to be interesting. And it's also, I realize about four seconds later, standing in the cold with a coffee going lukewarm in my paw, probably right.

"Nobody's tested tray-to-tray variance," I say, mostly to myself. "We've been, uh, assuming the trays are identical because they're manufactured to spec. Nobody's actually checked if spec means what we think it means."

"That a good idea or a bad idea."

"That's a very good idea, Wyaine."

He looks genuinely pleased about this in a way that has nothing to do with credit and everything to do with having been useful, which I like about him.

---

Getting Wyaine actual lab access takes the better part of the morning, several phone calls, and one very long conversation between Y'shari and someone in facility security that I'm not invited to but can hear the tone of through a door.

"He's not cleared for containment work," Y'shari says, when she finally comes to find me.

"He doesn't need to touch the containment unit. He needs to help me measure trays."

"That's a strange hill to defend a driver on."

"He had the idea. I'd like him in the room when we test it." I don't fully know why this matters to me as much as it apparently does. "It's a small ask."

She looks at me for a long moment, the specific look she gets when she's deciding whether something's worth the political cost of getting it.

"Two hours," she says. "Supervised. And if he touches anything he's not supposed to, that's on you."

"Understood."

"I don't think you do," she says, "but we'll find out."

---

Wyaine turns out to be, against every expectation I walked in with, genuinely good at this.

Not at the science, exactly. He doesn't know the vocabulary, keeps calling the calipers "the pinchy thing" no matter how many times I correct him, and once refers to the entire containment wing as "the scary room," which Bejors overhears and adopts immediately and permanently. But he's got a mechanic's patience for tolerances, a sense for where a manufactured object might quietly disagree with its own spec sheet, that neither Bejors nor I have, because neither of us has spent years underneath vehicles finding the half-millimeter gaps that turn into real problems at highway speed.

"There," he says, on the fourth tray, pointing at a reading that's a hair off from the other three. "That one's got a lip. Real small. You'd never see it lookin', you'd only feel it if you ran a claw round the edge."

I run a claw round the edge. He's right. There's a lip.

"How did you," I start, and don't finish the sentence, because I don't actually know how to finish it in a way that isn't just repeating the question.

"Dunno. Just a feel thing." He shrugs again. It's becoming his signature move. "Cars are the same. Two parts made off the same mold, they ain't ever really identical. Close enough for government work, not close enough for some jobs."

"This is one of the jobs where it matters."

"Guess so."

## Day Five

We map every tray in inventory. All of them. It takes the whole day and most of the next morning, Wyaine running measurements while I log them and Bejors cross-references the batch history against tray serial numbers, and by the early afternoon of Day Five we have something that looks, for the first time since I got here, like an actual answer instead of a growing pile of unexplained variance.

"Six trays out of forty," Bejors says, reading off the final tally. "All from the same manufacturing run. All with the same lip, give or take a hair."

"And every batch that ran high," I say, pulling up the cross-reference, "used one of those six."

Nobody says anything for a second. Weeks of shapeless problem, and now it has an actual edge to it, something you could point at and name.

"So swap the trays," Wyaine says, like it's obvious, because to him it apparently is.

"Swap the trays," I agree.

Bejors looks between the two of us with an expression I can't fully read, something between amused and genuinely surprised.

"You found this," she says to Wyaine, "by feel."

"Yeah."

"You should probably get a bonus or something."

"I'll take a sandwich," Wyaine says. "I'm starvin'."

## Day Six

The bad trays get pulled from rotation and the propagation numbers stabilize almost immediately, which should feel like more of a victory than it does, mostly because there's still seven days until the launch window and a hundred smaller problems behind this one, each waiting patiently for their turn.

Wyaine keeps showing up anyway. Officially his shift ended when the tray problem got solved. Unofficially he's found some paperwork reason to keep clearing him for the lab wing, and I've stopped asking how, because I don't actually want to know how many rules are being quietly bent to keep a driver in a containment facility.

"You don't have to keep coming in," I tell him, on Day Six, watching him recalibrate a sensor array with the same unhurried mechanic's patience he brought to the trays.

"I know."

"I mean it. This isn't your job."

"Wasn't my job to find the lip on tray fourteen either." He doesn't look up from the sensor. "Turns out I'm good at noticin' when somethin's a little bit wrong. World's endin', might as well be useful with it while I still can."

I don't have a response to that, so I pass him the next component instead, and we keep working.

---

At four o'clock Y'shari collects me from the lab bench without asking whether I'm busy.

"Demo bay," she says. "Bejors says you should see this, and for once I agree with her."

The demo bay at Port Jiji is the one room in the complex that is clean in a way that feels like a threat. Concrete floor, steel rafters, a smell of hot metal and floor cleaner, and a lot of empty floor where something is clearly supposed to go.

Six people in matching flight team jackets are standing in the middle of it, and Y'shari steps slightly in front of them.

I take a spot at the back with a clipboard I'm not using, because Bejors has already told me to stand where I can see the plate.

"Flight team," Y'shari says, and they turn. "This is Dr. Ethyn Vasek, from the Phengoclept side. He's the reason the cultures in Lab A haven't died this week."

"Wyaine's the reason," I say.

"Dr. Vasek," says Y'shari, "is being modest on purpose."

The Pilot is a lean, tired-eyed man who looks at everything like it might need landing. The Software Engineer is a woman with a laptop she is not going to put down. The Medic gives me a business card without ceremony. The Mechanical Engineer nods once and gives me nothing. The Lead Scientist shakes my paw briskly and is already looking past me at the far end of the bay. The last one is a man with a badge that says SECONDARY SCIENTIST, which is, I realize with a tiny wrong flutter, a job title I could do.

Everyone says their name. I hear the sound of it and lose it, as I always do in a crowd, six names stacked on top of each other and none of them landing.

"Study guide guy," says the Pilot, grinning. "I read it on the long flight over."

"Which edition?"

"The one with the coffee stain on the cover."

"That's all of them."

"Then all of them."

Bejors has found me anyway. Marker on her paws, a whiteboard that wasn't there ten seconds ago, and the look she gets when an argument is about to go well.

"You filed my paper under MAYBE SOMEDAY," she says, quietly, so only I hear.

"I filed it where I keep the good ones."

"Someday is soon," she says, and goes to find a marker.

---

Kalen is the last one in. A thylacine in a work jacket, slim black square glasses pushed up, safety glasses pushed up over those, a name patch on his chest that says KALEN. He carries a heavy gray box about the size of a loaf of bread, in both paws, like it might wake up.

He walks right past me.

I turn to follow him, and that's when I see it, against the back wall behind all of us, where I would have sworn there was nothing but a stack of crates a minute ago. A steel tube. A test chamber about the size of a phone booth laid on its side, with a thick round door standing open and a small round window set into it. I don't know how I missed it. It's the size of a small shed.

Inside, on the floor of the chamber, sits a solid steel block, a dense, ugly cube about the size of a cooler.

Kalen steps into the tube, sets the gray box down beside the cube, and bolts it to the floor. Then he runs two hoses from the box to ports on the wall of the chamber, one on each side, and a cable as thick as my wrist from the box to a socket by the door. He checks each connection twice, with the patience of someone who has once not checked.

He steps out. He swings the round door shut and spins the wheel. It seals with a heavy, final sound, like a refrigerator the size of a bus.

The rest of us drift toward the little round window.

"This is a Phengomotor," Kalen says, "a small one. On the plate in that box is about as much Phengoclept as would fit on the tip of a claw. Phase one, we attract the cells. Phase two, we expose the plate to space and pulse it. Phase three, we scrape off the dead ones. Today we're skipping phase two."

"Why?" asks the Pilot.

"Because I want you to see what phase two is for."

He flips a switch on the wall. Through the window, a small lamp comes on inside the tube. The front of the box slides open like a shutter, and the cells crowd toward the plate like they've been waiting for it, which, I suppose, they have.

"What happens, uh, if one lands on something solid?" I ask. I'm a Phengoclept guy, not a drive guy. I've never seen this part. "Not the plate. Something else."

"It doesn't land," Kalen says. "It drops. And it dumps everything it's holding. Heat and blast, all at once. Not a laser. People always say laser. It's a bang."

"How big a bang?"

"Depends how many." He pushes his glasses up, a tiny, practical movement, like he's about to correct me before I've said anything. "That's the whole problem. It scales badly."

He looks at the clock on the wall.

"Alarm in four seconds. You'll want to turn your ears down. Three."

I don't know what turning my ears down means. I do it anyway, and so does everyone else.

A thin white line crosses the room, and then the floor jumps. The sound arrives a half-second behind, a dull, flat thud with all the air squeezed out of it, and the little window flares white at the edges. Through it, the steel block is a slumped, glowing shape, sagging at the corners, running down into a puddle of slag that pools where the cube used to be.

Nobody says anything. Somewhere in the group, the Mechanical Engineer says, quietly, "Efficient." I think he's being sarcastic. I'm almost sure.

"Oh, hell," I whisper. Nobody gives me a look. Under the circumstances, it's a rounding error.

"That's the cells dumping everything," Kalen says, almost cheerful. "That's what you don't want. A Phengomotor is that same energy, but asked politely. You attract the cells, pulse them in order, and let them leave the plate one at a time, pushing, instead of all at once, pushing the wrong way."

"So the safe version," I say, and I can hear my own voice doing that annoying thing it does when it stops being scared and starts being curious, "is making them want to leave."

"Yes," Kalen says, and smiles for the first time, a small, grateful smile, as if no one's ever said it that cleanly. "That's exactly it."

---

Afterward the others drift off in pairs. I stay at the little window a little longer than I mean to, watching the slag cool from white to orange to something almost red, and I find the Pilot beside me, paws in his pockets.

"You looked like someone doing arithmetic with a bad ending," he says.

"I do the math on everything. It's a condition."

"Does it come out okay?"

"It comes out," I say, "that I'm very glad I don't fly."

He laughs, short and real, and turns his face to the window so I can't see it for a second.

"I'd like to say I was brave," he says. "I'm mostly just not good at standing around. You get a seat on the thing, you take it."

"That's not bravery."

"No," he says. "It's a pretty good imitation, though."

He claps me once on the shoulder, like he expects to see me again, and goes to catch up with the rest.

I stand there a while longer. The slag is the color of an ember now. Bejors is already back at her whiteboard, arguing with it.

I think about asking the Pilot his name again. I'd lost it the first time, in the noise of the introductions, and by now it's late enough that asking would be embarrassing.

I'll ask him tomorrow.

*END OF CHAPTER SIX*
