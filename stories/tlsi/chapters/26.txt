Seven years and nine months is a long time to be most of the way somewhere.

Then, all at once, it isn't. ARACCSIM wakes all five of us on the same morning, for the first time since the flush, and the medbay fills up with the sound of people being polite to each other at half speed. Petryn stretches like a man who's going to need a minute. Marsh is already dressed. (How? When? I was awake first. I'm almost sure I was awake first.) Maslin sits up, looks at the four of us, counts us under his breath, and lies back down, satisfied.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Braking burn in thirty-one hours."

"Thank you. I formally protest the timing."

"Noted. The burn does not move."

"It never does. That's what makes it a burn and not a suggestion."

A very short pause, with the flourish in it. "That is not a distinction I would have drawn."

Before the burn, Petryn insists on one more dinner with a floor.

He sets up the folding table in the middle of the medbay. Same one. A little more scratched than it was eight years ago. He puts five tubes on it in a row. Nobody says it's tubes. We've all learned.

Marsh sits down last. She's carrying something. She sets it on the table very carefully, steps back, and doesn't say anything.

It's one of the pots.

The plants came over from MK2 a long time ago. They've lived in the hallway outside the medbay ever since, under the brightest lamp we have. I've walked past them every night of every stretch, and at some point I stopped noticing them, the way you stop noticing anything you've decided is safe.

This one has grown. It's about twice the size it was. And at the top, small and pale and folded open like it isn't sure it's allowed yet, there's a flower.

Wait. A flower? Out here? On a wick and a water bag and a lamp?

"It flowered!" I say. It comes out way louder than I mean it to.

"Last month," Marsh says. "I did not want to make a speech about it."

"You can make a speech about it."

"I would prefer not to." She sits, straightens her napkin, and looks at the flower. Then, very quietly: "It is very hard to give up on a day that has a shape."

Nobody says anything. Maslin reaches over, slowly, like the flower might startle, and moves it an inch toward the middle of the table, where everybody can see it.

I do some math I can't help doing. Plants flower when they decide conditions are good enough to bet on the future. That's the whole biology of it. Flowers are expensive. You don't make one unless you think there'll be a next generation to make it for.

So this plant looked around at a lamp in a hallway, seven years from anywhere, and decided: yes. Okay. Worth it.

I like this plant a lot.

We eat. It's tubes. It's the best meal I've had in eight years.

The spin comes down with two keys. The cables reel in. The crew section climbs back down to the tanks and engines, and the latches close with the thunk I feel in my teeth every time. The ship goes back to being tall. For the last time, I hope.

---

There's one thing I do before the braking burn, and I do it alone.

I go to the medbay. It's empty now, all six berths pushed back into their cubbies. I take out my tablet and open the letter.

It's shorter than it was. Line four is gone. The second time I said I understood her is gone, and so is the first. What's left is mostly true. That's more than I can say for most of what I've written in my life, including a study guide that sold rather well.

"ARACCSIM. Queue a message for Port Jiji. Attention Dr. Priya Emili Y'shari. Hold it until we're in range."

"Queued. Would you like to attach a subject line?"

Do I? What would it even say? *Re: you sent me to die*? *Update*? *Hi*?

"No," I say. "She'll know what it is."

I read it one more time before I let it go. Not all of it. Just line eleven, the one where I tell her I'm angry with her and I don't want to be. It's still true. I've had years to make it untrue, and it's refused.

Then I scroll to the bottom and add one line under the end.

*Jinzhou says line eleven is good. He's never met you. He says he doesn't have to.*

"Queued," ARACCSIM says again, when I tell it I'm done. Then, after a pause that doesn't have the flourish in it, just a pause: "Dr. Vasek. If the braking burn does not proceed as planned, this message will still be transmitted."

"I know," I say. "That's why I'm sending it now."

I put the tablet away and float there for a minute, in a room full of empty berths, with the ship humming around me. Then I go to the cockpit, where four people are waiting to be told it's time.

---

The braking burn is twenty-five hours long, and nothing goes wrong in it.

I'd like to say more than that. I'd like to tell you about the moment it nearly went sideways. The alarm. The clock. There isn't one. The fuel is clean. The motors are warm. The stack runs smooth from the first pulse. The weight comes in from the engine end, the gimbals turn everything to meet it, and then it just keeps coming. Steady. Hour after hour. The most boring twenty-five hours of my life.

I've never been so happy to be bored.

Maslin watches the fuel readout the whole time anyway. Every hour he reads the reserve out loud.

"two point five percent over," he says at hour one.

"two percent over," at hour twelve.

Huh. Wait. Shouldn't that hold still? If the burn's on profile, the margin over the profile shouldn't shrink. Why is it shrinking?

Oh. Cells age. That fuel has been sitting in the tanks for almost eight years. Old Phengoclept cells pulse a little weaker than new ones, so the stack burns a little more of them to make the same push.

I pull up Marsh's tablet on the side screen. There it is, in her typeset handwriting, from four years ago. A column labeled *cell age loss*. The numbers go two point five, two point zero, one point two, one point one. Hour by hour.

She knew. Of course she knew. She did the arithmetic on getting old before any of us got there.

"one point two," Maslin says at hour twenty-four. Then he stops reading it out. He just watches. His ears go very still.

"Phengomotor braking complete," ARACCSIM says at hour twenty-five. "Handoff velocity achieved. Reserve remaining, one point one percent."

One point one. Exactly her column. To the decimal.

The weight goes out of the room like somebody unplugged it. Maslin lets go of the readout. He looks at his own paws for a second, then at me.

"i didnt explode," he says.

"You didn't."

"i had a whole tab for it"

"I know. You can delete the tab."

"im gonna keep the tab," he says. "as a souvenir"

Petryn laughs, low and easy, from the medbay. Marsh writes something on her tablet and underlines it. Jinzhou says, "One point one," very quietly, like a man putting a number somewhere he'll be able to find it.

---

That night, Jinzhou and I end up in the cockpit, the way we always do, without either of us deciding to.

"What are you going to do," I ask him, "when we land?"

He thinks about it. Jinzhou always thinks about it. He's the only person I've ever met who treats a casual question like it's going to be graded.

"Count," he says.

"Count what?"

"The people who meet us." He looks at the screen. At a star that's going to be a sun in a few days. "I want to know how many there are."

"And then?"

"Then sleep. In a bed that does not slide out of a wall."

"That's a good plan."

"It is a short plan."

"Short plans are good," I say. "I've had a lot of long ones."

He's quiet for a while. Then: "And you?"

Huh. What am I going to do?

I think about a desk with a coffee cup on it and a stack of papers I'd been meaning to sort. A phone on speaker. A woman's voice telling me what was and wasn't for a study guide. A letter in a queue, waiting for range.

"I'm going to go find out if anybody read chapter nine," I say.

Jinzhou makes a sound that on anyone else would be a laugh.

---

A day later, Marsh lights the chemical thrusters.

It's nothing like the burn. No shaking, no clocks. A soft push from somewhere behind us, then another, then the long, patient quiet of a ship coasting the rest of the way on manners. The Phengomotors brought us down from most of the speed of light. The rest is a much smaller job, done by much simpler engines. Marsh does it with the face of a woman parallel parking.

"Correction burn complete," she says. "We are inbound."

I go to the cockpit to look.

The forward camera's up on the main screen. And the first thing I notice is that I don't notice anything.

That's the weird part. Four years ago, I looked out the medbay window and the stars were wrong. Thinned out and red behind us, crowded together somewhere up ahead. Now they're just stars. White and ordinary, spread across the screen, every one of them where a sky's supposed to put it.

Why? Because we're slow again. Slow enough that the light stops getting stretched and squished on its way in. Slow enough that the universe has stopped bending around us.

"The stars are right," I say.

"They were always right," Jinzhou says, from the right seat. "We were fast."

"Same thing, from in here."

And in the middle of the screen, a little brighter than everything around it, there's a point of light that isn't white. It's orange. Dim. Not as warm as I remember it, and a little smaller than the picture I carry around in my head.

Nakuet.

I don't say anything. Nobody does. Jinzhou doesn't look at me, which is how I know he's looking at the same thing.

Somewhere near that light, too small to see yet, is a planet that's been getting colder the whole time I've been gone. And somewhere on it, if it made it, is a little courier ship that should have landed about a year ago. A box of Asicell in its glass. A file called READ_THIS_FIRST_IT_DIGS.

I'm about to find out if anybody read it.

I look at the light for a long time. I don't do any math on it. I let it just be a light.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX*
