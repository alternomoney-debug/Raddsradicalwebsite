## Orbit

Wask-ki starts as a dot and takes nine days to become a place.

I watch most of it from the left seat. The dot gets a color first, a thin hard blue, and then a shape that isn't quite round, and then, around day seven, a thin white scar of cloud across the top that I look at for a very long time before I admit what I'm doing. I'm checking the weather. Out of habit. Nine days from the nearest forecast and I'm checking the weather on a planet that can't hear me yet.

Nakuet sits behind it the whole way. Dim and orange and a little smaller than the picture of it I carry around in my head. I don't do the math on that. I did the math on that for longer than I'd like to say, and I'm retired.

Nobody talks much on the approach. That isn't new. It's a different quiet from the one on the way out. That one had a floor missing. This one has a floor. The five of us drift in and out of the cockpit as if we have somewhere better to be, and we don't, and we keep coming back anyway.

Maslin has taken to standing behind my seat with his arms folded and saying nothing, which is a first. Marsh checks the fuel numbers every four hours and then, when the numbers are the same as they were four hours ago, checks them again. Petryn sits in the corner with a mug he's been holding for ten minutes and hasn't drunk from. I've decided not to mention it.

Jinzhou sits in the right seat with his paws flat on his knees. He's been like that since the planet got a color.

"You can say it," I tell him, around day eight.

"Say what."

"Whatever you're doing the math on."

"I'm not doing math."

"You're looking at the fuel curve."

"It's a good curve," Jinzhou says. After a moment: "I'm allowed to look at a good curve."

"You are."

"It has been a long time since I looked at one."

I leave it there. There are times to keep going and times you let a man have his curve.

---

On day nine, ARACCSIM says, "Orbital insertion in six hours. Transmission range to Port Jiji is within acceptable limits."

I've been waiting for that sentence for fifteen years, most of them asleep, and I'm surprised how little it sounds like anything. It sounds like an announcement about a train.

"Dr. Vasek," it says. "Would you like to compose the message, or shall I read the standard acknowledgment."

I think about the standard acknowledgment. I wonder who wrote it. I imagine a committee in a building at Port Jiji sitting around a table, years ago, deciding on the right way to say we survived, and failing to get anyone to agree on whether survived was a good word.

"I'll do it," I say.

"Understood."

"What's the standard one say?"

"Vanguard MK1 acknowledges. All systems nominal. Crew status to follow."

"That's terrible."

"It was approved by eleven people," ARACCSIM says.

"Which of them wrote it?"

"I am not able to determine that." A pause, the pause that has a flourish in it that nobody's ever explained. "I would not have chosen it."

Jinzhou makes a sound that on anyone else would be a laugh.

I practice. Out loud, at first, in the cockpit, which is a mistake, because the room is five people and they all listen. "Vanguard MK1 to Port Jiji. We have completed the mission." Too formal. "Vanguard MK1 to Port Jiji. We made it." Nobody made it. Four people didn't. "Vanguard MK1 to Port Jiji. Please advise." That one sounds like a man calling in a flat tire.

"You can say it badly," Jinzhou says, not looking up. "No one is grading."

"Someone is always grading."

"Then they are grading you on being here."

That, I think, is the kindest thing he has ever said to me, and he says it to a fuel readout.

I don't write anything down. I decide to say whatever comes out when I press the button, and I've decided that on purpose, because anything I write I'll edit, and anything I edit I'll sand down into the committee's sentence.

---

It's four hours later. The planet fills the whole window. There's a coastline now, and a long dark smudge of weather over it, and one thin line of light on the night side that's almost certainly a city.

Marsh tells me when we're in range. She says it like she's reading a gauge, which she is, and her voice breaks on the last word, and she coughs and says it again.

I press the button.

For a moment nothing comes out of me at all. I can feel all of them behind me. I can feel the dead behind that, the four empty beds, and the one photograph in my breast pocket that's gone soft at the corners from being touched. I keep my paw on the key and look at the line of light on the night side, which might be a city, which might be Port Jiji, which might be a lamp somebody left on.

"Vanguard MK1 to Jiji," I say. "We are home."

Nobody answers.

I count. I count because counting is what I do when I'm trying not to feel something, and the something is very large and very close to the microphone. One, two, three, four.

There's a delay on the line. There always is, even this close. I know there is. Knowing doesn't help.

At nine, the static breaks.

"Say again, Vanguard." A young voice, cracking in the middle of a sentence it didn't plan to finish. It's the voice of someone who got handed a headset an hour ago and a script a minute ago and hasn't been told what to do when the script runs out. "Please. Say again."

"We are home," I say, slower. "Five aboard. Ethyn Vasek. Jinzhou Kenzo. Maslin Corwin. Marsh Okonjo. Petryn Draven."

On the other side of the cockpit, Jinzhou doesn't look up. His ears go flat against his head, and then, very slowly, back up, which on him is a speech.

"Five," the voice says. "Five aboard. I'm, hang on. Hang on, please. Don't, um. Don't go anywhere."

"We're in orbit," I say. "We're not going anywhere."

Behind me, Maslin makes a noise like a kettle.

There's a scuffle on the line. The sound of a chair going over. A door. Footsteps, running, somewhere very far from me and very close at the same time. Someone shouting a name down a corridor that I'll never hear the end of.

Then a longer silence. Then a different voice.

"Vanguard, this is Jiji."

I know it before it finishes the first word.

Older. Lower. Steady on purpose, like a floor laid across a drop. The words land one at a time, flat and unhurried.

My paw tightens on the console. I don't decide to do that.

It's Y'shari.

She says, "Welcome back."

And my whole body does something I haven't given it permission for. It goes still. Not afraid exactly. Just very aware of where the edge is. Fifteen years. Four graves. A berth that carried me into the wall without asking. I remember her telling me, on a stairwell, the whole shape of it at once, because she thought I deserved to hear it. I remember how true that was, and how little it helped.

She doesn't say anything else. She lets the silence sit on the line, patient as ever, and I understand that she's waiting for me.

I don't answer right away.

Jinzhou's head turns toward me. Not the whole head. An ear, and the corner of an eye. He's seen my face.

"Thank you," I say, finally. It comes out level. Even I'm impressed. "We're glad to be here."

"We are very glad you are," Y'shari says.

There's a pause. In it I can hear her deciding not to say a dozen things. Whatever she carried, she's carrying it onto the open channel, where eleven technicians and a janitor and half the continent can hear her do it, and she isn't putting it down.

"Is it working?" I ask.

I hadn't planned to say that. It comes out of me like a cough.

"Dr. Vasek," she says.

"Is it working. The Asicell. The SRV. Nakuet. Is it."

"The curve has bent," Y'shari says.

I don't breathe.

"It's slight," she says. "I won't oversell it. It's the first bend in forty years. It's small and it may not hold, and we don't yet know what it costs." A beat. "Your courier came in last year. There was a file on it called READ_THIS_FIRST_IT_DIGS." Another beat. "We read it first."

"Good," I say. My voice does something. "Good. That was the whole point of the capital letters."

"It is bending, Ethyn."

Behind me, Maslin says something quiet in the dialect he keeps for his mother, and Marsh sits down on the floor of the cockpit, just sits, with her back against the console, and puts both paws over her face. Petryn finally drinks from his mug. It is, from the look on his face, completely cold.

I look at the sun. Dim and orange. Still there.

I think about what a bend looks like on a graph. A line that was going one way, going the other, a small crooked place where somebody's whole life was bent a few degrees from the grave. Four people are in that bend. Bejors is in that bend. It is two pixels wide and nobody will ever see it from the ground.

"Understood," I say, and the word breaks halfway through, and I let it. "Thank you. Understood."

---

They don't let us down.

I should have known that. I did know that. There's a protocol, and quarantine, and the Asicell cultures aboard that need a cold room and a suspicious group of strangers in hazmat suits before anybody gets to feel the sun on their face, and a debriefing that I'm told is a formality and turns out to be eleven months.

Eleven months in orbit around our own planet. It sounds like a joke. It's mostly paperwork.

There are medical scans. There are more medical scans. There's a questionnaire that asks, in forty different ways, whether I've had thoughts of harming myself, and I answer it forty times in the same flat voice, and a polite doctor on a screen asks me, at the end, whether I'd like to talk to someone, and I say yes, and I mean it, and that's the first thing I do on Wask-ki that I'm proud of.

There's a debrief that is mostly Y'shari's people asking the same questions in nine different orders, to see if the answers move. They don't. I tell the story the same way every time, and I find that I've started to tell it like something that happened to a man I know.

The samples go down in sealed cases, received like they might wake up. We watch them go from the window. I find I have an opinion about the carriers. Jinzhou finds he has several.

The SRV's colony, they tell me, is thriving in a sealed facility on the far side of the planet, eating what it was always supposed to eat. I ask for the footage. They send it. I watch a petri dish for nine minutes. There's nothing to see. I watch it again.

Other things.

Petryn teaches Marsh a card game that I'm fairly certain he made up. Marsh beats him at it on the third day, and Petryn takes the loss with a gravity that makes Marsh feel bad and a sulkiness that makes her feel better. Maslin discovers that there's a live feed from the cafeteria at Port Jiji, and watches it for hours, narrating the lunch orders of strangers, and nobody stops him.

And there's a lot of staring out of windows.

---

Bejors is in my breast pocket. Not her. Her photograph, the one from the roof. I take it out some nights and look at it, and I'm still not sure what the right amount of looking is. There ought to be a number. I haven't found it. I've stopped expecting to.

There are two whiteboards, actually. Hers in Lab A, her handwriting, her arrows, Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP, underlined twice. And my copy in Lab B, her header in my handwriting, with a pencil tally in the corner and a *41* and a line that says *Done at 28:20. Going home.* I've been told both can be photographed and taken down. I've been told they can stay. I don't ask which one I want. I just go down there at night and sit on the floor and look at it.

On the last night, Jinzhou finds me there.

He doesn't say anything at first. He stands beside me for a while with his paws behind his back, a posture he has when he's pretending he isn't here for a reason. The whiteboard hums faintly in the dark. Somewhere behind the wall, the ship ticks over, an old building settling in the night.

"She would have argued with the landing procedure," he says.

It takes me a second.

"She'd have won," I say.

"Probably." He considers it, with the full seriousness of a man who counts everything. "Annoyingly."

I laugh.

I didn't know I still had it. It comes out of me wrong, cracked down the middle, half a laugh and half something older underneath it. It goes on a little too long. I put the heel of my paw against my eye and I keep going and he doesn't say a word. He doesn't touch me. He doesn't need to. He just stays exactly where he is, one arm's length away, as he has the whole way.

When I'm done, he hands me a fruit bar from his pocket.

I stare at it.

"Where did you even keep these," I ask.

"I told you," Jinzhou says. "I note things."

I eat it. It's stale. It's the best thing I've ever had.

---

## The Shuttle

They send a return vessel up on a clear morning, and the five of us pile into it in our suits, people who have spent years in each other's pockets, and for the first time I get all of their faces in one place without an alarm going off.

Marsh checks everyone's seals twice. Mine she checks three times, and when I give her a look she says, "You always forget the left one," and she's right. Petryn is humming something under his breath in Russian that I'm almost certain is a rude folk song. Maslin has already told the pilot that he looks tired, and the pilot has already told Maslin that that's rich, and they have become friends in under a minute, which is how Maslin makes friends: being wrong at someone until they give in.

Jinzhou sits across from me with his paws folded and his eyes closed. Buckled in before anyone asked him to. When the pilot starts the pre-entry checklist, he mouths the steps along with her, silently, and gets every one right.

"You're allowed to open your eyes," I say.

"I'm saving them."

"For what?"

"I don't know yet," he says. "I'll know when I see it."

The shuttle drops into the top of the atmosphere with a long, shaking hum. The window goes orange, then white, a roar that comes up through the seat and into my teeth. And then, all at once, it's quiet.

We're above the clouds.

I don't have a better way to say it. Below us is a floor of white that goes all the way to the edge of the world, soft and piled and endless, lit gold from the side by a sun that's dimmer than it should be and is still the best light I have ever seen. The shadows of the tall ones stretch out across the low ones for kilometers. Nothing has an edge. Nothing is counting down. I've seen a lot of impossible things, a planet the color of weak tea, a star with a scar on it, a sky full of something that eats light, and none of it was this. None of it was pretty in a way that didn't want anything from me.

I put a paw flat against the glass.

Jinzhou opens his eyes.

He doesn't say anything. He looks out the window past my shoulder, and I watch his ears go up, and up, and then forward, a thing I've never once seen them do. Whatever he was saving them for, it's this.

Then we go down into it.

The window turns a soft, ordinary grey, like the inside of a cup, and water streaks sideways across the glass. Actual clouds. Wet ones. Rushing past, bumping us around a little, completely uninterested in being important. I laugh out loud at one of them, I don't know why, and nobody asks.

We come out of the bottom of the layer with the whole sky opening up above us and the ground coming up green and brown, full of roads. A river. A set of rail lines running to nowhere in particular. And a runway, a long grey stripe, with a crowd behind a fence at the far end, too far to make out properly.

The last part is the longest.

The pilot lines us up, and the runway stops being a stripe and starts being a place, and it just keeps getting bigger, slowly, the way a thing does when you want it to hurry. The gear goes down with a thunk I feel in my teeth. Nobody talks. Maslin has both paws on his knees. Marsh is checking her own seal, for no reason. Petryn has his eyes closed and is smiling. I'm counting, because of course I am, and the numbers don't help, and I count anyway.

At eleven, the wheels touch.

Nobody speaks.

The shuttle lands, and something in my chest lets go with it.

---

## Port Jiji

The first thing I notice is the air.

It smells like wet concrete and hot metal and fuel. It smells like a place with other people in it. I stand at the bottom of the ramp for longer than makes sense, with my eyes shut and my snout lifted, and nobody tells me to move. Behind me Marsh makes a small sound and sits down on the bottom step.

The second thing is the pad.

A rescue ship stands on it, gantry down, a drive team clambering over its flank with tools hanging from their belts. One of them straightens up as we cross the tarmac, a thylacine in a work jacket, slim black square glasses pushed up over his forehead and a second pair of safety glasses shoved up over those. A name patch on his chest.

KALEN.

I stop walking.

He's wiping his paws on a rag. He sees me looking, and gives me a small, polite nod, a stranger's nod. Then he goes back to the bolt in his paw.

I know that nod. I know the jacket, and the glasses he pushes up before he corrects something. I know nothing else about him. Not what he's been doing for fifteen years. Not whether he ever thought about the man who stood at the little window of his test chamber and said, out loud, that the safe version of a bang is making them want to leave.

"Kalen," I say.

He glances up, puzzled. "Do we know each other?"

"No," I say. "I think I never got your name right."

He looks down at his own chest, at the patch, and back at me. Then he shrugs a small, bewildered smile and says, "Well. It's Kalen."

"I know," I say. "I've got it now."

I let him have the last word, and I keep walking.

---

Past the fence there's a wall.

I don't remember the wall, because it wasn't here when I left. Black stone, long and very plain, with names cut into it in rows. No decoration. No inscription across the top. Just the names, in a typeface I've seen on lockers and food tubes and every piece of paper I've ever signed, because Argon puts the same font on everything, from a meal pack to a memorial.

I stop. Jinzhou stops beside me.

There are too many to read. I don't try. But there's a section near the end, small, set a little apart from the others, and I read that one.

LEV KARPOV. PILOT.
SARI WIJAYA. SOFTWARE ENGINEER.
FREJA LUND. MEDICAL OFFICER.
DR. HAN WEI. LEAD SCIENTIST.
DR. MARCUS TEAGUE. SECONDARY SCIENTIST.

I have the roles memorized. I've recited them in my head for years, in order, like a list I was afraid I'd be tested on. I have never once had the names.

I stand there.

A man shook my paw in a demo bay and took my job, and I never learned what to call him. I can see his badge in my head. SECONDARY SCIENTIST. It's right there, on the wall, next to a name I haven't said yet.

"Marcus," I say.

It sits in the air.

"Dr. Teague. I'm sorry. I'm wearing your coat." I don't mean the jacket. I mean the whole thing. "I tried to do right by it."

Jinzhou doesn't ask what I mean.

I look for the pilot. I find him, the first line, LEV, and a small hot ache goes through me that has nothing to do with the dead and everything to do with a tired man in a bay who clapped me on the shoulder like he expected to see me again.

"Lev," I say. "He told me he was mostly just bad at standing around."

Jinzhou nods, the smallest of nods.

"It's a good reason to go," he says.

"It's a better reason to come back."

He doesn't argue. He stands there beside me a little longer, with his paws folded behind his back and the wind in his fur, doing the one thing nobody has ever had to teach him, which is to stay.

---

## Y'shari

She's waiting at the end of the line.

I see her before she sees me, or I think I do. A Ferin woman in a grey coat, standing very straight at the edge of the crowd with her paws folded in front of her, just as she stood in a parking lot fifteen years and a lifetime ago when she handed me a ticket and told me to go home and pack. Her fur has gone silver along the muzzle. There's more of it than I remember, or less. Her tail is perfectly still.

She doesn't come forward.

I stop where I am, three steps from her, and the crowd keeps moving around the two of us.

I'm aware of everything. That's the problem with being on edge, it makes you a good witness. I'm aware of where her paws are. Of the two men in dark coats standing exactly the right distance behind her. Of the door behind them, and the second door beyond that. Of the fact that the last time Y'shari was this calm, this reasonable, this absolutely certain that she was doing the correct thing, I woke up in a berth with a ceiling the color of nothing and no idea who I was.

I'm not angry, exactly. I've had fifteen years to be angry and I spent most of them asleep, and the rest being busy. This is the thing under angry. It's what a person does when they know what's standing in front of them and what it's capable of, and have to decide whether to leave or stay and be polite.

I stay. I'm polite.

"Hello, Y'shari," I say.

"Ethyn."

Her voice is quieter in person. Everything about her is quieter in person.

Neither of us moves. Then she does, three short steps, and puts her arms around me.

I don't hug her back.

I want to be honest about that. My paws stay at my sides, and my back stays straight, and I stand in her arms like a post. She's not a small woman, and she holds on hard, and she smells like paper and coffee and something under it, a cold clean smell. Her chin is on my shoulder. Her whole frame is trembling very slightly, which reminds me of a wire after a weight comes off it.

One. Two. Three.

I think about a stairwell, and this same woman telling me the whole shape of it in one sitting because she thought I deserved to hear it. I think about all of the things that were true of her at once. That she did it. That she was right to be afraid. That I don't know, even now, how much of it she'd do again.

Four. Five.

On six, I lift one paw and put it on her back. Once. Lightly. A paw on a wall in the dark, checking that it's still there.

It's enough. She breathes in sharply, as if I'd put a paw on a wound, and lets go.

She steps back. Wipes under one eye with the back of her paw like it's an inconvenience. When she looks at me again she is Y'shari, most of the way, and the part that isn't is looking at the floor.

"You came back," she says.

"You said we wouldn't."

"I said I wouldn't count on it." Her ears flick. "I have been counting on it for fifteen years. I don't think I'm very good at admitting that."

"No," I say. "You're not."

She takes that, without flinching.

"You put the names on the wall."

"Every one," she says. "I had a clerk check the spelling twice. I checked it a third time."

"I read five."

"I know which five."

I look at her for a long moment. Her eyes are very tired. There's a version of this where I say the thing that makes it all right, and I can feel the shape of that sentence in my mouth. It would be so easy. It would be kind. It would also be a lie, and I've learned what a kind lie costs by the time it comes due.

"I'm not going to tell you it's all right," I say.

"I wouldn't ask you to." She looks at me a moment longer. "I got your letter."

I'd forgotten. I hadn't forgotten. I'd put it in a drawer.

"Line eleven," she says.

"Jinzhou says it's good."

"He is right," Y'shari says. "It is the truest thing anyone has written to me in fifteen years. I have read it more times than I am going to admit in a parking lot."

"Good." I hold her eyes. "Thank you for the wall."

Something in her face goes quiet. Not forgiven. Not even close. Just seen.

"Your apartment's still yours," she says, in a steadier voice, businesslike, giving us both somewhere to put our paws. "In Arktos. They've been paying the lease the whole time, in case. Everyone's homes. There's been housekeeping every month, so it looks like nobody ever left." A pause. "I'm told the plants are dead. I'm sorry about the plants."

"I never had plants."

"Then I'm sorry about the nothing."

It almost gets a laugh out of me. It gets as far as my chest.

She turns and lifts a paw without looking behind her. "Wyaine."

---

He's leaning against an SUV at the edge of the lot, cap pulled low, arms folded across his chest. A raccoon in a work shirt with a ring of dark around each eye, the same ones he had on the first day, pulled down over a grin. He's been there the entire time. I'm almost sure of it.

He pushes himself off the hood, slow, like it's a decision.

"Took you long enough," he says.

I'm across the lot before I decide to be. He catches me with one arm and thumps my back twice, hard enough to rattle my teeth, and I laugh into his shoulder, which is a better laugh than the one I had for Y'shari. A real one. Short. Unembarrassed.

"You got old," he says.

"You got a new shirt."

"I got six of 'em. I'm a man of means now."

---

The ride is the same ride, almost. Same seat, same slightly broken air vent, same radio low enough to be furniture. He doesn't narrate this time. We just watch Port Jiji go by through the window, the dorms and the bus stop and the building on Mayson Street, and neither of us feels the need to say what any of it means.

He pulls up at the airport curb and sits for a second with both paws on the wheel.

"I kept it," he says. "The list."

"The list."

"The little stuff." He reaches over and taps the dash, where a folded square of paper is wedged behind the instrument cluster, soft and gray at the creases. "I'm on item three hundred and something. 'Ethyn, home.' Been waiting on it."

"Cross it off, then."

He does. A pen from the cup holder, slow and satisfied, the stroke going all the way across. Then we get out and stand on the curb with the engine ticking, two people who don't know what to do with their paws.

He holds out a fist. I bump it. Then he thinks better of it and holds out his paw properly, and I shake it, and neither of us says anything stupid. It's a personal best.

I reach up to my jacket, to the strip of fabric sewn onto the chest. VASEK, E. Iron-on, a little frayed at one corner, put there by a machine in an Argon warehouse years ago and worn through more times than I want to count.

I peel it off. It comes away slowly, like it's still thinking about staying.

"That's yours," Wyaine says, looking at it.

"Not really. I'd like you to have it." I put it in his paw. "You were the first person on this whole thing who just told me your name."

He turns it over. His mouth does something complicated. For a second I think he's going to say something, and I can see him not, a man with a wrench in his paw that he's decided not to use.

Then he tucks the patch carefully into his shirt pocket, behind the pen.

"Be seein' you, Doc," he says.

"Yeah," I say. "I'll be home."

I take my bag and walk into the terminal and I don't look back. If I look back I'll stay on the curb. Behind me I hear an SUV door close, and the engine not start for a long, long time.

---

## Arktos

The plane is quiet. I sleep for most of it, which is a first.

They're waiting at the gate in Arktos, two men in dark coats and dark glasses with LP Defense stitched small on the sleeve. They don't introduce themselves, and I find I don't mind. One takes my bag. The other opens a door I hadn't noticed.

"Dr. Vasek," he says. "This way, please."

"Where are we going?"

"Home, sir."

It's a sedan, not an SUV. Gray, unmarked, spotless, with a smell of new leather that makes my nose twitch. I sit in the back with my knees against the seat and watch a city go by that I last saw on a different clock, in a different decade of my life. The same bridge. The same bad billboard for the same bad soup. A bus shelter with a poster of the Vanguard on it, bright and rendered, the ship's nose lit as if by a sun, ten feet tall and no longer a secret.

I look at it for as long as I can.

A sign slides past in the dusk.

ARKTOS APARTMENTS. 2 KM.

"Almost there, Doctor," says the man in the passenger seat, without turning.

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than he can know.

---

The street outside the building is full of people.

Barricades, and a line of LP men holding them, and behind that a crowd of cameras and microphones and bright rectangles of light. Someone is shouting my name, hoarse from hours of waiting to do it. Lights go off like small explosions, one after another, white and flat, and I put a paw up before I can stop myself and the car rolls on through.

The man at the door holds it open and doesn't make a show of it.

I stand on the threshold with my paw on the frame. The hallway beyond is warm and dim, and it smells faintly of clean linen, a room kept so well it looks like nobody ever left.

Behind me, a hundred people say my name at once.

I don't turn around.

I think about Bejors, and four empty beds, and a hatch I once didn't know how to read. I think about a pilot who was mostly just bad at standing around, and a raccoon who said what he saw, and a thylacine with a bolt in his paw. I think about a man at a bench in a lab who took the longest way home from a market because it had soup.

I think about the long shot. The one the whole thing was built around. How small it looked from the ground. How big it was in the end.

I step through the door.

It closes behind me with a soft click, and the noise goes out like a light.

The jacket on my chest is blue. Where the name patch used to be there's a clean, pale square, a place where the thread sat for a long time and doesn't anymore. Above it, over the heart, sewn in white, is the round mission patch: a ship, a star, a line that doesn't quite close.

THE LONG SHOT INITIATIVE.

I leave it where it is.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX*
