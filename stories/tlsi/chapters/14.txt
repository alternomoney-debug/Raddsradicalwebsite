
At thirty-one hours, the cubby hisses.

It's the good hiss. The wake-up one. Air cycling, straps letting go. I'm on my feet before the seal is done thinking about it. I didn't sleep. I was on the floor, though, which I'm told is a different thing.

His berth slides out. The arms hover over him, decide I'm not going to be any help, and they're right.

Jinzhou opens his eyes. He looks at the ceiling for a second, like he's checking it against a drawing. Then he turns his head and finds me.

"You did not sleep," he says.

"I was on the floor."

"That is not the same thing."

"I know. You can note it."

"Noted."

He lifts his left arm off the berth. It's in a matte gray brace from knuckles to elbow. He flexes the fingers one at a time, like he's counting them. He gets to the end and seems happy with the total.

"The last thing I said was 'hold the return,'" he says.

"ARACCSIM said holding needs continuous crew authority. Then you passed out, and I was already out, so, uh. Nobody was holding."

"ARACCSIM."

"Dr. Vasek's summary is accurate," ARACCSIM says. "The protocol assumes at least one conscious crew member."

"That is a flaw."

"Acknowledged."

"It also says you dragged me up the hull," I say. "With a piece of the ship through your arm."

"Yes."

"Thank you."

"You were on the end of my line. That made you mine to bring in."

"I was unconscious!"

"You were still on the line."

I look at the brace. I can't look at him and say the next part.

"You went out on the hull. You're not trained for the hull."

"No."

"Thank you."

"You would have done the same."

"I would have, uh, panicked first."

"You would have panicked and then done the same," he says. "There is a difference."

I almost laugh. It costs him something to almost smile back. He pays it anyway.

Then he looks past me at the room. The blanket on the floor. The empty cup. A notepad face-up with my handwriting going downhill.

"What did I miss?"

"Two things," I say. "One. A locker broke the ship. I'm claiming partial credit."

He waits.

"Two. I have notes."

---

He reads the way he does everything. Completely. Left arm in the brace, right paw holding the pad, one line at a time, no skimming. I stand there watching his eyes go down the page and try really hard not to narrate his face.

When he's done, he reads it again. Slower. Then he stops on one line and stays there. I know which line. I wrote it.

"'Cable,'" he says. "'Unknown.'"

"It's accurate."

"I know it is accurate." He sets the pad on his knee. "ARACCSIM offered to cut it."

"And I said no."

"I would have said no."

"You were on the winch."

"I was on the winch," he says. "I did not see the altitude. So we each have one."

It doesn't make the number smaller. It puts it on two people. Which is how you're supposed to carry anything heavy.

"Show me the coupon," he says.

In Lab A I slide it under the scope and step back. He looks for a long time. Jinzhou doesn't gasp. What he does is go very still and very quiet, and the quiet gets colder.

"Lot series seven," he says.

"Yes."

"Every container on the ship."

"Yes."

"The SRV."

I open my mouth to say something comforting. Nothing's there.

"Its sample chambers are lined," he says. "Its fuel cell is lined. Same lot."

Oh. Oh, no.

"If we load live samples into that," he says, "we mail the problem home."

"I hadn't gotten there yet," I say. "I was working left to right, and I was still on the left."

"It is the next item."

"I know it's the next item! I was about to be upset about it in order."

He doesn't say anything. He's good at that. It helps more than it should.

Okay. Think. That's the job.

The Asicell dig through lining. Fine. That's a fact now. So flip it around. What haven't they dug through? What's been sitting right next to them for weeks without a mark on it?

I look down. At the bench. At the rack of used slides. The ones I never threw out, because I'm a person who keeps his microscope slides.

They're glass.

Wait.

Every Asicell I've looked at since the very first collector sample has been sitting on glass. For weeks! Some of those slides sat for days in the autoclave tray, with a live smear and Phengoclept on them.

I grab the oldest one and put it under the scope and look at it harder than I've ever looked at anything.

Nothing. Not one tunnel. Not even a scratch.

"They don't dig glass," I say.

"Or they have not yet."

"Or they haven't yet. But I've got weeks of slides with nothing on them, and the lining didn't last nearly that long."

Why, though? Why glass and not the lining? Think it through.

"Okay," I say out loud. "The lining's a plastic, probably. Long chains of carbon. And carbon is food. Or close enough to food that something hungry will try it. But glass is just sand that got really hot once. There's nothing in it to eat. Nothing to dig toward."

I look up.

"I've been looking at the answer all month and calling it a slide."

"Borosilicate ampoules," Jinzhou says. "Autoclave stock."

"In a glass cradle. With nothing from Argon touching them."

"I can cut the cradle."

"With that arm?"

"I am not discussing the arm."

---

That's most of a day. I'll skip the boring middle.

We eat at some point. Real food. The arms bring it with the same quiet efficiency they bring everything. Today's sauce has a taste I connect with home. You never notice it for years, and then you're trillions of kilometers away and you notice it every single bite.

I don't say anything about that. I just eat.

Jinzhou eats like eating is a task and tasks deserve to be done properly. That used to creep me out a little. Now it just means Jinzhou.

"Options," he says, without looking up.

"Do I get to pick?"

"No. I am listing them."

"Then list them."

"One. We vent the tanks. No fuel, nothing to eat." He takes a bite. "We arrive at Wask-ki with nothing to brake with."

"That's not arriving. That's a very fast hello."

"Two. We do nothing. Three hundred and ten days, then the reserve drops below what it takes to get home anyway."

"Same hello. Just later."

"Yes."

"Do you have a three?"

"No."

I push my glasses up. "I have a, uh. Small three."

He stops chewing.

"In the tank, they were slow," I say. "I put 'slow' in the log with a question mark. And 'cold' with another one. And then the loss rate dropped the minute the freeze lines came on. So the tank walls are getting colder, and the Asicell are getting slower."

"So."

"So everything slows down when it's cold. That's not even science. That's just being a cell." I look at the slide rack. "So how cold before it stops?"

"ARACCSIM," I say. "At minute twenty-six, I asked if that was the freeze lines, and you said insufficient data."

"Correct."

"Upgrade it."

"Data sufficient. Cause, thermal suppression of Asicell activity."

"Thank you!"

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek."

Lab B has a cold stage. A little block under the microscope you can chill while you watch. Built for people who want to see what a cell does on its way into a freezer. I put a fresh smear on glass, drop it ten degrees at a time, and don't look away.

Minus eighteen degrees: slow. Drifting more than swimming. That's the tank. That's my question mark, answered.

Minus thirty: commuters.

Minus forty: commuters at the end of a really long day.

Minus fifty: they stop.

They don't die. I check. Then I check again, because I'm Ethyn. They just go to sleep. Curled up and fat and green. I hear that's catching.

"Asleep," I tell Jinzhou, who has been standing behind me for some time and hasn't asked to look.

"Good."

Okay. But can they wake back up? I turn the dial back up. At minus thirty-two, one of them twitches. Eighteen minutes later the whole smear is swimming around like nothing happened.

"Eighteen minutes," Jinzhou says.

"On a slide. A tank isn't a slide."

"A tank is a great deal more metal."

"A tank is a great deal more metal," I agree. "More metal means it takes way longer to warm up. Huh. I never thought I'd be grateful for thermal mass."

---

"ARACCSIM," Jinzhou says. "Can the freeze lines hold the tank walls at minus fifty-five degrees Celsius?"

"Level two can. Level two is not recommended."

"I remember," I say. "I said I'd stay on one. I'm changing my mind. Formally."

"Noted."

"Why isn't it recommended?"

"Level two draws ninety-four percent of the bus. Centrifuge operation is not compatible. Crew gravity would be unavailable for the duration."

"The duration of what?"

"Until the tanks no longer require level two."

"And when is that?"

"Undetermined."

"So we float until further notice."

"Crew would be in rest state," ARACCSIM says. "Straps are provided."

"I'm in favor of straps," I say. "Big fan. Love a strap."

"And the engines," Jinzhou says. "The stack is cold."

"A cold stack restarts badly. The spin motors run rough. I got a warning about that in the server room, and I put it in the drawer."

"ARACCSIM. The restart. If we tried to go home."

"Cold restart of the Phengomotor stack requires approximately twenty-two hours of warming. Spin motors will run roughly during the first six. The departure profile to Wask-ki requires sustained thrust for approximately five months."

"And them?"

"Estimated revival of Asicell in the feed line and motor housings, fifty-one hours after thaw begins." That hitch on the number. "Margin of error, six hours."

Okay. Let's line those up.

Twenty-two hours to warm the engines. Then a burn that has to run for five months. And at fifty-one hours, the diners wake up. In the one place on the ship where all the food goes past.

"So they'd be awake in the line two days into a five-month burn," I say. "Again."

"Correct."

"That's not a margin. That's a rerun."

"If revival comes before completion?" Jinzhou says, even though he knows.

"The reserve is consumed before the departure profile completes. Vanguard MK1 does not reach Wask-ki."

"Amazing," I say.

Jinzhou puts his fork down. "So we cannot go home on this fuel."

"Not while they're in it."

"And we cannot get them out of it."

"Not with anything I've got." I look at the smear on the cold stage. It stopped swimming at minus fifty and it's waiting, very patiently, for somebody to mess up the thermostat. "A burn is a buffet."

"Then we do not burn."

"Then we don't burn. We freeze them, we stop the bleeding, and we think." I hear how that sounds. "Hey. 'We think' is a real plan. It's been my only plan for a while, and it's mostly worked."

"Later-Ethyn can have it," Jinzhou says.

"He's going to be so annoyed with me."

---

The payload bay is under Lab A. I go the long way, through the hatch in Lab B and along the room underneath. I haven't really looked at this room since the first sweep. Too much else to look at.

Now I look.

It's smaller than I expected for something this important. Not cramped. Just tight. Built by people who knew every cubic meter was a negotiation. The SRV sits on a rail, docked. About the size of a big four-wheeler. I stare at it for a while.

"This is it," I say.

"Yes," Jinzhou says.

"Wask-ki's only sure shot at getting this home."

"If we do not make it ourselves. Yes."

"Right." I push my glasses up. "No pressure."

"The fuel cell," Jinzhou says. "Its own. Sealed at the factory. It has never touched a line on this ship."

"I know."

"Check anyway."

So I check. I swab the cell's seam, the fill port, and the cable connection. I carry three slides back to Lab A at a walk, because running seems rude to the slides.

Clean. Clean. Clean!

I carry that word back to the bay like a cup that might spill.

Loading takes most of the afternoon. Not because it's hard. The data hookup is simple, and ARACCSIM handles the transfer log without being asked. It takes all afternoon because we do everything twice. And then a third time on the parts that matter more. Which turns out to be all of them.

The Asicell samples go in glass ampoules. The ampoules go in a glass cradle that Jinzhou cut one-handed out of the lab's glassware racks. The cradle sits in a bare steel clamp on the SRV's own cold block. Minus sixty degrees. A gap around the whole thing. Nothing from lot series seven touching any of it. They'll sleep all the way home. That seems like the only polite way to mail something that digs.

I'm sending a digging organism home in a jar. I'm aware of how that sounds.

The coupon from the 2-E valve goes in the last slot, in its own glass tube. It's the only piece of lot series seven I'm sending on purpose. I label it in capital letters: LOT SERIES SEVEN. THIS IS WHAT THEY DO.

All the mission data, transferred and checked. My notes. Every reading, every density model, every spectral run from the last three weeks. Jinzhou's logs. Every diagnostic and repair.

And a text file I name READ_THIS_FIRST_IT_DIGS.txt. Three paragraphs. The first one is in capital letters. The second one is also in capital letters, but sadder. The third is lowercase, because by then I'd calmed down enough to say please check every lot series seven you own.

Bejors' paper. The one from MAYBE SOMEDAY. I send the whole file. Then I stop, and add one more: ASICELL_DISCOVERY_LOG.doc. The one I opened in the lab that day. Her name at the top. Mine underneath. PRIMARY THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK: B. BEJORS, PHD, right in the header.

She gets the credit. Whatever happens to the ship, whatever happens to us, whoever opens this box on Wask-ki is going to know whose idea it was.

I close the transfer and step back.

"Ready," I say.

"Confirmed," ARACCSIM says. "SRV payload complete. All data verified. Sample integrity nominal."

"Don't say nominal."

"Sample integrity is acceptable."

"Thank you. That was very reassuring."

---

The welding kit is in the maintenance locker at the far end of the payload bay. Not the one that hit me. A different one. Inside. Still attached. It has a compact arc welder rated for hull metal.

I've used a welder before. Twice. Neither time on a spaceship this far from anybody qualified. But metal's metal, and my paws remember what to do even when the rest of me isn't sure this is a good idea.

"You do not have to do it by paw," Jinzhou says, behind me.

"I know."

"ARACCSIM can seal the inner door remotely."

"I know."

He doesn't say anything else. Either he gets it, or he doesn't need to. He steps back and gives me room.

Why weld it, though? Because a closed door is a promise. A closed valve was a promise too, and look how that went. A weld on hull metal, with no lining anywhere near it, is a promise I can actually see.

The welder takes a few seconds to warm up. I run the bead around the inner payload door. Slow and even. The pace you use on something you want to hold. The metal cooperates. The bead sets clean.

I step back and look at it.

The inner door is welded shut. The SRV is on the other side. Loaded, sealed, checked. Nothing between it and space but two outer doors and a rail.

"ARACCSIM. Confirm inner door seal."

"Inner door sealed. Weld integrity confirmed. SRV release on your mark."

I take a breath.

"Mark."

The bay pumps down. The outer doors open on nothing. The rail engages. It isn't dramatic. It's not the HISS THUNK of the berths or anything else this ship does when it wants attention. Just a click. Then the small, final absence of something that was there and isn't.

On the hull camera, I watch it go.

It's tiny! A four-wheeler against all of that is so small. Its little thrusters fire first, nudging it away from the hull. Then its own Phengomotor lights, running off a cell that never touched a line on this ship, and puts it on a course of its own. It catches the light from Asimov's star once. Twice. Then it holds steady on the heading ARACCSIM gave it.

I lift a paw. Wave.

Nobody on the other end sees it. The SRV doesn't have eyes. I wave anyway. Bejors' name is on that thing. The Asicell data is on that thing. Somewhere in those files is every ridiculous decimal place of the Phengoclept mass that made me laugh in a lab a long way from anyone I used to know. It all deserves a wave, even if nothing waves back.

The SRV gets smaller.

Then it's a point of light.

Then I can't tell it from the other points of light.

"There it goes," I say. "The whole mission, and from here it's a dot."

"Okay," Jinzhou says.

He can have the one-word line. It's his.

---

Last job. The floor.

The centrifuge went up with two keys. It comes down with two, turned together at the panel in the cockpit. Last time I did this alone, I sailed past slot two like a man leaving a party. Now it's two people, a key each. Which is how whoever wrote that sign meant it.

We strap in. Me in the left seat. Jinzhou in the right, with the brace across his lap like a clipboard.

"On three?" I say.

"Together is sufficient."

We turn them.

SPIN-DOWN INITIATED. WEIGHTLESS IN 5 MINUTES. SECURE ALL LOOSE ITEMS.

The weight goes the way it came. A lean. Then less of a lean. Then none. My fur lifts off my arms like it's been told it can go. The notepad on Jinzhou's knee floats up, and he catches it without looking.

Then the cables start reeling in. It takes forever. The crew section has been hanging out on the end of them for days, the two halves of the ship swinging around each other like skaters holding paws. Now we climb back down to where we started. Slow. Careful. Until the latches close with a thunk I feel in my teeth. The ship is one piece again. The grates drop back into place, and the whole thing goes back to being tall.

"ARACCSIM. Level two."

"Level two is not recommended."

"I know. We've covered it."

"Confirm."

"Confirmed," I say.

"Confirmed," says Jinzhou.

"Level two engaged. Tank wall temperature falling."

It takes five hours and forty minutes for the last wall to hit minus fifty-five. Neither of us suggests leaving. We take turns reading the numbers out loud, because they feel better said. Minus twenty-two. Minus thirty. Minus forty-one. At minus fifty I can't sit still anymore, so I hang in my harness and stare at the number like it owes me money.

"Tank 2-E, minus fifty-five degrees Celsius," ARACCSIM says. "All tanks at target."

"Reserve loss rate."

"Indistinguishable from zero."

"Say it again."

"Indistinguishable from zero."

Zero! Basically zero!

I let go of the armrest with both paws and just hang there, grinning at a gauge. Jinzhou nods once. From him, that's a parade.

Setting up the handoff takes a while. Not because it's hard. ARACCSIM to LSI-ASM&C is a written procedure. Both systems were built to do it. It takes a while because I keep double-checking things, and Jinzhou keeps letting me. And at some point he starts double-checking things too. And neither of us mentions that this is the last time we'll be awake in this cockpit for a long, long time.

"Station set," Jinzhou says. "We hold here. Crew wake is scheduled in one hundred and eighty days, unless something wakes us sooner."

"Something like what?"

"A fault. Or a reason we have not thought of."

"And the SRV?"

"Home in about eight years. Whether we are or not."

"That's the point of it," I say.

"Yes."

Six months. I do the math once and don't do it again. Six months asleep. Drifting in a system that doesn't want anything from us. The food lasting longer. The diners staying cold. Nobody, anywhere, on the way. Later-Ethyn gets the whole problem at once, well rested. I hope he appreciates the nap.

"ARACCSIM. Start the handoff to LSI-ASM&C."

"Confirmed. Handoff sequence initiating. LSI-ASM&C will maintain station, the freeze lines, and the wake schedule. It has no other functions. Estimated handoff duration, four minutes."

"It's cruise control," I say.

"Correct."

"For a freezer."

"For a freezer," ARACCSIM agrees.

Four minutes. I sit and look at the screens. The star map. The trajectory line. A little blinking marker that used to be the SRV, already too far away to see properly.

I look at Jinzhou.

He looks back. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't have to. He's Jinzhou. He notes things. This is one of them.

"LSI-ASM&C active," ARACCSIM says. "Primary systems transferred. I will maintain monitoring functions in background mode."

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Dr. Vasek."

"Thanks. For everything. Even the nominals."

A pause. Longer than usual. Maybe it's processing. Maybe it's something else. I've decided I'm allowed to believe it's something else, just this once.

"Safe travels, Dr. Vasek," it says. "Mr. Kenzo."

"We're not traveling," I say.

A pause. "Then rest well."

"You too," I say. And then I realize I just told a ship to get a good night's sleep. I decide not to take it back.

We float to the medbay.

---

The hallway is long and the hatches are blue, and I bonk into one of them. Jinzhou drifts down the same hall like he's been weightless his whole life. One paw trailing the rail, the brace held close.

The medbay is quiet. The arms are ready. Both berths are slid out of the wall and waiting, straps open.

I hold the rail. There isn't any sitting anymore.

I think about Wask-ki. Not anything specific. Not the apartment with the coffee on the desk, or the parking lot in the snow, or the hallway outside the containment lab. Just the shape of it. A whole planet that was your whole world, and then suddenly wasn't. And the slightly sick feeling of going back after so long that you don't know who you'll be when you get there.

I think about Bejors' whiteboard, still up in Lab A. Still her handwriting. I didn't take it down. It felt wrong to. It can wait with us. Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP, underlined twice, in a ship that's mostly asleep, in a lab nobody's opening until there's a floor again.

I think about the SRV. Already a dot. Going home without us, with everything that matters packed in glass.

I think about Jinzhou. Who went out on a hull he wasn't trained for, got us up with a hole through his arm, and has refused to discuss the arm twice now.

I push my glasses up one more time and look over. He's already in his berth with his straps done. He's decided doing it is easier than thinking about doing it. He's usually right.

"Hey," I say.

"Yeah," Jinzhou says.

"Good work."

He looks at me for a second. Something moves in his face, barely. Not quite a smile. Close enough.

"You too," he says.

The arms come down.

HISS.

THUNK.
