Day twenty-four starts at four in the morning, because I never actually went to sleep.

I tried! I was in the bed. That counts for something. The lights were off. That counts for something else. I had the pillow. Good-faith effort, by any standard. And at some point I think I got close. My body stopped arguing. Then my brain ran config four again, the one where the cable drag beats the thrust budget, and that was that.

So I got up. Made coffee. Sat at the Lab A bench and stared at the approach simulation until the numbers stopped looking like threats and started looking like a checklist. That's the only useful thing you can do with numbers at four in the morning.

By the time Jinzhou comes through from the dorms, I've printed the checklist. On paper. Two copies.

He looks at the copies. Then at me.

"You did not sleep," he says.

"I was in the bed."

"That is not the same thing."

"I know."

He takes his copy without another word. Either he's letting it go or he's saving it for later. With Jinzhou it's honestly hard to tell. I pour him a coffee without asking, and he takes that without a word too. I'm calling that a win.

---

The checklist takes two hours.

Not because it's complicated. Because we do everything twice. Every seal, every system, every coupling on that three-hundred-meter cable. Jinzhou checked them all by paw yesterday. We check them again today. Yesterday was yesterday. A gas giant doesn't care how careful you were twenty-four hours ago.

The probe slides out of the payload bay on its rails and we go over it together. Jinzhou calls the items, I confirm. Halfway through we switch, because two people checking in the same order miss the same things.

Sample chambers: sealed. Pressure sensors: good. Insulation: intact. Winch: works, with the note about the motor running a little hungry. We both logged that yesterday. Neither of us brings it up again. Saying it twice won't make it better.

Cable connections, main and backup. All six tether points. Telemetry transmitter, live.

"EVA tether," Jinzhou says.

"Confirmed and clipped." I tug it. It holds. "I'm not planning to go outside."

"Log it anyway."

"Already logged."

He makes a small sound that might be approval and moves on.

We finish at six forty-two. I make another coffee, because the first one has been sitting for two hours and tastes exactly like you'd expect. Six light-years from the nearest coffee bean, two hours old.

It's bad. I drink it anyway.

---

"ARACCSIM," I say, once we're both in the cockpit and the approach burn is loaded and waiting. "One thing before we start. The window. Home."

A pause.

"Orbital transfer window for return trajectory to Nakuet system opens in." A tiny hitch, a number being slotted into a sentence that was recorded to hold any number. "Two hundred and eighty-three days. Window duration is approximately fourteen days. Next available window after closure is not calculable within current fuel parameters."

I look at Jinzhou. He's already running the same math on his screen.

"Two hundred and eighty-three days," I say. "That's about nine and a half months."

"Yes."

"Wait. This was supposed to be one way. No return fuel. None." I frown. "I don't remember who told me that. I just know it."

"For six people," Jinzhou says, not looking up. "Doing a full survey of the system for however long it took." He taps his screen. "We are two. And if this goes right, we go down once."

Okay. Does that actually work? Think about it.

Six people eating, breathing, doing survey burn after survey burn for months. Versus two people and one trip down. Fuel isn't just for getting home. It's for everything you do while you're here. Do less, and there's more left over. Enough left over, and suddenly "home" is on the table.

Huh. I don't love how much sense that makes.

"And if we miss the window?"

"Current fuel reserves do not support an alternative return trajectory outside the window," ARACCSIM says. "Missing the transfer window would result in indefinite station at Asimov."

Indefinite station. That phrase is exactly as bad as it sounds.

"Okay," I say. "So we don't miss it."

"No," Jinzhou says. "We do not."

Two hundred and eighty-three days. Nine and a half months to find the Asicell, collect enough to matter, figure out how to keep it alive, and point the ship the right way. I've had worse deadlines. They were all papers.

Starting now.

The centrifuge spins down first. It always takes longer than I want. The slow unwind. Walls and floors trading back. Loose things drifting up and looking for a new place to land. When everything's still and pointed the right way, I give the order.

"ARACCSIM. Start the approach burn."

"Confirmed. Approach burn initiating in thirty seconds. Please secure."

I click my harness. Jinzhou clicks his. The cockpit hum climbs into something louder.

---

The drop below the Stephfren line takes eleven minutes.

I know that because I counted. It did not feel like eleven minutes. Around minute four, the air starts grabbing at us. A low buzz through the hull. It starts small and stays small, but it never goes away. The engines push back against it in little nudges, and the thrust readout rolls up and down like a slow wave.

"Drag compensation holding," Jinzhou says. "Config two."

Not config four. Good! Love config two. Config two is my new favorite number.

The Phengomotors are firing, but not to push. At this depth ARACCSIM runs them slow, just enough to brake. Each cell leaves the plate, flies a few meters out ahead of us, dumps everything, and burns. We're not really flying through the air. We're hiding behind a wall of little controlled bangs. Kalen said a Phengomotor is that same energy, asked politely. Down here it's asked politely and told to hurry up.

I can't see any of it from the payload bay. Just a steady flicker on the thermal readout. Like a pulse.

"Cable out to sixty meters," ARACCSIM says. "Probe telemetry nominal."

I'm at the payload bay station. A screen and a row of manual controls bolted to the wall across from the probe rails. The probe's feed is live. Numbers climbing as it drops toward the cloud tops. Pressure. Temperature. Particles.

"Eighty meters. Probe entering upper cloud layer."

The particle count jumps. Then it climbs, and climbs, and levels off right where the simulation said it would.

Exactly where! That's the first thing all day that's matched the simulation on the nose. I appreciate it way more than I should.

"Probe is in the layer," I say. "Readings good. Starting the collection timer."

"Confirmed," Jinzhou says. "Target dwell time, forty minutes minimum."

Forty minutes. That's what my density model says. Long enough for the Asicell to notice the Phengoclept smell in the probe's sample draw and show up in numbers worth bringing home. Maybe longer. Not so much longer that config four comes to visit.

I watch the collection numbers. I try not to watch the clock. I fail.

---

At twenty-two minutes, Jinzhou says "still config two," without me asking.

At thirty-one minutes, the probe's filter catches its first confirmed Asicell. I know because my screen throws a flag I set up three days ago, just for this.

My screen goes green.

Green!

I stare at it.

"Jinzhou."

"I see it."

"That's an Asicell!"

"Yes."

"In the actual air of the actual planet! A live one!"

"Yes, Ethyn."

"Bejors was right."

A pause. Shorter than I expect.

"She was," he says.

I look at the green flag. At the numbers behind it. The absorption profile. The density. Proof that the thing she described from math is real, and right here, and being pulled into a sealed chamber three hundred meters under us on a cable.

"Hey," I say. "Uh. The planet."

"What about it."

"It doesn't have a name. It's been Asimov-09D since we got here."

"Yes."

"You should name it."

A pause.

"Me," he says.

"You found the server room hatch. You checked every coupling on that cable by paw. You fixed the microscope. You've done at least as much as I have to get us here." I look at the feed. "Name the planet."

It's quiet for a moment. Not his thinking quiet. A different one.

"Bejors," he says.

I don't say anything.

"She described it before anyone knew it existed," he says. "That seems right."

I look at the green flag again. A live Asicell, in the atmosphere of a gas giant six point four light-years from home, exactly where her paper said it would be. Collected by a probe she might have helped design. For an organism she never got to see.

"Yeah," I say. "Bejors."

I open the mission log and type it in.

ASIMOV-09D: DESIGNATED BEJORS. NAMED IN RECOGNITION OF LEAD SCIENTIST B. BEJORS, PHD, PRIMARY THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK FOR ASICELL PREDATOR HYPOTHESIS. CONFIRMED CORRECT.

I sign it with both our names.

"Thirty-eight minutes," ARACCSIM says. "Approaching minimum dwell time."

"Hold position," I say. "We're going to forty-five."

The buzz in the floor has gone from a hum to something with opinions. Why? I guess the probe's weight on the cable. It's a good guess. Its best quality is that it was the first one I had.

---

At forty-three minutes, the hull makes a sound.

Not a good sound. Not the steady buzz I've been listening to for forty minutes, which at least had a rhythm. This is sharp and short, somewhere behind the payload bay. My paws are on the manual controls before my brain has a vote.

"ARACCSIM," Jinzhou says, fast. "Hull."

A pause. Too long.

"Hull integrity nominal," it says. Same flat voice it uses for a full tank or a green checklist. "Minor stress detected at aft external storage mounting, port side."

"Define minor," I say.

"Mounting fatigue within alert parameters. Monitoring."

"Motor six," Jinzhou says, eyes on his board. "The pulse was off. A cell went wide."

"Phengomotor six, irregular pulse," ARACCSIM says. "One cell dispersed outside the shield envelope. Energy release at eleven meters, aft port."

Eleven meters.

I think of Kalen's steel cube slumping into a puddle. The thin white line across the demo bay, right before the floor jumped. That was a whole plate's worth, in a sealed tube. This was one cell. Loose. Eleven meters from a locker bolted to our hull.

One cell is way less than a plate. I tell myself that twice.

"How often does that happen?" I ask.

"The simulation says once every ninety minutes," Jinzhou says. "Not zero."

"So we've used up our one."

"That is not how statistics work."

"I know. I'm trying out optimism."

I look at the cable controls. Forty-three minutes. Two more until I planned to start reeling in. The Asicell count is good. Not perfect. Good enough. So which is better: good enough now, or a little more in two minutes?

I decide before I finish asking.

"Starting retrieval. ARACCSIM, start the winch."

"Winch sequence initiated. Estimated retrieval time, four minutes twenty seconds."

I hear it from here. The motor kicking in. The cable starting to wind. It sounds fine. A little higher-pitched than I'd like, that extra draw Jinzhou flagged, but fine. Pulling the probe up through the clouds toward us.

"Two hundred and ten meters. Probe ascending nominal."

Then the cable counter stops.

The motor doesn't. I can hear it. Still pulling. Still climbing toward that hungry number. And the counter on my screen has quit moving.

Wait. Those two things don't go together. Why would a motor keep pulling and a cable not move?

There's only one way. Something's holding the other end.

"Fudge," I say.

"Cable retrieval obstructed," ARACCSIM says. "External entanglement at aft starboard secondary boom. Cannot resolve remotely."

I'm already moving.

"I'll go," I say.

"Ethyn."

"I'm faster on the hull than you. You run the winch from here."

He doesn't argue. That's not agreement. That's math.

---

The suit locker is right where I left it, laminated card on the door. I read the card anyway. All of it. The card is the only grown-up in this situation, and I'm going to respect it.

I take suit one. It passed everything on the first try, so it's my favorite. Seals. Oxygen, main and backup. Heaters. The outside temperature is a number I looked up once and decided never to think about again. I look it up again. I decide never to think about it again, again.

The airlock is the same one we used for the burials. I decide not to think about that either. I think about it for the entire cycle.

"EVA tether," Jinzhou says over the suit radio.

"Confirmed and clipped." I tug it. It holds. "I need to amend something I said this morning."

"You said you were not planning to go outside."

"The plan has changed."

"Log it."

"Already logged."

---

The outer door opens. I look up.

The sky is on fire.

Not all of it. The part in front of us. A few meters out from the hull there's a ceiling of pale orange flashes. Cell after cell leaving the plates, giving up everything, burning. One little bang after another. So regular it looks scheduled. Which it is. Past the flashes, the cloud tops are the color of an old penny, closer than they ever looked on a screen.

"Oh," I say. "Oh. The sky is on fire."

"That is the shield," Jinzhou says. "It is working."

"It's very orange for something that's working!"

"It is supposed to be orange."

"I read the simulation four times. I never once checked the color."

Every one of those flashes is the same little organism I've had under a microscope for three weeks. Dying a few meters at a time so I don't. I'd apologize to all of them, but I'd have to do it one at a time.

I go out on all fours. Standing on a hull in the dark is a decision for people with more courage and less sense. The tether plays out behind me. Every rail is exactly where the schematic says it is. That's the nicest thing anyone's done for me all day.

The junction bracket is aft starboard, where ARACCSIM said. The cable is wrapped around it twice. A cable has no business being there. How did it even get there? Doesn't matter. Hang three hundred meters of anything off a ship that's busy being on fire, and it'll find something to grab.

Both paws on the first loop. The winch strains against me. I'm taking that as encouragement.

The first loop comes off clean.

Then I see the line.

Thin. White. Cutting across the dark off to my left, from somewhere behind us, heading somewhere that is definitely not the shield. A cell that didn't get the memo. I know what it is before I know that I know. And what I hear is Kalen, in a demo bay that feels like somebody else's life: *Alarm in four seconds. You'll want to turn your ears down.*

I turn my ears down. Inside the helmet, where nobody can see.

The flash is bigger than the one in the tube. I brace for the floor to jump. For the flat, airless thud.

Nothing comes.

The loudest thing I've ever watched happens in total silence. I stay crouched there, braced, a second too long, waiting for a noise that isn't coming.

Oh. Right. Thin air. Sound needs something to travel through, and up here there's barely anything. So the flash gets to me, and the bang doesn't.

I make myself look back at the bracket. The second loop is hooked over the top. The only way to lift it clear is from above, with both paws and some leverage. Which means standing up.

I stand up. I said standing was for people with more courage and less sense. I'll let later-Ethyn work out which one I'm short on.

Standing gets me a better view. That's the problem with a better view.

The cloud tops are supposed to be below us. I watched them for forty minutes on three different screens. Copper-colored, flat, politely underneath. They are not underneath. There's a pale ceiling about a hundred meters over my head, lit from below by our own fireworks, and I'm standing under it like a guest inside somebody's lamp.

Okay. Deduction. The clouds are above us. We used to be above the clouds. So we've been sinking. Steadily, for a while, in a ship that was supposed to be holding. And nobody said anything. So nobody knew. Jinzhou would have said.

"Jinzhou. What's our altitude?"

"Holding."

"Are we? Because I'm looking at the cloud layer, and it's over my head."

A pause. Keys clicking. A longer pause.

"ARACCSIM," Jinzhou says.

"Altitude loss exceeds projected drift by three hundred and ten meters," ARACCSIM says. "Displayed on secondary panel four."

"I have been watching the winch," Jinzhou says.

"I told you to watch the winch."

"That does not make it a good plan."

I look at the orange. It used to be a few meters out. Now it's at arm's length, and it's not regular anymore. The pulses are coming ragged. One motor late, one early.

Why? Think. The timing was built for thin air. Now every cell is leaving its plate into thicker air than it was built for. Thicker air slows it down. A slow cell goes off late, and a cell that goes off late goes off in the wrong place. So the shield isn't a wall anymore. It's a gap with fire in it, and the fire stopped keeping time.

"Climb," I say. "Climb as soon as, as soon as..."

"Cable first," Jinzhou says. "If we climb with it fouled, it rips the winch off the wall."

"Cable cut is available," ARACCSIM says.

"No!" It comes out way faster than I mean it to. "No. Everything we came for is on the end of that cable. I've got it. Give me one minute."

I get both paws under the second loop and heave.

Something goes off on the rail next to my knee.

Not a flash. A crack. Small and sharp, with the whole atmosphere behind it. That silence earlier was a luxury. The air was thin. The air is not thin anymore.

That wasn't the shield. That was a cell. A motor dropped it wrong, and instead of going off out in front of us, it went off on us.

"Jinzhou," I say. "Did you feel that?"

"Yes."

"Was it a good feeling?"

"No."

"Okay. Just checking."

Another one, off my shoulder. A thump through the suit, like a door slamming on my arm from the other side. Another on the bracket. *Ping.* *Ping.* *Ping-ping.* The hull sounds like a tin roof in a hailstorm, if the hail were on fire and held a grudge.

The motors are misfiring. Not just six. All of them. Every ragged pulse throws a few cells wide, and every cell that goes wide lands on us and goes off along the rail. Little bright explosions. Very personal ones.

The ship is getting lit up. That's the only way I can say it. Bright pops all along the rail, tracing the hull, like somebody testing a string of party lights where every bulb is a mistake.

The loop is heavier than it looks. I lean into it.

"Come on," I say. "Come on, come on, come on..."

It clears the bracket! The counter on my suit display jumps from two hundred and ten to one eighty-eight in one go and keeps dropping.

"Clear!" My voice cracks in the middle. "Clear! Winch it. Winch it now!"

"Winching," Jinzhou says.

That's when the panels go.

They're thermal covers. Sheets of metal the size of doors, bolted flat over the fuel tank housings behind me, for exactly this part of the trip. I watch them lift off one after another, like a deck of cards thumbed from the edge. They flip, catch the orange, and go tumbling past. Tank 2-E's goes first. I know it's 2-E because the label is stenciled on the underside in letters big enough to read from a hull, and I read it. Of course I read it. I read everything.

"Jinz- Jinzhou. The tank covers. They're coming off. All of them. 2-E is, 2-E is bare."

"Understood." His voice has changed. Still level. But level the way a table is level when somebody's standing on it. "Ethyn. Come in. Now."

"Coming."

I mean it. I clip over to the walkway rail and start back toward the airlock, paw over paw, the way the card says.

Halfway there, the sky changes.

The misfires are lighting up the bottom of the cloud layer now. Every bad pulse, every cell that goes wide, throws a flash up into the cloud a hundred meters over my head. And the cloud holds it. The light spreads. It rolls out across the ceiling in waves, orange and white, starting right above the ship and running away toward the horizon in every direction. Fading. Then another wave, right behind it.

Like the lights over home in the winter.

Upside down.

I stop walking.

I don't decide to. My paws just stop on the rail, and the rest of me goes along.

"Ethyn," Jinzhou says.

That's the Phengoclept up there. Billions of them, in a cloud the size of a continent. I've spent my whole life writing about them in a little room in Kanada. And they're lighting up over my head like somebody's switching the planet on.

It's beautiful.

Nobody put that in the study guide.

I wrote the study guide.

"Ethyn. Airlock."

"Yeah," I say. "Yeah. Sorry. I'm. Yeah."

I turn toward the airlock. That's when I see another one.

Low. White. Coming in across the hull from the left, fast. And it's not headed for me. It's headed for the locker. The EVA tool locker on the port rail. The one motor six took a shot at earlier. The one ARACCSIM called "minor."

The cell hits the locker's last bracket. A flash, and a crack I feel in my teeth, and the whole locker leaves the rail straight up, like a lid popping off a pot. It turns end over end against the orange. Then it comes back down on a line that, I notice, includes me.

I have time for exactly one thought.

*Amazing.*

It's the last word I've got. It's still the right one.

The locker clips me on the way past.

Then I don't remember anything for a while.

*END OF CHAPTER TEN*
