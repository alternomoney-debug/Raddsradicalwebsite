
The burn starts so quietly I'm not sure it has.

I'm in Lab A with my paws flat on the table and my eyes on a screen, and nothing moves. There's no shove and no roar. The ship is weightless and stays weightless, and the only evidence that something enormous has begun is a number on ARACCSIM's readout that has stopped being zero.

"Phengomotor burn in progress," ARACCSIM says. "Deceleration, point zero zero four of a G."

"That's it?"

"That is the first minute, Dr. Vasek. It accumulates."

"Thank you. I'd have panicked if you'd said it was exciting."

"I would not say that."

"I know. That's why I like you, for about ninety seconds a day."

Somewhere in the aft end of the ship, three enormous motors are doing what Kalen drew for us a long time ago. Attract. Pulse. Scrape. Cells drawn forward, cells fired, cells thrown out the back with their work finished, dead, harmless, looking at nothing. I can't hear it. Petryn can feel it through his boots, and he says it feels like standing on the back of a very large cat who's decided to purr.

"How's the stack?" I ask him.

"Steady, man. Real steady. She's lopsided in a way I can live with."

"Can you live with it for twenty-five hours?"

"Ask me at hour twenty-four."

---

Hour thirty-one. Hour thirty-six. I stop saying the times out loud, because Maslin has asked me to, and because the sound of them is starting to do things to the room.

I eat when someone hands me a tube. Marsh reads the stack temperature every ten minutes in a voice she has clearly decided is neutral and which is not. Maslin refills the fuel readout and says, "ninety-one percent of the way to a boring day," and then, quietly, to nobody, "please be boring."

The tracking panel in front of me shows a row of dots in the aft housings. They've been green and still since the thaw started. Dormant. Waiting, in the dark, the way they've waited for years, with all the patience of something that's never had to learn what a deadline is.

At hour forty-three and ten minutes, one of the dots turns amber.

I don't say anything at first. I think there's something wrong with the display, which is how you know I've been at this too long. I look at it. The amber dot looks at me.

"ARACCSIM," I say.

"Asicell motion detected in aft housing four." The hitch in its voice is bad this time, a skip like a record someone's bumped. "Activity is above the model estimate. Revival has begun."

Everything goes very quiet.

"Hour forty-three," Jinzhou says, in my ear, flat as a table. "The estimate was fifty-six. The bad end was fifty."

"I know."

"It is seven hours early."

"I know, Jinzhou."

"The burn has four hours remaining," Marsh says. She sounds very far away and very precise. "Dr. Vasek, how long until they are in the feed line?"

I'm already looking at it. I have to look at it because I don't know, and I have to know. The dot in housing four moves a little, and then a little more. It's not swimming. It's drifting, the way they drift at minus forty, toward the warm side of something. They've gone looking for the one thing they were made to find. It's not that they've woken up early. It's that the stack is hotter than the model, because the burn's been running harder than the thaw plan expected, and the housings are warming faster than anything on a cold-start chart.

"ARACCSIM. When do they reach live fuel?"

"At present rate, approximately two hours and thirty minutes."

"And the burn is four hours."

"Three hours and fifty-one minutes."

"That's a gap of well over an hour."

"One hour and twenty-one minutes," ARACCSIM corrects, because it cannot help itself.

I put both paws over my face for exactly one second. It is the only luxury I allow myself. Then I take them off, and I look at the whiteboard on the wall, and the pencil strokes in the corner, and a stray thought arrives, whole, clean, the way the good ones do.

*Eleven percent.*

"Ethyn," Jinzhou says. "I have the same thought. Do you want me to say it first?"

"Say it."

"The reserve is eleven percent above braking minimum." No hesitation, no color. "We went to a great deal of trouble to keep it. We can spend it."

"How fast can we burn?"

"The stack is rated for a higher pulse rate than we are running. It was held back to keep the housings cool." A pause. "It will also waste cells. The faster the pulse, the more cells leave the stack before they finish. Efficiency falls."

"By how much?"

"I would estimate six percent of the reserve."

"Six."

"At the pulse rate needed to finish before the feed line, yes. Six."

I do the arithmetic, which takes me less than a second and feels like most of a day. Eleven minus six is five. Five percent above braking minimum. Not zero. Not three short, like when we'd have vented 2-E. Five, with nothing left over for anything we haven't thought of yet.

"Petryn," I say. "Can the stack take it?"

"Man." Petryn's voice comes up from the crawlway, and I can hear his teeth in it. "I just got her sounding nice."

"I know."

"She's gonna walk."

"Can you hold it?"

"I can hold it for two hours if I don't blink and if Jinzhou keeps the software from fightin' me."

"The software will not fight you," Jinzhou says.

"Say it again."

"The software will not fight you."

"That's the sweetest thing I ever heard from a man with no inflection," Petryn says. "All right. Do it."

I look at Marsh. She's very still in the cockpit, both paws on the controls, and she has the face of a woman who's been waiting a long time for a decision to be made by somebody else and has just realized she wouldn't have it any other way.

"Dr. Vasek," she says. "Your authority."

"Spend six," I say.

It's the shortest sentence I've said in two days. It lands in the room like a stone dropped in still water, and the ripples go all the way out.

"Logged," ARACCSIM says. "Pulse rate increasing. Efficiency declining. Estimated burn completion, two hours and ten minutes."

"That's the same time as the Asicell," Marsh says.

"Not exactly the same," I say. "It's twenty minutes before. We have a twenty-minute margin."

"A twenty-minute margin is not a margin."

"It's a gap with a good attitude."

---

The ship starts to shake.

It isn't violent. It's a deep, sideways rattle that comes up through the bulkheads and into the teeth. The tick from the first six hours is back, louder, and it's not a clock any more. It's a hundred clocks. Dust lifts off every surface in Lab A and floats in the lights, thousands of motes, tumbling slowly, caught between a hundred different ideas about which way was down.

"Aft housing four," ARACCSIM says. "Asicell concentration rising. Housing three, motion detected."

"They're coming," Maslin says, in a small flat voice that's the least like him I've ever heard.

"They're not coming," I say. "They're drifting toward something warm. That's different."

"is it different"

"It's different in the part where we get to be a little smug about it."

I'm not sure anyone laughs. I'm sure somebody breathes out.

The clock on the wall says forty-five hours and fourteen minutes. I am standing in front of a row of dots that have stopped being green, and I'm talking to them. I'm not proud of this.

"Please," I say. "Please be slow. I wrote a book about your cousins. I said very nice things."

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Asicell in housing four have reached the feed line junction. Feed line contact with live fuel in nineteen minutes."

"Burn completion in?"

"Eleven minutes."

Eleven against nineteen. I know how to do that one. I wish I didn't.

The ship shudders. A tube of something floats off a shelf and drifts the length of the room, serene, unbothered, and nobody reaches for it. Jinzhou says, "Pulse rate holding." Petryn says, a long way off, "She's walkin' but she's still walkin' *straight*, man, she's still straight, keep it comin'." Marsh says a sentence I've never heard her say, with a word in it that she's clearly saved up for a lot of years. Maslin is saying something quietly, over and over, and when I listen I realize he's counting backward from a hundred.

"Revised," ARACCSIM says. "Housing temperatures are rising with pulse rate. The Asicell advance has accelerated. Feed line contact in nine minutes."

"Burn?"

"Eight."

That's not a margin. That's a coin balanced on its edge, and the coin is us.

"Jinzhou."

"I have it."

"Can we shave a minute?"

"I can push the pulse rate one more step. It will cost a little more of the six."

"How much?"

"Under half a percent."

"Do it."

"Authority."

"Yours," I say. "Mine. Whoever's. Do it."

The shaking changes pitch. The hundred clocks lean forward. Somewhere below the floor, a very large machine is doing a very large thing very quickly, and I find that I've got both paws on the edge of the table, and my ears are all the way down, and I couldn't tell you when that started.

"Burn, five minutes," ARACCSIM says. "Feed line contact, six."

Nobody says anything. I don't breathe. It's not a figure of speech. I notice, around minute two, that I've stopped, and I let the air out and take another one, and the only number that matters is five against six.

"Four minutes. Contact, four."

"Asicell at the junction," Marsh says.

"Three. Contact, three."

The dots in front of me go from amber to a color I don't have a name for, and then, one by one, the dots in housing three go dark, as if someone were turning them off.

"Two."

"They are in the lines," Jinzhou says. "They are not at the stack."

"One. Contact, one."

"Pulse nominal."

"Thirty seconds."

I find out something about myself in those thirty seconds. I find out that I'm not afraid of dying. It's an entirely different thing. It's that I've spent a very long time rehearsing every outcome, and I'm not ready for the one where it works.

"Ten," ARACCSIM says. "Nine. Eight."

Maslin's counting finishes. "...three. two. one," he says, a full half-second off.

"Phengomotor burn complete," ARACCSIM says. "Handoff velocity achieved."

And the clocks stop.

---

The silence is enormous.

It's not like the cold shutdown, when the ship fell quiet because something had been taken away. This one has an edge on it, like a held breath finally let go. Dust settles very slowly in the lights. The tube of whatever it was bumps softly into the far wall and stops. I put my paw down on the table, and it's shaking, and I leave it there.

"Stack status," Marsh says, in a voice that's almost her own.

"Phengomotors shut down," says LSI-ASM&C, flat as a stone. "Reason code, complete."

"Spent cells?"

"Scrape phase complete," Petryn says. His voice is cracked and warm, and he's laughing, I think, or something near it. "All of 'em. Every last one went out the back lookin' at nothin'. Nothin' at all, man."

"Asicell?"

"In the feed lines," I say. I look at the board. The dots in the aft end have come together in a dark smear around the stack, the way ants gather around something sweet. They've found what they came for. It's a few drops of leftover fuel, in a stack that no longer has anywhere to be. "They're eating what's left. They can have it."

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Remaining Phengoclept reserve stands at five percent above braking minimum."

"Thank you."

"Chemical thruster stage is ready. Ship is on handoff velocity, with a trajectory into the home system."

"Is it a good trajectory?"

"It is the trajectory we planned."

"That wasn't what I asked."

A pause. A flourish.

"Yes," ARACCSIM says. "It is a good one."

I think I'll remember that for a long time.

---

Nobody moves for a while. Then we do, one at a time, drifting down the long hall toward Lab A like people who've heard someone say the party's still going.

Petryn comes up out of the crawlway with grease to the elbow and the kind of grin that takes up most of a face. Marsh comes out of the cockpit and doesn't straighten her jacket, which is the loudest thing she's ever done. Maslin arrives upside down, which is the way he arrives at everything, and hangs in the air with both arms around his knees, and doesn't say anything at all.

Jinzhou arrives last. He holds the rail beside me, a little too long. Then he lets go with one paw and puts it, flat, on the whiteboard, near the corner where my numbers are.

"Five," he says.

"Five what?"

"Five of us." He's not looking at me. He's looking at the board. "I counted. I get five every time. It is a very consistent number."

"It's a good number."

"It is not a large number."

"It's the one we have."

He nods, once. I'm absolutely not going to say anything about his ears.

I pick up the pencil. Under *47 / 56 ± 6*, under Maslin's one straight stroke, I write the last line of the row, small, in the corner where Bejors' lines don't have to share.

*Done at 45:25. Yes.*

I look at it for a long time.

Then I put the pencil down, and tell the ship, very sincerely, "Thank you."

"You are welcome, Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says.

Later-Ethyn can have the drawer. I'm keeping this one.

*END OF CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE*
