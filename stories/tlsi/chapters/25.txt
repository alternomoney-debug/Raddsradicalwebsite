
The middle of a trip like this one isn't a story. It's a schedule.

Marsh wrote it. It's taped to the wall of Lab B in handwriting that looks typeset, and it tells five people when to be awake, when to be in a berth, and when to eat what's left of the salvaged rations. Two awake, three asleep, the pairs rotating on a cycle I'd explain if it were interesting. It isn't. That's the point. The whole job of the schedule is for nothing to happen in it.

Nothing has happened for over three years. I'm very proud of it.

I'm on day eleven of my current stretch. I know because I mark them on the edge of the Lab B whiteboard with a pencil nobody else uses. Bottom corner. Small. Under her header, not on it. Y'SHARI-LINE AND PHENGOCLEPT RECAP is still up there in my copy of her handwriting, underlined twice the way she did it. I've never once thought about wiping it. Eleven little pencil strokes sit under it like somebody counting days in a very polite jail.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Time to destination, four years, two months, eleven days."

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

"I was about to look at the clock and pretend I wasn't counting."

"That is what I assumed."

Huh. Was that... nice? It's a very small thing. It's also the first time in months I've caught it being almost kind. I decide not to ask which part was the almost.

Food. Marsh says the margin is forty-one days. Jinzhou says forty-one days. I check it myself about once a week, in case one of them is wrong in my favor. (Rations left, divided by five people, divided by daily intake, minus days to go. It comes out forty-one. It always comes out forty-one.) Neither of them has been wrong about anything since the cold shutdown. I've decided that's rude.

---

My last stretch was with Jinzhou. It was a different kind of quiet.

He didn't cook. He ate whatever the medbay arms gave him, at exactly the time the schedule said. Then he folded the wrapper into a small neat square and clipped it into a pouch with the other small neat squares. I never asked what the squares were for. My guess? Counting. Something that got bigger at a steady rate. I'd like it if I were him.

What I did on that stretch was write a letter.

Not a log. Logs go to ARACCSIM, and from ARACCSIM to whoever at Port Jiji is still reading them. This was a file on my own tablet, addressed to Dr. Priya Emili Y'shari. I knew while I wrote it that it might never get sent. I wrote it anyway. My paws needed a job.

On day six, Jinzhou came up behind me and read it over my shoulder. He didn't ask. Jinzhou doesn't ask to read things. He reads them the way he reads code. All at once, eyes moving along a line only he can see.

"Line four is a lie," he said.

"Which part?"

"The part where you say you are fine."

"That's a figure of speech."

"It is a figure of speech that is a lie." He pointed, not quite touching the screen. "Line eleven is true. You say you are angry at her and you do not want to be. Keep that one."

"Okay. Thank you. Any other notes?"

"Paragraph three repeats paragraph one. You say you understand why she did it. Twice."

"Maybe I'm trying to convince myself."

"Then say it once and mean it, or do not say it." He looked a moment longer. "The end is good."

The end said: *We made it this far. Five of us. I'd like you to know that, whatever else.*

"You can keep the end," he said.

"I wasn't asking permission."

"I know. I am giving it anyway."

I deleted line four. I kept eleven. I took out the second time I said I understood her. Then I looked at the first one for a long time, and I took that out too. Because I didn't. Jinzhou was right, and I'd rather send her nothing than send her something that isn't true.

The letter's still on my tablet. Shorter. Better. Still maybe going nowhere.

Jinzhou went into his berth on day nineteen of that stretch. Before the cubby closed, he said, "When you send it, tell her I said line eleven was good."

"You've never met her."

"I have read line eleven," he said, and the berth slid shut.

---

Every night of every stretch, before I sleep, I go to the medbay and check the beds.

Not the screens. The screens are fine. ARACCSIM watches the screens all day and all night and has never missed a number, and I trust it with them. I check the little analog dials on the bed frames. The ones Jinzhou found our first week. The ones you'd only see if you were looking for them. Small needles. They don't need me. I check them anyway.

Three berths are slid out and occupied. Jinzhou on the left, very still, the way he sleeps, like somebody set him down carefully and walked away. Marsh beside him, ears flat, one paw curled up near her chin. Maslin on the right. Even asleep, he looks like he's in the middle of saying something.

One more berth is out, and the duffel's in it. Strapped down with the berth's own straps, more than three years after he asked if it could have its own bed and nobody said no. Nobody's moved it since. I'm not going to be first.

The last two are pushed into their cubbies, mine and Petryn's, waiting for us.

I go along the row. Heart rate. Breathing. The slow drift of the temperature needle. All where they should be. All boring. Boring is the best thing a number can be in this room.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says, very quietly. It's learned to say things quietly in here. I don't know when. "Their vital signs are monitored continuously."

"I know."

"You check them each night."

"I'm monitoring them discontinuously. It's a different service."

A pause, with the flourish in it. "Understood."

Here's the thing. It's not that I don't trust the ship. It's that four people went to sleep in these same beds, on this same ship, with the same screens watching, and the screens said they were fine right up until they weren't. Does me looking at a needle change where the needle points? No. I know that. I'm a scientist. I just can't be the kind of person who doesn't look.

I check Jinzhou's dial twice. I don't tell anyone that part.

The window's across from me. Same porthole as always. I float over, because I want to see what going home looks like.

The stars are wrong.

Not wrong like a bad memory. Wrong like a bad measurement. There are way fewer than there should be. And the ones that are left are dim and faintly red, like coals in a fire somebody stopped feeding.

Huh.

Okay. Why would stars go red?

Light goes red when the thing making it is moving away from you. The waves get stretched on the trip. So either every star behind us decided to leave at the same time (unlikely, and also a little rude), or we're the ones leaving.

We're the ones leaving. Obviously. This window faces the engine end. Backward. We're looking at where we came from, and we're leaving it at a big chunk of the speed of light. Light from back there has to chase us down to get in the window. It arrives tired. Stretched. Redder than it left.

And the empty middle? Same reason, different trick. The faster you go, the more the sky gets swept forward toward the nose. The stars ahead crowd into a bright little patch. The stars behind thin out. So the window isn't showing me fewer stars because there are fewer stars. It's showing me fewer because we're fast.

How fast?

"ARACCSIM. Current velocity, as a fraction of light."

"Point eight seven."

Oh, that's a lot. I didn't know the number. I knew "a lot," and I knew "red," and the two of them met in the middle.

Let's see. Stretch factor going backward is the square root of one minus that over one plus that. One minus point eight seven is point one three. One plus is one point eight seven. Point one three over one point eight seven is about point zero seven. Square root of that is about a quarter.

So light from behind us shows up with its waves stretched about four times longer. A yellow star like ours would get pushed way past red, out into infrared, where my eyes can't follow it. Gone. Which means the stars I can still see back there, the dim red ones?

Oh! They're the ones that started out blue. The hottest, brightest stars in the sky, stretched all the way down to looking like embers. Everything else just fell off the edge of my eyes.

That's beautiful. That's also a little sad. I'm not sure which one to feel first, so I go with both.

"If I went to the cockpit and looked forward," I say, "what would I see?"

"The forward sky is compressed and shifted toward blue. Brightness is elevated."

"A crowd," I say. "Everything we're heading toward, packed into the front window like people at a door."

"That is not a term I would use."

"You said that last time."

"It remains accurate."

Somewhere behind all that red is Bejors. A tank's worth of Asicell drifting home to it. A dead ship with a hundred and twenty-two marks by its cockpit door. Wask-ki's the other way, in the crowd up front, where I can't see it from here. I don't try. The window's job tonight was to tell me how fast I'm going. It told me. I'd like to let it stop.

Then I go to bed.

---

Petryn and I have the same stretch this time. She cooks.

I want to be clear about what that means. The medbay arms heat a tube, or a pouch, or whatever the Argon packaging department decided to call a meal that week. What Petryn does is take the least promising item in the salvaged MK2 stores, hold it up to the light like a jeweler judging a diamond, and say, "Oh, you're gonna like this one."

"I'm not," I say.

"Green one, man. Try it."

"It's called Garden Medley. There's no garden in it. I've read the label." (I have. Twice. Third ingredient is "vegetable concept.")

"Try the green one."

I try the green one.

It's good. It's warm and a little sharp, and there's something like lemon underneath that has no business surviving in a tube. How? Citric acid, probably. Shelf-stable. Still. I chew with a perfectly neutral expression, a skill I built in a school cafeteria and have never lost.

Petryn watches me over the top of her own pouch.

"It's not bad," I say.

"Mm-hm."

"It's adequate."

"Your ears came up, man."

I put a paw on one. They had. "That's circulation."

"Sure it is," Petryn says, and goes back to eating with the face of somebody who's not going to say it, because she doesn't have to.

I open my mouth to defend my ears, and something comes back. Whole. Like the rest of my facts did.

A bar. A table with a wobble. A chipped bowl set down in front of me, and across from it a stocky canine with a book face-down by his elbow and no expression at all.

"You'll like it," Nelson says. "You won't say so."

"I'm not going to like it."

He waits. He's very good at waiting. He once sat through a six-minute argument about a movie just so he could say one word at the end.

I eat. I look at the soup for a second longer than the soup deserves.

"I liked it."

"I know," Nelson says, and goes back to his book.

That's the whole memory. It's not even a good one. Soup, a bar, and a man who never gloated, because gloating takes a kind of effort he'd have charged me extra for. I wait for more. More doesn't come. But the feeling underneath it does, all at once, like a leg falling asleep in reverse. I put my pouch down on the table, very carefully, and hold on to the edge.

"Ethyn?" Petryn says.

"Give me a second."

"Sure thing."

The tally paper went out the airlock with him. I put it in the staging area beside him myself, because it was his. But I looked at it one more time first. And it turns out I'm a person who counts things even when he doesn't know he's counting.

I close my eyes, and it's there. A small, soft square of paper, folded and unfolded more times than anyone would admit to. Two columns. His initials and mine at the top. A total at the bottom, circled twice, in his handwriting, which is small and precise and slightly annoyed.

Sixty marks.

I've spent a long time assuming I owed him sixty marks. Money. A debt I'd forgotten the reason for, which seemed like exactly the kind of thing I'd do. I said so out loud, at the airlock, with his body between us. That I didn't know what I owed him sixty marks for, and I was sorry.

Wait.

Two columns. Why would a debt need two columns?

It wouldn't. A debt needs one number. Two columns is a game. Two columns is a score.

Oh.

Oh, Nelson.

It was never a debt.

I count the strokes under my initials, the ones behind my eyes. Sixty. One for every time he said *you'll like it* and I said *I won't*, and then I did. A place to eat. A book. A song with no words. A repair shop that fixed things nobody else would touch. Sixty times I was wrong. Sixty times he marked it down and never mentioned it to anybody. Including me.

He wasn't keeping track of what I owed him. He was keeping track of how often he was right, in the quietest way anyone's ever been right. And then he let me think it was money.

"I liked it," I say. To a piece of paper a very long way behind us. In a small voice that sounds ridiculous in an empty medbay. "All sixty."

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "I did not register a query."

"It wasn't one."

"Understood."

A pause. Then, with the flourish nobody's ever explained: "I will not log it."

"Thank you."

I open my eyes. Petryn, to her credit, has found something extremely interesting on the far wall.

"The green one was good," I tell her after a minute. "I'm saying it out loud so it counts."

"I know," says Petryn.

It's a coincidence that she says it the same way. I'm almost sure.

---

The shift change comes four days later. Maslin bursts into Lab B like he's been looking forward to it for a while.

"ok so," he says, before the door's done closing. "i did the logistics on the braking burn."

"Please don't," Petryn says.

"and the logistics say i will probably explode, but in a really organized way. like i have a spreadsheet for it. there are tabs."

"Maslin."

"its color coded"

"That is not funny, man."

"its a little funny," Maslin says, and looks at me for backup.

I give it the seriousness it deserves. Is it funny? Objectively? It's a man joking about exploding, on a ship that's tried to kill all of us at least three times. (Four, if you count the Garden Medley before Petryn got to it.)

"It's a little funny," I say. "I'd like to see the tabs."

"the third tab is just you," he says. "its called ethyns face when someone says the number."

"Which number?"

"any of them"

Petryn makes a sound that's half a laugh and half a prayer. Maslin looks enormously satisfied. For about a minute, the lab is the way it was in the first days. Five people who don't totally know each other yet, being gentle the only way they know how.

Then it passes, the way it does. Maslin wanders over to the whiteboard and reads my little row of pencil strokes. His mouth goes quiet.

"fifteen," he says, not really to anyone. "thats a lot of strokes."

"It's the part of the trip where fifteen counts as a lot."

"oh." He considers the board. He considers me. "do you want me to do one for the next ones? i got good handwriting."

"You really don't."

"i got legible handwriting"

"That one I'll accept."

He takes the pencil and draws one small stroke, very straight, at the end of my row. Then he stays a second, one paw still on the board. Under the header I copied from Bejors. Under the shape of her theory. Under the whole careful structure of a woman who could see this far and no farther.

"were gonna make it," he says. It's not a prediction. It sounds more like he's trying it on to see if it fits.

"We have a margin of forty-one days."

"thats not what i said"

"I know. I'm doing the math on what you said."

"and?"

"It's a lot of math." I look at the pencil. "I'm leaning toward yes."

He smiles at that, a little crooked, and heads off to check the stores like a man who's been given an assignment.

I stay with the board. Four years and two months. A very small stroke at the end of my row, in handwriting that isn't mine.

I take a second pencil and write, in the lower corner, small, where her header won't have to share:

*41*

Forty-one days of food at the end of the longest trip anyone's ever taken. I write it down so I can see how small it is. And so I can see that it isn't zero.

Later-Ethyn can have the rest.
