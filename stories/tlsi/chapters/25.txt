Seven years, nine months is a long time to be most of the way somewhere.

Then, all at once, it isn't. ARACCSIM wakes all five of us on the same morning for the first time since the flush, and the medbay fills up with the sound of people being polite to each other at half speed. Petryn stretches like a man who's going to need a minute. Marsh is already dressed. Maslin sits up, looks at the four of us, counts us under his breath, and lies back down, satisfied.

"Dr. Vasek," ARACCSIM says. "Braking burn in thirty-one hours."

"Thank you. I'd like to formally protest the timing."

"Noted. The burn does not move."

"It never does. That's what makes it a burn and not a suggestion."

A very short pause, with the flourish in it. "That is not a distinction I would have drawn."

Before it does, Petryn insists on one more dinner with a floor.

He sets up the folding table in the middle of the medbay, the same one, a little more scratched than it was eight years ago, and puts five tubes on it in a row. Nobody says it's tubes. We've all learned.

Marsh is the last to sit down. She's carrying something, and she sets it on the table very carefully, and steps back, and doesn't say anything.

It's one of the pots. The plants came over from MK2 a long time ago and have lived in the medbay under the brightest lamp we have ever since, and I've walked past them every night of every stretch and stopped noticing them, the way you stop noticing anything you've decided is safe. This one has grown. It's twice the size it was, and at the top of it, small and pale and folded open like it isn't sure yet whether it's allowed, there's a flower.

"It flowered," I say.

"Last month," Marsh says. "I did not want to make a speech about it."

"You can make a speech about it."

"I would prefer not to." She sits down, and straightens her napkin, and looks at the flower, and then, very quietly, she says, "It is very hard to give up on a day that has a shape."

Nobody says anything. Maslin reaches over, slowly, like the flower might startle, and moves it an inch closer to the middle of the table, where everybody can see it.

We eat. It's tubes. It's the best meal I've had in eight years.

The spin comes down with two keys. The cables reel in. The crew section climbs back down to the tanks and engines, and the latches close with the thunk I feel in my teeth every time, and the ship goes back to being tall, for what I hope is the last time.

---

There's one thing I do before the braking burn, and I do it alone.

I go to the medbay, which is empty now, all six berths pushed back into their cubbies, and I take out my tablet, and I open the letter.

It's shorter than it was. Line four is gone. The second time I said I understood her is gone, and so is the first. What's left is mostly true, which is more than I can say for most of what I've written in my life, including a study guide that sold rather well.

"ARACCSIM. Queue a message for Port Jiji. Attention Dr. Emili Y'shari. Hold it until we're in range."

"Queued. Would you like to attach a subject line?"

I think about it.

"No," I say. "She'll know what it is."

I read it one more time before I let it go. I don't read all of it. I read line eleven, the one where I tell her I'm angry with her and I don't want to be. It's still true. I've had years to make it untrue and it's refused.

Then I scroll to the bottom, and I add one line under the end.

*Jinzhou says line eleven is good. He's never met you. He says he doesn't have to.*

"Queued," ARACCSIM says again, when I tell it I'm done. And then, after a pause that doesn't have the flourish in it, just a pause: "Dr. Vasek. If the braking burn does not proceed as planned, this message will still be transmitted."

"I know," I say. "That's why I'm sending it now."

I put the tablet away and float there for a minute, in a room full of empty berths, with the ship humming around me. Then I go to the cockpit, where four people are waiting to be told it's time.

---

The braking burn is twenty-five hours long, and nothing goes wrong in it.

I'd like to say more about it than that. I'd like to tell you about the moment it nearly went sideways, the alarm, the clock. There isn't one. The fuel is clean. The motors are warm. The stack runs smooth from the first pulse, and the weight comes in from the engine end, and the gimbals turn everything to meet it, and then it just keeps coming, steady, hour after hour, the most boring twenty-five hours of my life.

I've never been so grateful to be bored.

Maslin watches the fuel readout the whole time anyway. Every hour he reads the reserve out loud, and every hour it's a little smaller, and every hour it's exactly where Marsh said it would be.

"three percent over," he says, at hour one.

"two percent over," at hour twelve.

"one point two," at hour twenty-four, and then he stops reading it out, and just watches it, and his ears go very still.

"Phengomotor braking complete," ARACCSIM says, at hour twenty-five. "Handoff velocity achieved. Reserve remaining, one point one percent."

The weight goes out of the room like somebody unplugged it. Maslin lets go of the readout. He looks at his own paws for a second, then at me.

"i didnt explode," he says.

"You didn't."

"i had a whole tab for it"

"I know. You can delete the tab."

"im gonna keep the tab," he says. "as a souvenir"

Petryn laughs, low and easy, from the medbay. Marsh writes something on her tablet and underlines it. Jinzhou says, "One point one," very quietly, like a man putting a number somewhere he'll be able to find it.

---

That night, Jinzhou and I end up in the cockpit, the way we always do, without either of us deciding to.

"What are you going to do," I ask him, "when we land?"

He thinks about it. Jinzhou always thinks about it. He's the only person I've ever met who treats a casual question like it's going to be graded.

"Count," he says.

"Count what?"

"The people who meet us." He looks at the screen, at a star that's going to be a sun in a few days. "I want to know how many there are."

"And then?"

"Then sleep. In a bed that does not slide out of a wall."

"That's a good plan."

"It is a short plan."

"Short plans are good," I say. "I've had a lot of long ones."

He's quiet for a while. Then: "And you?"

I think about it. I think about a desk with a coffee cup on it and a stack of papers I'd been meaning to sort, and a phone on speaker, and a woman's voice telling me what was and wasn't for a study guide. I think about a letter sitting in a queue, waiting for range.

"I'm going to go find out if anybody read chapter nine," I say.

Jinzhou makes a sound that on anyone else would be a laugh.

---

A day later, Marsh lights the chemical thrusters.

It's nothing like the burn. No shaking, no clocks. A soft push from somewhere behind us, and then another, and then the long, patient quiet of a ship coasting the rest of the way on manners. The Phengomotors brought us down from most of the speed of light. The rest is a much smaller job, done by much simpler engines, and Marsh does it with the expression of a woman parallel parking.

"Correction burn complete," she says. "We are inbound."

I go to the cockpit to look.

The forward camera is up on the main screen, and the first thing I notice is that I don't notice anything. That's the strange part. Four years ago, on a different stretch of this trip, I looked out the medbay window and the stars were wrong, thinned out and red behind us and crowded together somewhere up ahead where I couldn't see. Now they're just stars. Spread out across the screen, white and ordinary, every one of them where a sky is supposed to put it.

We're slow enough again that the universe has stopped bending around us.

"The stars are right," I say.

"They were always right," Jinzhou says, from the right seat. "We were fast."

"Same thing, from in here."

And in the middle of the screen, a little brighter than anything around it, there's a point of light that isn't white. It's orange. Dim, and not as warm as I remember it, and a little smaller than the picture of it I carry around in my head.

Nakuet.

I don't say anything. Nobody does. Jinzhou doesn't look at me, which is how I know he's looking at the same thing. Somewhere near it, too small to see yet, is a planet that's been getting colder for as long as I've been gone. And somewhere down there, if it made it, is a little courier ship that should have landed about a year ago with a box of Asicell in its glass and a file called READ_THIS_FIRST_IT_DIGS. I'm about to find out if anybody read it.

I look at it for a long time. I let it be a light.

Later-Ethyn can have the drawer. I'm keeping this one.
