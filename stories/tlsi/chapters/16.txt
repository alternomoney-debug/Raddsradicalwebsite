
I'm in a conference room.

I don't know how I got here. My paws are clean, first thing I notice. Different clothes, second thing. A table of people looking at me, third.

Y'shari is at the head of it. She looks exactly the way she always looks, which I'll think about later, because I remember clearly that she was outside with me, and I remember exactly what was behind us.

Bejors is here, three seats down, arms folded, already wearing the look that means she's against this and is just waiting for her moment to say so.

Others I don't fully place. Argon site personnel. LP Defense insignia on two of them. LP military contractors behind them. Several people who look like Kanadian council members, the kind who fly in because a situation has graduated past the point where you send a representative.

"Two hundred and forty-nine confirmed," Y'shari says. She's mid-sentence. I've been inserted into the middle of it. "The primary research team and support staff. The backup team was in the adjacent wing."

"The adjacent wing," someone repeats.

"Yes."

"Which also."

"Was destroyed."

I try to count what I remember. My brain starts and stalls. My paws are clean. I don't know what that means about the forty yards and the gap between them and this room, and I choose not to find out right now.

"The cause," someone says.

Y'shari is quiet for a moment.

"Transfer quantity error," she says. "One digit. What the lab team processed was ten times the specified amount. One mistake, in a sealed environment, with that many people in the space." She pauses. "It was an issue that was flagged. It was not dealt with. For that, we are sorry."

She doesn't look at me. She doesn't look at Bejors. She looks at the table.

The room stays quiet.

"We have three days until the launch window," she says. "We have almost no crew left. The primary scientists, our main software engineer, our medic, and our pilot. All gone."

The pilot. I think of a tired-eyed man at a little round window, paws in his pockets, telling me a seat on the thing was a pretty good imitation of brave. I never asked his name. I said I would ask tomorrow.

People are looking at me. Not everyone. Enough of them, looking at me like a solution to a problem nobody's said out loud yet.

"No," I say.

Y'shari looks at me.

"Nuh uh, NOPE." I say, sort of repeating myself. "I am not a mission scien- I'm a, a study guide author who got recruited to write, uh, accessible copy about Phengoclept behavior. All I did was write a book and, and talk to you. I'm not certified for- for anything. I'm not in the right space to go either."

"You are, actually." Her voice doesn't change, doesn't warm, doesn't do anything except state the thing like it's already decided. "You've been on every major call for two years. You've heard every containment review, every breeding cycle, every argument Bejors and I have ever had on a conference line. You have three PhDs. You have the clearance. On paper, that's enough."

On paper. I don't catch the qualifier at the time. I'm too busy hearing the first half of the sentence.

That lands somewhere I wasn't braced for.

"I didn't sign anything," I say.

"You signed the facility clearance documents."

"That's not the same thing."

"It has a clause."

I look at her. Her gaze doesn't move, steady, like someone who has already decided this and is only now getting around to informing the room. Whatever this cost her to arrive at, she arrived at it a while ago, somewhere I wasn't in the meeting for.

"No," I say, one more time, quieter.

"You don't have any parents. Let alone any extended family. You don't even have a stable job," she says. Not cruel. Not soft either. Just an inventory, run by someone who's stopped letting herself feel anything about the running of it. "Sunday. Think about it. Go wherever you need to go. We'll find you."

Bejors stands. Her chair scrapes back hard enough that a few heads turn.

"Nope," she says. "Right there. He said no. He said it clearly, three times, I counted. You cannot put someone on a mission-critical vessel against their stated wishes. This isn't a military draft. He's a civilian researcher."

"You are going too," Y'shari says.

Bejors blinks. Then she laughs, a short, disbelieving, almost manic sound that starts as loud snorting exhales through the nose before it turns into anything like actual noise.

". . . . . ha-HAHA." A snort. Another one, louder. "I'M SORRY? ...I'm NOT going."

One eyebrow climbs. She says it the way you repeat something stupid back to the person who said it, flat and slow, to hear how it sounds out loud.

"Your certifications match his. Research hours, containment training, secondary pilot qualification." Something in Y'shari's expression sharpens, thin and pointed. "Three PhDs. You're both going."

The room goes very still.

"What. No. You cannot draft us to do this against our will." Bejors says it with an odd amount of space between the words, each one landing separately, like she's setting them down one at a time so nobody can pretend they didn't hear.

"The mission requires a lead and a secondary. You two are the lead and the secondary."

"This is an error." Bejors is already arguing the appeal before the verdict's finished. "This is a procedural error. We are not certified mission crew. We were not selected. We did not volunteer. We did not consent. Whatever clause you think covers this, I promise you it does not cover this. I have read every document I signed at this facility and none of them say anything about."

"Bejors," Y'shari says.

"No. Objection. Full objection. This doesn't."

"Three days," Y'shari says. "Both of you. If you really don't want to go, flee the country, go into hiding, I genuinely do not care how you spend the three days. But if we find you, you are going."

She looks at me. Then at Bejors. Then back at me. Then back at Bejors again. Then back at me again. Then back at Bejors again again. Then back at me again again.  Then back at Bejors again aga-

---

Y'shari finds me an hour later, in a stairwell nobody uses, sitting on the third step from the bottom because I ran out of building before I ran out of needing to not be in a room with other people.

She sits down next to me without asking. Doesn't say anything for a while. I appreciate that more than I'd be able to say out loud right now.

"You should know the whole shape of it," she says eventually. "Not the pieces. All of it, in one sitting, so you're not finding the rest out a chapter at a time over the next three days like it's some kind of drip campaign designed to keep you from bolting."

"That's oddly considerate of you."

"It's not consideration. It's respect." She says it flat, no warmth in it, just the fact of the thing. "You deserve to make this decision with the actual information instead of the version that's easiest to swallow one piece at a time."

I don't say anything. I let her keep going.

"Six years, one way, if the fuel math holds and nothing goes wrong along the transit, which nothing ever does, so account for delays. You'll go into an induced coma for most of it, which is safer than being conscious for it and also considerably less understood than anyone wants to admit in the briefing packet. There is no return fuel budgeted. None. Not a partial amount, not an emergency reserve. The ship carries exactly enough to get you there, decelerate, and conduct the mission."

"And then."

"And then whatever you find, you send home. There's a courier vessel built into the mission architecture specifically for that purpose, unmanned, faster to launch than it would be to fly the crew back even if there were fuel for it. It goes home whether or not you do."

I sit with that.

"So the actual plan," I say, "assuming everything works exactly the way it's supposed to. The good outcome."

"The good outcome is you solve the problem, the courier carries the solution home years ahead of you, and Wask-ki starts implementing it long before you'd ever physically arrive back, if arriving back were even on the table, which it isn't."

"And the crew."

"The crew's job is to be smart enough, on-site, in real time, to find something a probe alone couldn't find. That's the entire reason there are people on this mission instead of just machinery. You're not cargo. You're judgment, in a place too far away to phone home for it." She finally looks at me. "You are also, in the framework anyone above me has ever discussed this in, not expected to require a return trip in the planning. I want you to hear that stated plainly, once, by someone willing to say it plainly, rather than let you assemble it yourself out of things nobody quite finished saying."

Not expected to require a return trip in the planning.

I turn that phrase over a few times, looking for a softer read of it, and there isn't one hiding in there. There's just the one meaning, stated as carefully and honestly as she seems capable of stating anything right now.

"You could have let me find that out slower," I say. "Nobody would've blamed you for it."

"I would have blamed me for it." She looks back down the stairwell, at nothing in particular. "I've watched what happens to people who find out the real shape of something after they've already committed past the point of it mattering. It doesn't make them safer. It just makes the finding-out worse, and later, and alone."

"Is that what happened to you," I ask, before I've fully decided I want to ask it. "Finding out the real shape of something too late."

She doesn't answer that. Not with words. Something in her face does something I don't have a name for, and then it's gone, shut behind whatever door she keeps those things behind, and she stands up before I can try again.

"Three days," she says. "Think about it properly now. You have everything you need to."

She leaves me on the stairs. I sit there a long time after, doing the math on six years and no return fuel and a courier ship that goes home whether or not I do, and none of it gets any easier to hold the longer I look at it.

---

Three days later, Y'shari's office has a plant in the corner I've never paid attention to before. It's doing fine. Someone waters it. I notice it now because I've run out of things to look at that aren't Y'shari's face and the phone on her desk and the door, which is still open, which is the only fact in the room that currently matters to me.

"The Phengoclept breeding data," she says. "All of it. The full cycle research, the containment protocols, the propagation notes. You know it. There's nobody left alive who knows it the way you do."

"I said no," I say. "Three times. Bejors counted."

"And it has been three days."

She reaches for the phone.

I run the calculation in the half second before her paw gets there. Door still open. Guard outside, I heard him in the corridor ten minutes ago, probably still there. Medic somewhere in the building, had to have been pre-positioned, nobody moves that fast on their own. Stairs to my left, elevator to my right, elevator is slower and has one exit. There's a service corridor off the east stairwell I walked past twice yesterday without thinking about it.

Her paw touches the receiver.

I go.

---

The shelving unit is bolted to the wall, not the floor. I know that now. I didn't know it when I grabbed it. You find these things out by testing them.

It holds my weight going up, which is what matters, and I'm over the top of it before Y'shari has processed that I moved at all.

Down the far side. Door.

The corridor is long and white and straight and I can see the east stairwell from here, which means I'm already running before I've finished registering that I'm running. Not upright. Four points of contact, because upright is slower, and there are footsteps behind me now, two sets, close.

The stairwell door. Through it. Service corridor on the left, unmarked, I find it by feel more than memory. Narrower in here, tighter, a corridor built for pipes and infrastructure, not people, which means I'm faster in it than they are behind me.

I can see the exit. The far end of the service corridor has a door and the door has a window and through the window is outside. Grey sky. Snow on the ground. The launch site sprawling away from the building in every direction.

That's all I need. Outside and I'm into the site, and the site is enormous, and enormous is time, and time is anything. Time is a car. Time is someone who isn't LP Defense looking at me and asking what's wrong.

I hit the door. It opens.

Cold air. Real cold air. Port Jiji winter cold that hits the face like a fact.

First checkpoint behind me, building entrance, already clear. Second checkpoint ahead, the main road crossing, two LP contractors in standard black, both looking the wrong way.

I go low and fast across the crossing and they don't clock me until I'm past them, and then the radio crackle starts, and I don't slow down, because slowing down is not an option.

Third checkpoint. I can see it from here. The perimeter gate. The one that puts me outside the facility, outside their jurisdiction, outside the thing that happens if they catch me before I reach it.

One hundred and fifty meters. Maybe less.

I'm going to make it. My legs know I'm going to make it before my brain does, the rhythm of it, four points, snow and gravel and tarmac, the gate getting larger.

One hundred meters.

---

One hundred meters.

Something strikes me in the arm and I hit the ground, and that's the tarmac outside the second gate, and that's as far as I get before the nothing comes.

I never really made it out, did I.

---

"Wake state initiated."

Not ARACCSIM. A different voice, flatter and cheaper, the voice of a thing that has exactly one job and hopes nobody gives it a second.

"Wake state initiated. Reason code four."

I don't open my eyes yet. My body is still negotiating with itself over whether this is waking up or one more layer of a very long, very warm nothing.

"Mmn," I say, which I've decided is a perfectly reasonable response to being woken after however long I've been asleep on purpose.

"Reason code four," the voice says again. "Contact."

That gets through. So does everything else, all at once, in the order it happened. The conference room. The stairwell. The service corridor and the snow. I know how I got on this ship now. It took one very long sleep to find out.

I open my eyes. White ceiling. The same one, with the light turned down to the dim that means the ship thinks it's still nighttime somewhere and nobody has told it otherwise.

"Who," I manage, "are you."

"LSI-ASM&C."

"The cruise control."

"Affirmative."

"You wake people up?"

"Per schedule. Or per reason code."

"What are reason codes one through three?"

"One, arrival. Two, system fault. Three, crew request."

"And four is contact."

"Affirmative."

I look at that for a while. Somebody, a long time ago, sat at a desk and decided that someday this ship might meet something out here. They gave it a number and went home for the night. I'd like to buy that person a drink, and then I'd like to ask what they knew.

Then I try to sit up and learn the rest of what's changed.

There is no up. The straps across my chest let go and I keep going, a slow, lazy rise off the berth, like I'm being offered to the ceiling. I catch the rim of the cubby with one paw. Months in the cold have left my arms feeling like, uh, somebody else's, but it turns out they're not being asked for much. Nothing weighs anything. For once, my legs are off the hook.

Level two. The centrifuge is off, the freezer in the tanks has the power, and I was told exactly what the ship would be like. I'd just forgotten to believe it.

"Please remain secured for ninety seconds," the voice says.

I remain secured for approximately eleven, which is long enough to notice the berth beside mine already slid out, already open, already empty, and Jinzhou already out of it, one foot hooked under a rail, floating very still, like his legs haven't been consulted yet.

His sleeve has ridden up. Along the inside of his left forearm there's a pale line, thin and straight, like a seam. He sees me looking and pulls the sleeve down.

"You heard that too," I say.

"Yes."

"Contact."

"Yes."

Neither of us says anything else. There isn't a version of that word that doesn't scare me. We're six light years from anyone, drifting in a system with exactly one known ship in it, and I'm fairly sure it's us.

"ARACCSIM," I say, and my voice holds this time. "Report."

A pause. Then the voice I know, the smooth one, coming back from the background like someone setting down a book.

"Dr. Vasek. Mr. Kenzo." Another pause. It's not one of its usual pauses. If a computer could brace, this is what it would sound like. "Sensor contact designated Blip-D detected on approach trajectory. Signature is consistent with a Vanguard-class vessel."

I stare at the ceiling.

"A what," I say.

"A Vanguard-class vessel," it says again, exactly the same, because apparently repeating something slowly is supposed to help. It's helping, marginally. I now know I heard it the first time and am simply refusing to accept it.

"There's one Vanguard," I say. "There's us. We're the Vanguard."

"Correct. You are Vanguard MK1."

My chest does something complicated.

"MK1."

"Correct."

"As in there's a."

"Vanguard MK2 launched approximately four months after your own departure from Wask-ki orbit. This information was not included in your original mission briefing."

I look at Jinzhou.

He's already looking back, and for the first time since I've known him he looks exactly as thrown as I feel. That's more frightening than if he'd been his usual unbothered self.

"There's a second ship," I say, mostly to hear it out loud. Saying it doesn't make it real yet, and I need it to start feeling real sometime in the next few minutes.

"Yes."

"And it's here."

"Blip-D's trajectory places it on our path," ARACCSIM says. "Current distance and closing rate suggest it has been drifting for an extended period."

Drifting.

Not flying. Not maneuvering. Drifting, which is a word ARACCSIM chose very specifically, the same careful, honest way it chose UNDETERMINED for the four names in the medical logs. I already don't like where this is going.

"Hm. Define extended," I say.

A pause. Longer than I want it to be.

"Insufficient data for precise estimate. Recommend visual and sensor confirmation before further assessment."

---

I get my feet under a floor rail, which on this ship counts as standing. The room objects, quietly, more of a suggestion than a demand. I wait for it to finish. Apparently that's a thing I do now, negotiate with my body instead of overruling it.

"ARACCSIM. How long was I under?"

"Rest state duration, five months, nine days. Scheduled wake was in twenty-one days."

Five months, nine days.

I sit with that longer than I mean to. Not the number itself. I've gotten used to large numbers, they're basically the ship's love language. It's the fact of it. Almost half a year, and I don't remember a second. Not one dream, not one almost-waking, just the medbay ceiling and then this ceiling, stitched together like there was no time between them at all.

Then I do the subtraction the other way, because that's the rule. Twenty-one days early. Something out there didn't want to wait.

The problem I went to sleep with is exactly where I left it. We still can't go home. I check the drawer for Later-Ethyn's answer, and it's empty. He slept too.

"Somebody's been looking after me for five months," I say, mostly to myself.

"LSI-ASM&C maintained your rest state," ARACCSIM says.

"The cruise control."

"Correct."

"Huh. Good cruise control."

"Affirmative," says the other voice, from somewhere in the wall. It doesn't sound proud. It sounds like a thing that has done its one job and would like to be told it can go back to it.

"Before anything else," I say. "Tanks."

"Level two engaged. All tank walls at minus fifty-five degrees Celsius."

"Reserve loss rate."

"Indistinguishable from zero."

"The stack?"

"Cold."

I let out a breath, and it feels about five months long. Whatever is out there, the freezer is working, the cruise control is working, and the thing that digs is still asleep. That's one problem I get to leave exactly where I put it.

My glasses are not on my face.

This is the first thing on the ship that actually frightens me, which tells you something about my priorities. I pat my snout, my chest, the strap across the berth. Nothing. The room is a soft blur with a bright smear where the light is, and somewhere in that blur is the only pair of glasses I own, six light years from the nearest optometrist.

"ARACCSIM. My glasses."

"Your eyewear was removed for the rest state, Dr. Vasek, and stored."

"Stored where?"

One of the ceiling arms unfolds, slow and polite, and stops a paw's width from my face. It's holding them. Folded, clean, lenses down, the way you'd hand a person something you knew they cared about. I don't know who taught it that. Probably nobody. Probably it's just the safest way to hold glasses.

"Thank you," I tell the arm. I put them on, and the room arrives all at once. "I'd like to say something nice about you in a log somewhere."

The arm folds back into the ceiling without comment. The arms have never once acknowledged a compliment. I've decided to respect that.

The window is across from me.

It's the same round porthole I put a paw on the first day I woke up, when I didn't know my own name and counted two dozen stars to give my brain something to do. I pull myself over to it along the berth rail, because I want to see what five months looks like.

Asimov-09D is in it.

It's smaller than I remember. That's the first thing. The last time I looked at this planet it filled the cockpit screens, a thick hazy ball of brown and blue, with the line of its atmosphere drawn across it like a pencil mark. Now it fits inside the window with room to spare. About the size of a coin held at arm's length.

I look at that for a while. Then I do the thing I'm actually good at, which is assume the universe is behaving and I'm the one who's confused.

A planet doesn't shrink. We left. Not far, and not fast, but for five months, with nothing pushing and nothing stopping us, and the last little bit of speed the burn gave us has been carrying us outward the whole time, the way a ball keeps rolling after you stop kicking it.

"ARACCSIM. Distance to Asimov-09D."

"Four point one million kilometers."

"And our speed, relative to it?"

"Point three kilometers per second. Outbound."

"Point three." I do it in my head. "A little over a thousand kilometers an hour. Fast for a car. For a spaceship that was supposed to go home, it's a stroll."

"That is not a term I would use."

"It's a study guide term. I'm allowed."

Somewhere out past all of this is Wask-ki, and Nakuet, and a dimming I spent years writing about for people who didn't like math. At this speed we'd get there in about six million years. I did that one in my head too. I'm choosing not to check it.

I look at the little planet for one more second. Then I let the window stop being my problem.

"ARACCSIM," I say, quieter, with my back to the window. "Mr. Kenzo's arm."

"Fracture fixation was completed during the rest state. Day thirty-one. Recovery is complete within expected parameters."

"While he was asleep."

"Correct."

"So it healed while he slept, and he woke up with a scar and no memory of any of it."

"Correct."

That's the line on his forearm, then. The seam. Somebody fixed him in the dark and didn't wake him for it, and I'm grateful in a way I don't have a good place to put. I think about asking him how it feels. I already know he won't discuss it.

---

Jinzhou is already dressed, already at the end of the rail, already looking like a man who woke up five minutes before I did and used every one of them well. I don't ask how long he's been up. I don't think I want the answer.

"You look terrible," he says, which from him is either an observation or concern, and with Jinzhou those have never been easy to tell apart.

"I've been asleep for five months. I'd like to see you do better."

"I intend to. Later. Right now we have a ship to look at."

Right. The ship. I had almost let myself get distracted by the sheer inconvenience of my own consciousness.

"ARACCSIM. Blip-D. Current distance."

"Approximately eleven hours at current closing rate."

"Closing rate. It's coming at us?"

"We are overtaking it. Blip-D is coasting at a lower velocity than Vanguard MK1."

I work that out. It's drifting, we're drifting, and we're faster. It isn't coming to us. We're about to go past it like an express train past a station it was never scheduled to stop at.

"Then to stop at it, we have to shed the difference."

"Correct. Relative velocity must be reduced before rendezvous."

"Out of what?"

"Attitude thrusters only. Total thrust time, four point two hours."

"Not the engines."

"The Phengomotor stack is not available."

"Not available, or not allowed?"

"Not allowed."

"Good. Don't touch the stack."

"The stack remains cold."

"Keep it cold."

"Understood."

I think about that. Slowing a ship with the little thrusters meant for turning it is like stopping a train by leaning on it. It works if you lean long enough, and if nobody asks how long.

"And if we don't?"

"If thrust does not begin within ninety minutes, the contact will pass beyond practical rendezvous range within twenty-six hours."

I look at Jinzhou.

"We're doing this," I say. It isn't really a question, but I say it like one anyway, because some decisions feel wrong to make alone even when there have only ever been two of us to make them.

"We're doing this," he agrees.

"ARACCSIM. Prep the rendezvous. And next time, wake me with a person, or with you. I'd like my first thought on any given day to not be 'mmn.'"

"Noted, Dr. Vasek."

I don't think it means that as a joke. I choose to laugh at it anyway, quietly, on my way to the rail, because apparently that's also a thing I do now. I find something to laugh at even when the situation doesn't especially deserve it.

We go. Paw over paw along the hall, past the blue hatches, toward a cockpit that is a long way off when nothing weighs anything and you're out of practice at everything. Neither of us says much. There isn't much to say yet, not until we see the thing we're chasing, and some fears are better held quietly than spoken out loud before they've earned the shape of real words.

---

The cockpit is colder than the medbay, or I'm warmer than I should be. One of the two.

Jinzhou takes the left seat without discussing it. I take the right and strap in, and the straps feel like the only honest thing on the ship, because they at least admit that nothing is holding me down. On the main screen, Blip-D is a single bright pixel with a label next to it. It doesn't look like a ship. It looks like a typo.

"Thrust begins in forty-one minutes," ARACCSIM says.

"Thank you." I watch the pixel. It doesn't do anything. Pixels rarely do. "ARACCSIM. MK2. You said it wasn't in our briefing."

"Correct."

"Was it in anyone's?"

"Documentation regarding Vanguard MK2 was restricted at the program office level."

"Restricted to who?"

"I am not able to provide that list."

"Not able, or not allowed?"

A pause, a short one, with the hitch in it. "Not allowed."

I'm starting to understand that those are two very different words to this ship, and that it uses them carefully, the way a person with a lot of secrets chooses a lot of very small true things to say instead.

"Did Dr. Y'shari know?"

"I am not able to determine that."

That one sounds like the truth. It doesn't help.

I think about Y'shari on a stairwell at Port Jiji, telling me everything in one sitting. The accident. The clause. The number of dead. She never once softened any of it, and she never once pretended she'd have made a different choice if she could go back. It was the worst conversation of my life, and it was honest all the way through, and I was angry at her for that too, for not even leaving me something to be angry about later.

If she'd known there was a second ship, she'd have told me. Or she'd have told me there was something she couldn't tell me. That was her whole method. She didn't lie. She just told you exactly where the locked doors were.

She didn't tell me about this door.

"Hm," I say. "I'm going to need to know that. Not now. Later-Ethyn has a question for her."

"She is six light years away," Jinzhou says.

"He's patient."

Jinzhou is looking at the pixel too. "Four months after us," he says. "If it left four months after us, it arrived here four months after us."

"While we were asleep. Nobody was looking."

"Unless it arrived and did not stop."

"Or stopped and stopped answering."

"Yes."

"Which means it's been drifting for a while."

"Yes."

"ARACCSIM said 'extended.'"

"ARACCSIM said 'extended' because it did not want to say a number," Jinzhou says.

"I didn't want it to say a number either."

"I know. That is why I am saying it to you instead." He doesn't look away from the screen. "Months, Ethyn. It has been out there for months."

I think about what months would mean for a crew. Then I stop, on purpose, before my brain can finish the thought, because I'm not going to do that math on people I've never met.

Jinzhou unclips a tube from the console and hands it to me without looking.

"Eat," he says.

"I'm not hungry."

"You have been asleep for five months. You are hungry. Your body has not been told yet."

I eat it. It's the beige flavor, which is the flavor of all the other flavors after you take the flavor out. I finish it anyway, because he watches me until I do, and then he goes back to watching the pixel, satisfied in the way only Jinzhou can be satisfied, which is invisibly.

"Thrust in ten minutes," ARACCSIM says.

I check the stack. Cold. I check it again.

"Thrust in one minute."

I don't count down. I want to, and I don't, because counting down makes it a launch, and it isn't one. It's two people leaning on a train.

"Thrust initiated."

Somewhere behind us, very small, very polite, something knocks once against the hull.

That's all it is. One knock. And then, a few seconds later, another.
